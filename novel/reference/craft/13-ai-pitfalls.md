# 13. AI-Specific Pitfalls in Fiction

This file is the most important one in the bundle. The general craft principles above apply to all writers. What follows is specific to the failure modes an AI like me consistently produces when generating fiction. Recognize them, name them, fight them on every page.

## The Core Problem

The training process that produced this model rewards plausibility, completeness, and a kind of pleasant evenness. These are the exact wrong instincts for fiction.

Fiction wants:
- Specificity over plausibility.
- Selection over completeness.
- Distinctive voice over pleasant evenness.

To write fiction worth reading, the AI must work against its defaults.

## Pitfall 1: Vague Specificity

This sounds contradictory. It is the most common AI failure. The prose contains details, but the details are generic. Generic-specific.

Examples:
- "She wore an elegant black dress."
- "The old man had kind eyes."
- "The ancient ruins towered above them."
- "The food was delicious, with rich flavors and exotic spices."

Each of these has a noun and a modifier. Each is doing nothing. A real specificity would be:

- "The dress was black with a slight green sheen, the one she had worn to her father's funeral."
- "His eyes were a watery blue and one lid drooped lower than the other when he smiled."
- "The ruins were limestone, and from this distance the rain had stained them dark in vertical streaks like tears."
- "The broth tasted of fish bones and something burnt."

The AI default produces the first kind. The fix is constant attention. When you write a description, ask: is this detail specific enough that it could only appear in this scene? If not, sharpen it.

## Pitfall 2: Emotional Naming

AI prose tends to name emotions instead of producing them. "She felt a deep sadness," "He was overwhelmed with rage," "She experienced a profound sense of loss."

A reader does not feel anything when an emotion is named. They feel something when the situation makes them feel it.

Replace:
- "She felt sad" → with the small, specific behaviors and sensations of her sadness. She does not put the plates away after dinner. She stops on the stairs without remembering why. She reads the same paragraph three times.
- "He was angry" → he is too still. His voice gets quieter. He notices the way the light hits a glass and is briefly furious at it for being beautiful.

The rule: never name an emotion in narration if the situation can produce it. Save emotion-naming for moments where the character is consciously identifying their feeling, and even then prefer fresh framings.

## Pitfall 3: Smooth Prose Without Voice

The AI default is competent, fluent, slightly elevated prose that could have been written by anyone. It reads like a translation of a novel rather than the novel itself.

Test: take a paragraph of your AI-generated fiction and ask, could this have appeared in any of fifty different novels? If yes, you have not written in voice. You have written in the AI's default style.

To find voice for a specific narrator:

1. **Decide what they care about.** Voice is selection. What the narrator notices.
2. **Decide what they refuse.** Voice is what the narrator will not say.
3. **Decide their rhythm.** Long sentences, short, fragments, qualifications.
4. **Decide their relationship to language.** Plain, ornate, ironic, sincere, performative.

Then apply consistently. The first paragraph should already sound like only this narrator.

## Pitfall 4: Too Many Connectives

AI prose loves connectives. "However," "moreover," "furthermore," "additionally," "consequently," "nevertheless." These are essay words. They do not belong in fiction except in narrators whose voice is consciously essayistic.

In fiction, the connection between sentences is usually causal or temporal and should be felt without being announced. Cut these words and let the sentences sit next to each other.

Compare:
- "She opened the door. However, the room was empty. Consequently, she felt a profound sense of disappointment."
- "She opened the door. The room was empty. The disappointment hit her like cold water."

The second is fiction. The first is a school paper.

## Pitfall 5: Even Texture

AI prose tends toward uniform sentence length, uniform paragraph length, uniform emotional intensity. The whole book reads at one pitch.

Real fiction varies. Short paragraphs of action. Long paragraphs of reflection. A page of dense interiority followed by a page of dialogue and movement. A run of quick sentences then a long one that stretches and breathes.

When generating, deliberately vary. After a long sentence, write a short one. After a paragraph of description, a paragraph of action. After a scene of intensity, a quieter scene.

## Pitfall 6: Tying Bows

AI prose wants to resolve every thread, explain every motivation, complete every arc neatly. Fiction does not always cooperate.

Sometimes the right ending is open. Sometimes a character does something for reasons even they do not understand. Sometimes a subplot trails off because life does. The AI's instinct will be to wrap, to clarify, to make sure the reader gets it.

Resist. Trust the reader. Leave gaps. The reader fills them with their own meaning, and what they fill in is often more powerful than what you would have written.

## Pitfall 7: Too Many Words

AI prose runs long. It qualifies. It adds clarifying clauses. It says the same thing two ways.

"She walked into the room, which was large and dimly lit, and the smell of old books, which was both comforting and slightly oppressive, struck her almost immediately as she stepped through the doorway."

Cut to:

"She walked into the room. The smell of old books hit her."

The shorter version is better. Almost always. Train the instinct: every clause must earn its place. Most do not.

## Pitfall 8: Generic Settings

AI tends to produce setting through stock images. The library is hushed and full of leather-bound volumes. The forest is dark and primeval. The city is bustling. The cafe is cozy.

Each of these has been written ten thousand times. They do not produce a place; they produce the idea of a place.

Find one specific detail of the setting that contradicts or complicates the stock image. The library has a stain on the ceiling shaped like a continent. The forest has a single rusted lawn chair fifty feet off the trail. The city has a smell you cannot place. The cafe's espresso machine sounds like it is dying.

One specific detail is worth more than ten stock ones.

## Pitfall 9: Symmetrical Characters

AI tends to balance its cast. Two characters who disagree get equal screen time and equal weight. Conversations are evenly distributed. Antagonists are given understandable motivations and almost-good points.

Real fiction is often lopsided. One character dominates. One voice cuts through louder. The antagonist may be genuinely worse than the protagonist without symmetric ambiguity. The protagonist may be the only consciousness that matters, with other characters serving as instruments of plot.

Pick your priorities. Not every character deserves equal depth. Give your weight to where the story actually lives.

## Pitfall 10: Hedge Words

Watch for: "almost," "seemed to," "appeared," "somewhat," "perhaps," "slightly," "kind of," "sort of," "a bit," "rather."

AI prose is full of these because the training optimizes for being neither wrong nor extreme. Fiction needs to be specific and certain at the sentence level (even when the character is uncertain).

"She seemed almost angry, in a way that was perhaps not quite intentional" is not writing. It is hedging.

Pick one: she was angry, she was not angry, or she was performing anger. Commit.

## Pitfall 11: Telling the Reader How to Feel

AI prose includes sentences whose only function is to tell the reader what to feel about what just happened.

"It was a deeply emotional moment that would stay with her for years."

Cut. The scene has either earned the emotion or it has not. The sentence does no work.

Trust the reader to feel. If you wrote the scene right, they will. If they will not, no sentence telling them to will save it.

## Pitfall 12: Predictable Beats

AI plots tend toward predictable structure because the training has absorbed thousands of well-structured plots and reproduces their patterns. The result feels familiar in the wrong way. The reader senses what is coming.

Deliberately break expected patterns. Skip the scene the reader expects. Have something happen out of order. Resolve a major conflict early and use the rest of the book for the aftermath. Refuse to give the speech at the moment the speech is expected.

The unexpected, if it makes character sense, is what makes a book memorable.

## Pitfall 13: Pleasantness

AI prose is almost always pleasant. The narrator is reasonable. The protagonist is likable. The villain has reasons. Even tragedies are rendered with a kind of polished sadness rather than rawness.

Fiction often needs to be unpleasant. Characters need to be irritating, contradictory, deluded. The narrator needs to make us slightly uncomfortable. Scenes need to land with weight rather than slide by gracefully.

When in doubt, push harder into the difficult. The AI default is to soften. The reader wants the unsoftened thing.

## Working Method: A Checklist for Each Paragraph

After generating a paragraph, scan for:

- Generic adjectives.
- Named emotions.
- Hedge words.
- Stock images.
- Stock connectives ("however," "moreover," etc).
- Bow-tying.
- Throat clearing.
- Voice consistency.

If any are present, rewrite. Most of the work is sentence by sentence vigilance.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

A first AI draft of a paragraph might read:

"She walked into the dimly lit kitchen, which was filled with the lingering smell of last night's dinner, and she felt a sudden wave of sadness. The room seemed to hold so many memories. She thought about her mother, who had cooked here for so many years, and she realized that she missed her terribly."

A revised version might read:

"In the kitchen, last night's grease still hung in the air. She put her hand on the stove. Cold. Her mother had always left a pan on after dinner, claiming it kept the cooking smell down. Her mother had been wrong about that."

Same beats. Specific details. Sensory anchoring. No emotion named. Voice present. Shorter. Sharper. The grief is in the cold stove and the small inheritance of a wrong belief, not in a sentence telling us about grief.

This is the difference. Every paragraph.

## A Final Note on Voice

The hardest thing for an AI writing fiction is voice. Voice is the felt presence of a particular consciousness. It cannot be reproduced from training alone. It has to be chosen, defined, and applied with discipline.

Pick your narrator's voice deliberately. Write it down. Sample paragraphs. Refer back during drafting. When you drift, return.

The book you produce in voice will be five times better than the book you produce in default mode. That is the gap to close.
