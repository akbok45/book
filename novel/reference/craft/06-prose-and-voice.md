# 06. Prose and Voice

The novel happens sentence by sentence. A reader can forgive a creaky plot if the prose is alive. They will not forgive dead prose for any reason. This is the level most beginners under-invest in.

## What Makes Prose Alive

Living prose has four properties:

1. **Specificity.** Concrete nouns and active verbs that produce images.
2. **Rhythm.** Sentence lengths and stresses that move the reader forward.
3. **Voice.** A consistent consciousness behind the words.
4. **Restraint.** The sense that more was held back than shown.

Dead prose is generic, monotonous, anonymous, and over-explained. The cure for all four is the same: stop writing what is expected, write what is true to this character in this moment.

## Specificity

The first law: a specific detail is worth ten general descriptions.

"She wore an expensive dress" is dead.
"She wore the green dress, the one with the loose button" lives, because we feel the missing button and the history it implies.

Specific does not mean elaborate. It means selected. One precise detail beats five vague ones. Your job is to find the detail that makes the moment unmistakable.

Quick test: read your description. Could it apply to a hundred different versions of this scene? If yes, it is generic. Replace it with a detail that could only apply to this one.

## Verbs

Verbs carry the energy of a sentence. Strong verbs make prose move. Weak verbs make it sit.

- "She walked slowly across the room" weak.
- "She crossed the room" stronger.
- "She drifted across the room" stronger still and now carrying mood.

Cut "ly" adverbs and replace the verb. "Ran quickly" should be "sprinted." "Said softly" should be "whispered" or "murmured." "Walked angrily" should be "stalked" or "stormed."

Watch for the be-verbs (is, was, were, are, being) and the auxiliary verbs (had been, would have, was going to). They flatten prose. Often the sentence can be recast around an active verb:

- "There was a tree growing by the river" becomes "A tree leaned out over the river."
- "She was running down the hall" becomes "She ran down the hall" or "She raced down the hall."

Not every be-verb needs to die. Use them when they fit. Just notice when you are using them out of habit.

## Sentence Rhythm

Sentence length controls pace. Short sentences sprint. Long sentences breathe.

The most boring prose is uniform sentence length. Vary deliberately.

A short sentence stops the reader and forces them to feel the weight of what just happened. Use them at moments of impact.

She opened the door. He was gone.

A long sentence carries the reader on a current of thought, accumulating detail and emotion, building pressure that releases at the period.

Read your prose aloud. Where does it drag? Where does the rhythm flatten? Edit for music.

## The Five-Senses Rule and Its Limit

The standard advice: engage all five senses. This is half right. Most amateur prose engages only sight. Smell, taste, texture, sound, the proprioceptive sense of one's own body in space, all are underused.

The half wrong: not every sense in every scene. A scene with a dutifully checked box for each sense reads like a writing exercise. Pick the sense that does the most work for the moment. Often it is the one the reader would not expect.

A funeral scene where the protagonist notices the smell of the new carpet has more weight than one where she notices everyone wearing black.

## Show vs Tell

The most misunderstood rule in fiction. "Show, don't tell" is shorthand. The actual principle: dramatize the emotional core, summarize the rest.

You should show:
- Emotional turning points.
- Decisions that reveal character.
- Confrontations.
- Moments the reader needs to feel.

You should tell:
- Transitions between scenes.
- Backstory the reader needs but does not need to relive.
- Routine activity that is not the point.
- Information necessary for the next scene to land.

A novel that shows everything is bloated. A novel that tells everything is flat. The skill is knowing which to do when.

The classic tell: "She was angry."
The classic show: "She put the cup down so carefully that the saucer did not even tremble. When she spoke, her voice was a notch quieter than before."

Show when the emotion matters. Tell when you just need to move on.

## Telling Through Voice

Sometimes telling is better than showing because it is in voice. First-person narrators tell us things constantly. That is how voice works.

"My mother was a difficult woman. By difficult I mean she once threw a saucepan at the priest."

The "telling" sentence is followed by a specific detail that earns it. Voice can announce and then prove. Pure showing without summary feels coy. Pure telling without specifics feels lazy. The hybrid is often the best move.

## Description

Description has three jobs:

1. **Orient the reader in space and time.**
2. **Establish mood.**
3. **Reveal the POV character through what they notice.**

The third is usually the most neglected. Two characters in the same room would describe it differently. The architect notices the load-bearing walls. The mother notices the sharp corners on the coffee table. The grieving widow notices the empty chair.

Your description is always also characterization. Treat it that way.

Avoid the inventory list. "The room had a couch, a coffee table, two chairs, a bookshelf, and a fireplace." This is real estate copy. Pick the one thing about the room that matters to the moment and the character, and let the reader fill in the rest.

## Dialogue Within Prose

How dialogue is wrapped in prose shapes how it reads. The same line can land differently depending on what surrounds it. See file 07 for dialogue specifically, but note here:

- A line of dialogue with no surrounding interiority is fast and external.
- A line followed by a beat of interiority slows the reader and weights the line.
- A line tagged with action (not "she said" but "she set down her fork") shows the speaker through behavior.

Vary these patterns. Pure exchanges of dialogue and pure interiority both flatten.

## Purple Prose and When It Works

Purple prose is overwritten: too many adjectives, strained metaphors, vocabulary inflated past what the moment supports. It is mostly a vice.

But sometimes prose needs to swell. A character looking at their dying mother. A view from a mountaintop after a long climb. A first kiss that means everything. Suppressed beauty is not a virtue when the moment demands beauty.

The test: would you cut this if the moment did not earn it? If yes, leave it. If the prose is decorating a moment that does not deserve decoration, cut.

## Common Sentence-Level Failures

- **Filtering verbs.** "She felt the cold," "He heard the door close," "She saw the man approach." Cut the filter to bring the reader closer: "The cold cut through her coat." Use filtering when you want to remind the reader of the perceiver.
- **Stacked adjectives.** "The tall, dark, brooding man." Pick one. The one that does the most work.
- **Said-bookisms.** Replacing "said" with retorted, exclaimed, pronounced, articulated, queried. "Said" is invisible and that is its virtue. Stick with it.
- **Adverb dialogue tags.** "She said sadly." Either show the sadness in the line, or use an action beat, or trust the line.
- **Echoes.** The same word used twice within a few sentences. Notice and replace.
- **Throat clearing.** Sentences that postpone the actual content. "She thought to herself that perhaps it might be wise to consider whether..." Cut to the action.

## Voice Maintenance

The most ambitious sentence-level goal: every paragraph should feel like only this narrator could have written it.

To maintain voice across a long draft:

1. **Reread the previous chapter before writing the next.** Get back into rhythm.
2. **Keep a "voice document"** with sample paragraphs that capture the narrator at their best. Refer when drifting.
3. **Pick a few distinctive habits** the narrator has (a metaphor they would reach for, a kind of joke they make, a thing they refuse to say directly) and use them consistently.
4. **Read aloud during revision.** Voice is rhythm and you can hear it when you read.

## The First Paragraph

The first paragraph promises the book. It tells the reader what kind of voice they will be living with, what tone, what level of intimacy. Spend time on it. Rewrite it after the draft is done.

Test: does the first paragraph contain a sentence that could not appear in any other novel? If yes, you have voice. If the first paragraph could open any number of books, you have not yet found the voice for this one.
