# 02. Story Structure

Structure is not a formula. It is the shape that emerges when characters under pressure pursue goals against resistance over time. Every framework below describes the same underlying physics from a different angle. Pick the one that helps you see your specific story most clearly. Do not pick more than one at a time during drafting.

## The Universal Spine

Beneath every framework is the same five-beat skeleton:

1. **A character with a stable but flawed life.** They have a wound, a lie they believe, an emptiness they may not acknowledge.
2. **An inciting event** shatters that stability and forces them out.
3. **Escalating attempts** to restore stability or pursue a new goal, each failing harder.
4. **A crisis** where they must change who they are to succeed (or refuse to and fall).
5. **A new equilibrium** that reflects what they have become.

Every structure on this page is a different decoration of this spine.

## Three-Act Structure

The default Western dramatic shape. Goes back to Aristotle.

- **Act One (25%):** Setup. Establish the world, the protagonist, what they want and lack. End with the inciting incident and their commitment to pursue (Plot Point 1).
- **Act Two (50%):** Confrontation. Rising complications, deepening stakes. A midpoint reversal flips the situation or reveals new information. End with the dark night of the soul, the lowest point (Plot Point 2).
- **Act Three (25%):** Resolution. The protagonist, transformed by everything previous, confronts the final test. Climax. Denouement.

Why it endures: it tracks the natural curve of escalation and reversal that holds audience attention. The midpoint is structurally critical. Most sagging middles sag because there is no real midpoint shift.

## Save the Cat (Blake Snyder)

A 15-beat refinement of three-act, originally for screenplays but adaptable to novels. Percentages assume a 100k word book.

1. Opening Image (0%)
2. Theme Stated (5%)
3. Setup (1 to 10%)
4. Catalyst (10%) inciting incident
5. Debate (10 to 20%)
6. Break Into Two (20%) commits to new world
7. B Story (22%) often the relationship or thematic counterpoint
8. Fun and Games (20 to 50%) promise of the premise
9. Midpoint (50%) false victory or false defeat
10. Bad Guys Close In (50 to 75%)
11. All Is Lost (75%)
12. Dark Night of the Soul (75 to 80%)
13. Break Into Three (80%) the insight, the new plan
14. Finale (80 to 99%)
15. Final Image (100%) mirror of opening

Useful because it forces you to identify a specific midpoint event and a specific dark moment. Both are commonly missing in failing drafts.

## Hero's Journey (Joseph Campbell, simplified by Vogler)

Best for adventure, fantasy, coming of age, and any story where the protagonist crosses into an unfamiliar world.

1. Ordinary World
2. Call to Adventure
3. Refusal of the Call
4. Meeting the Mentor
5. Crossing the Threshold
6. Tests, Allies, Enemies
7. Approach to the Inmost Cave
8. Ordeal
9. Reward
10. The Road Back
11. Resurrection
12. Return with the Elixir

The journey is psychological as much as physical. The cave is whatever the hero most fears. The resurrection is the climactic test of who they have become. Use lightly; the literal twelve-step version produces formulaic stories.

## Dan Harmon's Story Circle

A stripped down hero's journey in eight beats, especially good for episodic structure and shorter forms.

1. **You** (a character in a zone of comfort)
2. **Need** (they want something)
3. **Go** (they enter an unfamiliar situation)
4. **Search** (they adapt to it)
5. **Find** (they get what they wanted)
6. **Take** (they pay a price for it)
7. **Return** (they go back to their familiar situation)
8. **Change** (having changed)

The genius is the symmetry. Beat 5 mirrors beat 1, beat 6 mirrors beat 2, and so on. The change is measured by the contrast between mirrored beats.

## Kishōtenketsu (Japanese four-act)

A non-conflict structure common in East Asian narrative.

1. **Ki (introduction):** Establish the situation and characters.
2. **Shō (development):** Develop without major change.
3. **Ten (twist):** An unexpected element, often unrelated to the previous beats, reframes everything.
4. **Ketsu (conclusion):** The reconciliation of the twist with what came before.

There is no antagonist required. The energy comes from juxtaposition rather than conflict. Good model for slice of life, literary fiction, and stories where the goal is meaning rather than resolution.

## Story Grid (Shawn Coyne)

A genre-aware framework. Coyne argues every scene must contain a "value shift" (positive to negative or vice versa on the genre's primary value, for example justice for crime, love for romance) and every scene must answer five questions:

1. What is the inciting incident of the scene?
2. What is the progressive complication?
3. What is the crisis (the dilemma)?
4. What is the climax?
5. What is the resolution?

If a scene does not shift values and answer all five, it is not a scene. It is filler. This is harsh but useful. Run failed scenes through this filter before cutting them.

## Romancing the Beat (Gwen Hayes), the romance frame

Romance has its own non-negotiable structure because the genre is the contract.

- **Setup:** Heroine in her ordinary world. Meet the love interest. Sparks of conflict.
- **Falling in Love:** Increasing intimacy, growing attraction, deepening stakes.
- **Retreat:** Internal and external forces drive them apart. The "all is lost" moment is the breakup.
- **Fighting for Love:** Grand gesture, growth, commitment.
- **Happily Ever After or Happily For Now.**

If you are writing romance, this is not optional. The genre contract demands it.

## Picking a Structure

For commercial plot driven fiction: Save the Cat or Story Grid.
For mythic adventure: Hero's Journey or Story Circle.
For literary or slice of life: loose three-act or kishōtenketsu.
For romance, mystery, thriller, horror: genre-specific beat sheets exist; use them.

Use the structure during planning and revision. During drafting, forget it. Trying to hit beats consciously while writing prose produces mechanical work.

## Common Structural Failures

- **No real midpoint.** The middle drifts because nothing pivots at the halfway mark. Fix: insert a revelation, betrayal, or escalation that makes the second half a fundamentally different game.
- **Climax handed to the protagonist.** The hero solves the final problem with luck, a deus ex machina, or someone else's help. Fix: the climax must be the protagonist using what they learned, paying the price they avoided.
- **Anticlimactic dark night.** The "all is lost" moment is too tame. Fix: take everything from them, including the thing they thought they could not lose.
- **Resolution that does not resolve theme.** Plot ends but the thematic question hangs. Fix: the final image should answer (or refuse to answer in a meaningful way) the question raised at the start.

## Subplot Architecture

Subplots are not garnish. They serve specific functions:

- **Mirror subplots** show the protagonist's journey from another angle (different character facing similar choice).
- **Contrast subplots** show what happens when someone makes the opposite choice.
- **Pressure subplots** add complications that force the protagonist to act faster or sacrifice more.
- **Relief subplots** vary tone (comedy in tragedy, tenderness in horror).

A subplot must intersect the main plot at climax. If you can remove the subplot without the ending changing, it is decoration and probably needs cutting.

## Pacing Across the Whole

Pacing is not "fast" or "slow." It is the rate at which questions get raised and answered. Steady pacing means new questions raised slightly faster than old ones close, with a periodic clearing that lets the reader breathe before the next surge.

Tools for controlling pace:

- **Scene length.** Shorter scenes accelerate. Longer scenes deepen.
- **Chapter breaks.** A cliffhanger at chapter end pulls the reader forward.
- **Time compression.** Summary handles time you do not want the reader to live through.
- **Real-time scenes.** Drag the reader through every second of the moments that matter most.

A book that is the same speed throughout reads as monotone. Vary it deliberately.
