# 03. Character

Plot is what characters do under pressure. If the characters are not real, no plot can save the book. Conversely, weak plotting can be partly redeemed by characters the reader genuinely loves. Spend disproportionate effort here.

## Want vs Need

The single most important distinction in character design.

**Want:** the external, conscious goal. What the character thinks will make them happy. Often visible to the reader from the first chapter.

**Need:** the internal, often unconscious truth about what they actually require to be whole. Frequently the opposite of, or in tension with, the want.

The arc is the journey from pursuing the want to recognizing the need. The climax is the moment they must choose one or the other.

Examples:
- **Want:** win the case. **Need:** forgive himself for the case he lost ten years ago.
- **Want:** find her missing sister. **Need:** stop using her sister as the excuse not to live her own life.

The want drives plot. The need drives theme. Both must be specific and concrete.

## The Wound, the Lie, the Truth (Lisa Cron, K.M. Weiland)

A useful frame for building protagonist interiority.

- **The Wound:** a past event that shaped the protagonist before the story begins. It does not have to be dramatic. It has to be formative.
- **The Lie:** the belief about themselves or the world that the wound produced. "I am only safe when I am in control." "Love always ends in abandonment." "If I am not useful, I do not deserve to exist."
- **The Truth:** what they need to learn to be free. The lie's antidote.

The story is the process of testing the lie until it breaks and the truth replaces it. Every major plot event should pressure the lie. The dark night is when the lie's full cost becomes visible. The climax is when they act on the truth, often at great cost.

## Character Arcs: Three Types

**Positive arc:** Character begins believing the lie, suffers, and ends embracing the truth. The dominant arc in commercial fiction. The protagonist grows.

**Negative arc (corruption):** Character begins with at least a glimpse of the truth, is tempted by a lie, embraces it, and is destroyed or diminished by it. Macbeth, Walter White, Michael Corleone.

**Flat arc:** Character already knows the truth. The story tests their commitment to it while they change the world around them. Common for series leads (Sherlock Holmes), Westerns, and many literary tragedies. The character changes others rather than being changed.

Pick one arc type per protagonist and commit. Mixed arcs feel like the writer did not know what they were doing.

## Interiority

Interiority is the texture of a character's mind on the page. It is what distinguishes fiction from screenplay. Without it, you are writing a transcript.

Interiority includes:

- What the character notices (and what they do not).
- How they interpret what they notice.
- Memories triggered by present events.
- Judgments, opinions, biases.
- Physical sensations rendered as feeling.
- The gap between what they think and what they say.

A scene in a coffee shop with no interiority is two people ordering drinks. A scene in a coffee shop with interiority is one person aware of the way the other will not meet their eyes, remembering the last time they sat at this table together, calculating how to phrase what they came to say, noticing how warm the cup feels in their cold hands.

Rule of thumb: every page of dialogue and action needs at least one beat of interiority anchoring you in whose head we are in.

## Voice

Each character should sound like only themselves. This is not just dialect or vocabulary. It is what they care about, what they refuse to talk about, how long their sentences run, whether they use metaphor and what kind, what they joke about, what they take seriously.

Quick test: cover the dialogue tags. Can you tell who is speaking? If not, the voices are not differentiated enough.

To find a character's voice:

1. What do they fear most? (Avoidance shapes language.)
2. What is their best skill? (Expertise shapes metaphor.)
3. Where did they grow up? (Rhythm and idiom.)
4. What is their relationship to honesty? (Direct, evasive, performative, deadpan?)
5. What is their education and how do they feel about it? (Vocabulary and self-consciousness.)

## The Supporting Cast

Supporting characters exist to:

1. **Pressure the protagonist** toward or away from the truth.
2. **Reveal facets of the protagonist** that would not otherwise be visible.
3. **Embody thematic positions** that argue with the protagonist's worldview.
4. **Provide texture and contrast.**

Every named character should have at least one clear function. If a character can be cut without changing the protagonist's journey, cut them or combine them with another.

Common supporting roles:

- **Mentor:** holds the truth the protagonist needs to learn. Often dies, leaves, or is proven flawed at midpoint.
- **Foil:** structurally opposite to the protagonist; their choices illuminate by contrast.
- **Ally:** loyal, often the voice the protagonist refuses to hear.
- **Love interest:** in romance, the co-protagonist. Elsewhere, often the embodiment of what the protagonist could become if they embraced the truth.
- **Antagonist:** see below.

## The Antagonist

The antagonist is the character whose goals most directly oppose the protagonist's. They do not have to be evil. They have to be effective.

The strongest antagonists:

1. **Want something specific** (not "world domination" but "to keep my daughter from finding out who I am").
2. **Believe they are the hero of their own story.** From their POV, the protagonist is the obstacle.
3. **Are good at what they do.** Incompetent antagonists produce boring conflict.
4. **Reflect the protagonist's fear** of who they might become if they embraced the lie.
5. **Force the protagonist into impossible choices** rather than easy fights.

If you can give the antagonist a scene from their own POV that makes the reader briefly sympathize, they are working.

## Showing Character Through Choice

Characters reveal themselves through what they choose under pressure. Not what they say about themselves. Not what other characters say about them. What they do when something is at stake.

The first time we meet a character, give them a small choice that hints at their core. Not exposition. Choice.

Example: a character could enter the book by getting a cup of coffee. Or they could enter the book by giving the last seat on the bus to a stranger, then resenting them quietly for the rest of the ride. The second version has already done more work.

## Backstory: Use Sparingly

The reader does not need to know how the character got this way. The reader needs to feel the consequences. Hold backstory until it is necessary to understand the present moment. Drip it in. A long flashback chapter near the start is almost always a mistake.

Rule: introduce a character through their present action, let the reader form impressions, then drip the backstory that complicates those impressions.

## The Cast Audit

Before drafting, list every character and answer for each:

1. What do they want in this story?
2. What do they fear?
3. What is their relationship to the protagonist's wound or lie?
4. What unique function do they serve?
5. What is their voice in one sentence?

Any character without clear answers gets cut or merged with another.

## Names

Names matter more than writers admit. They are the first impression the reader gets of a character and they recur on every page. A few principles:

- Avoid similar-sounding names in the same book (Jon, Jane, June, James in one cast confuses readers).
- Vary syllable counts.
- Match the world. A medieval fantasy character should not be named Tyler unless that incongruity is doing thematic work.
- Read names out loud. If they trip the tongue every time, change them.
- Avoid meaning-heavy names ("Crystal Snow" for a cold character) unless the genre supports it.
