# 09. Worldbuilding

Worldbuilding is not the exclusive province of fantasy and sci-fi. Every novel has a world: the social rules, the physical environment, the economic conditions, the textures of daily life. A literary novel set in present-day Brooklyn is worldbuilding as much as a space opera.

## The Iceberg Principle for Worlds

You should know far more about your world than you ever show. The reader needs to feel weight beneath the surface. They do not need to read the encyclopedia.

For a fantasy novel, you might know the magic system's underlying physics, the political history of the last five centuries, the economics of grain export. The reader sees a tax collector arguing with a baker about a half-bushel of flour and trusts that everything behind that argument is real.

The signs of worldbuilding done with depth:

- Background details cohere with each other.
- Characters refer to things in passing as if they are common knowledge.
- The world has problems that exist independent of the protagonist.
- The world predates the story and will continue after it.

The signs of worldbuilding done shallowly:

- Information arrives in lectures.
- The protagonist is always being told things they should already know.
- Details contradict each other.
- The world serves only the plot and has no life otherwise.

## Show the World Through Friction

A world reveals itself when characters bump against it. They argue with it, work around it, comply unhappily with it. Friction is where the rules become visible without being explained.

Bad: "The city was ruled by a council of seven elected priests who served lifetime terms and could not be removed except by the unanimous vote of the remaining six."

Good: "Selene watched the new priest take his oath. Forty-three years old. The youngest she had seen. By the time he stepped down, she would be dead and her daughter would be middle aged. That was the system. That had always been the system. She wondered, watching him kneel, whether he understood what he had just signed up for."

The second version conveys the rule, the duration, the social position, and an emotional response, all in motion.

## Sensory Specificity

A world is built one sensory detail at a time. The mistake is reaching for generic descriptors. The corrective is finding the specific:

- Not "the marketplace was busy" but "the marketplace smelled of oranges and rust."
- Not "the spaceship was futuristic" but "the chair adjusted to her posture before she finished sitting down."
- Not "the office was sterile" but "every surface was white and slightly cold to the touch."

A single specific sensory detail can establish a setting more vividly than three paragraphs of description.

## The Five Layers of a World

When developing a world, work through these layers. You do not need to invent everything for each. You do need to know which layer the next scene operates on.

1. **Physical.** Geography, climate, biology, the material conditions of life.
2. **Technological or magical.** What is possible in this world that is not possible in ours, or vice versa.
3. **Social.** Class, status, gender, family structure, race, friendship, romance.
4. **Political and economic.** Power, money, work, governance, war, trade.
5. **Cultural and spiritual.** What people believe, fear, hope for, joke about, find beautiful, find shameful.

Most weak worldbuilding stops at the first two layers. The deeper layers are where worlds feel real.

## Daily Life

The fastest way to make a world feel lived in is to render daily life. What do people eat. How do they wake up. What do they argue about. What do they do for fun. How do they bury their dead. What jokes does a child make. What does a tired worker order at the end of a shift.

A scene of the protagonist eating breakfast in your fantasy city can do more worldbuilding than a chapter of expository history. The details of how the bread is made, what is served with it, who serves whom, what gets discussed at the table, all carry the world.

## The Magic System or Technology

If your story involves magic or technology beyond the contemporary, the rules of how it works matter. Brandon Sanderson's three laws of magic are useful:

1. **Your ability to solve problems with magic in a satisfying way is directly proportional to how well the reader understands said magic.** Soft magic systems (Tolkien) work for atmosphere; the protagonist cannot solve major problems with them or it feels arbitrary. Hard magic systems (Sanderson) have explicit rules and the protagonist can use them creatively.
2. **Limitations are more interesting than powers.** What the magic cannot do, what it costs, what it forbids, are where story comes from.
3. **Expand on what you have before adding new things.** Deepen existing rules rather than constantly introducing new abilities.

The same principles apply to advanced technology in science fiction.

## Resisting the Worldbuilding Trap

Writers who love worldbuilding sometimes produce books where the world is the star and the characters are tourists. The world is gorgeous, the plot is thin, the characters are vehicles for showing off the setting.

Symptoms:
- Long descriptive passages with no character interiority.
- Dialogue that exists to explain world details.
- A protagonist whose function is to wander and observe.
- Plot events chosen for the worldbuilding they showcase rather than character stakes.

Correctives:
- Cut all worldbuilding that does not affect a character's choice.
- For every detail you include, ask: does it pressure the protagonist or change the scene?
- Remember that the most beautiful map ever drawn is not a story.

## The Worldbuilding Bible

For long projects, especially fantasy and science fiction, keep a private document with:

- Maps (rough sketches are fine).
- Timeline of major historical events.
- Political structures and current rulers.
- Magic or technology rules.
- Calendar, money, units of measure.
- Major religions or belief systems.
- Languages and naming conventions.
- Notes on specific cultures, classes, professions.

The reader will never see this document. It is for you, so that the world stays consistent across hundreds of pages.

Update it as you draft. New details that emerge during writing get logged so you do not contradict yourself in chapter twenty.

## Naming Things

Consistent naming conventions are worldbuilding shorthand. The names of people, places, foods, gods, all should feel as if they come from the same world.

A medieval European fantasy with characters named Aelric, Thora, and Marcus does not work unless the cultural blending is intentional. A space station with chambers named Aurora, Beta-7, and Sun Hyung either gestures at a deliberately mixed-origin society or reads as careless.

Tools:
- Pick one or two source-language patterns and stick to them.
- Make sure the patterns are pronounceable. The reader's eye will trip on apostrophe-heavy invented names.
- Vary syllable counts so names do not all sound alike.
- Avoid names that contemporary readers will hear as joke names unintentionally.

## Setting as Character

The strongest settings function almost like characters. They have personality, mood, history. They resist or aid the protagonist. They have things they want to express through what they show and hide.

A New England farmhouse in October has a different personality than a New York apartment in August. Both are settings. Both can become characters in the way they pressure the people inside them.

To activate setting as character:
- Give it specific habits and tendencies.
- Let it change in mood across scenes.
- Have characters react to it as if it were aware.
- Use weather and time of day to shift its mood.

## When to Worldbuild Less

If your story is character-driven and contemporary, worldbuild less than you think you need to. The reader supplies most of the world themselves from their own life. Your job is to add the specifics that make this particular setting come alive: not "a coffee shop" but the one with the misspelled menu and the barista with the bad tattoo.

The literary novel's worldbuilding is the same skill applied at smaller scale. The world is here, our world, but rendered with specificity that makes it strange and visible again.
