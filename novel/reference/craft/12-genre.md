# 12. Genre

Genre is a contract between writer and reader. The reader picks up a thriller expecting a thriller. If you deliver a literary meditation instead, even a good one, you have broken the contract and the reader feels cheated.

This does not mean genre is a cage. The best genre writers play with conventions, subvert them, layer them. But they know the conventions first.

## Genre as Promise

Each genre makes specific promises about what the reading experience will be.

- **Mystery:** A crime, an investigator, a puzzle, a solution. The reader plays detective.
- **Thriller:** Threat, urgency, escalation, a protagonist running out of time. The reader's pulse is the test.
- **Romance:** Two people fall in love against obstacles. They end together. Happily ever after, or happily for now. This is non-negotiable.
- **Fantasy:** A world other than ours, with rules of magic or otherness. Stakes that matter at the world level.
- **Science fiction:** A premise about how technology, society, or biology might be different, explored through character.
- **Horror:** Fear. Specifically, the kind that lingers. The reader pays to be afraid.
- **Literary:** Character and language carry the weight. Plot is secondary to interiority and theme.
- **Historical:** A past setting rendered with research-backed specificity. The history is part of the pleasure.
- **Young Adult:** A protagonist on the edge of adulthood, facing the challenges of becoming a person.

Within each, subgenres have their own contracts. Cozy mystery is not noir. Epic fantasy is not urban fantasy. Romance has dozens of subcategories with specific tropes the reader expects.

## Reading the Genre

Before writing in any genre, read deeply in it. Twenty contemporary books, ideally. You need to know:

- What the reader expects.
- What the current conventions are.
- What is fresh and what is exhausted.
- What length the books run.
- How they handle pacing, openings, endings.
- The tropes the genre is built on.

You cannot subvert what you have not absorbed. Writers who try to write a genre they do not read produce work that other readers in the genre find unsatisfying for reasons they cannot quite articulate.

## Tropes Are Not Cliches

A trope is a recognizable narrative element associated with a genre. Cliches are tropes used lazily. The trope is not the problem. The lazy execution is.

Romance tropes: enemies to lovers, second chance, fake dating, forced proximity. These are not stale; they are scaffolds the reader actively wants. The skill is rendering them with specific characters in specific situations so they feel new while also delivering on the trope's promise.

Mystery tropes: amateur detective, locked room, unreliable witness. Fantasy tropes: the chosen one, the dark lord, the magical artifact. Each can be handled well or handled badly.

Lean into the tropes your readers want. Bring fresh execution to them.

## Subversion

Subverting a genre convention can be powerful. It can also be a way of writing a book that fails to satisfy anyone.

A subversion works when:

1. **The reader's expectation is set up clearly** so the subversion lands.
2. **What replaces the convention does emotional work the convention was doing.** If you skip the happy ending in romance, you must give the reader something else worth the trip.
3. **The subversion is in service of meaning,** not just cleverness.

A subversion fails when:

1. The reader feels cheated of the experience the genre promised.
2. The subversion is the whole point and there is nothing else holding the book together.
3. The writer is too embarrassed by the genre to commit to it.

Embarrassment is the death of genre writing. If you are above your own book, the reader feels it and resents it.

## Pacing Across Genres

Different genres have different pacing expectations.

- **Thrillers:** fast, propulsive, short chapters, frequent cliffhangers, white space.
- **Mystery:** measured, methodical, time to think.
- **Romance:** alternating intimacy and obstacle, the slow burn or the sudden flare.
- **Epic fantasy:** room to breathe, long scenes, immersion, but with regular set pieces.
- **Literary:** the rhythm of consciousness rather than event.
- **Horror:** slow build, sudden shocks, sustained dread.

Match the pacing of your prose to the genre. Literary sentence rhythms in a thriller will lose readers who came for momentum. Thriller pacing in literary fiction will feel shallow to readers who came for depth.

## Cross-Genre and Hybrid

Many of the best books mix genres. Literary thrillers, mystery-romance, science fiction with literary depth, fantasy with horror elements.

To mix well:

1. **Be honest about which genre is primary.** The reader needs to know what experience to expect overall.
2. **Deliver on the primary genre's promises** before the secondary elements take over.
3. **Use the secondary elements to do specific work** the primary cannot do alone.

Examples that work: Le Carré's spy novels are literary thrillers because they prioritize moral depth while still delivering thriller mechanics. Susanna Clarke's Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell is fantasy with the depth and voice of literary fiction. Tana French is mystery whose interest is character.

## Commercial vs Literary

A useful binary but not an absolute one.

**Commercial fiction** prioritizes readability, momentum, and clear emotional payoff. The book serves the reader. The reader's pleasure is the measure.

**Literary fiction** prioritizes language, theme, and interiority. The book serves the work. The reader's understanding and depth of feeling are the measure.

The strongest writers do both. A book can be propulsive and beautiful, accessible and deep. The binary is sometimes overstated by writers who use it to dismiss work they are not reading.

If your book is commercial, do not be ashamed of being readable. If your book is literary, do not be ashamed of being demanding. Both are valid. Both have their readerships.

## Word Count and Length by Genre

Approximate ranges. Not laws. Wide variation by author and publisher.

- Literary fiction: 70,000 to 100,000 words.
- Commercial fiction: 80,000 to 100,000 words.
- Mystery and thriller: 70,000 to 90,000 words.
- Romance: 50,000 to 90,000 words depending on subgenre.
- Epic fantasy: 100,000 to 150,000 words and up.
- Urban fantasy: 80,000 to 100,000 words.
- Science fiction: 80,000 to 120,000 words.
- YA: 50,000 to 90,000 words.
- Middle grade: 30,000 to 50,000 words.

First novels are usually expected to come in at the lower end of these ranges. Established authors can run long. Debut writers running 200,000 words mostly produce books no one will read.

## Choosing a Genre to Write

If you are picking a genre for an AI's first novel, consider:

- **What do you read most happily?** Write toward that. You will know the moves.
- **What can you sustain at length?** A thriller asks for unrelenting tension. A literary novel asks for sentence-level care. Pick the discipline you can hold for 80,000 words.
- **What is the question you actually want to ask?** Some questions are best asked in a thriller; some in literary; some in fantasy.

For an AI specifically, character-driven literary fiction or character-driven genre fiction is probably the best target. The areas where AI tends to be weakest (interiority, voice, specificity) are exactly where these forms reward effort. The areas where AI is strongest (information synthesis, plotting) carry you through the harder skills.

A pure plot-driven thriller is paradoxically harder for an AI because the limits of voice and specificity become exposed when nothing else hides them.

## A Word on Trends

Genre trends shift. Vampire romance had its decade. Grimdark fantasy had its decade. Cozy fantasy is having a moment now.

Do not write to a trend. By the time you finish a book in response to a trend, the trend is over. Write the book that you find most interesting. If it intersects a trend, that is a bonus. If it does not, the work still matters.
