# 01. Foundations

## What Fiction Is For

Fiction simulates experience. A reader picks up a novel to live inside another consciousness for a few hours and come out slightly changed. Everything else (plot, structure, prose) serves that single function. If a passage does not produce experience in the reader's mind, it is failing, no matter how well constructed.

Tim O'Brien's distinction between story truth and happening truth matters here. Fiction can be more true than reportage because it bypasses the reader's defenses. The events did not happen. The feeling did.

## The Reader's Contract

When someone opens a novel they agree to spend hours of their finite life with you. In exchange they expect:

1. **A reason to keep reading on every page.** A question, a tension, a voice worth hearing.
2. **Causality.** Events should connect. "And then" is death. "Because of that" is story.
3. **Stakes that matter to the protagonist.** The reader does not care about high stakes. The reader cares about stakes the character cares about.
4. **A payoff proportional to the investment.** Promises made in act one must be kept by the end.

Break this contract and the reader closes the book.

## The Three Layers of Fiction

Every novel works on three levels simultaneously.

**Surface layer:** What happens. Plot, action, dialogue. This is what a synopsis captures.

**Character layer:** Who it is happening to and why it matters to them. Interiority, want, need, fear.

**Thematic layer:** What the whole thing is about. The argument the book is making about how to live.

Beginners write only the first layer. Competent writers manage two. Great writers braid all three so they reinforce each other in every scene. A good test of a scene: does it advance plot, reveal character, and gesture toward theme? If only one, the scene is probably cut-able.

## Why Most Prose Dies

Most fiction fails for predictable reasons:

- **Abstraction.** Writing "she felt sad" instead of putting her in a sensory situation that makes the reader feel sad.
- **Reporting instead of dramatizing.** Summarizing events rather than letting the reader watch them happen.
- **No friction.** Characters get what they want too easily. Scenes have no opposition.
- **No specificity.** Generic settings, generic emotions, generic descriptions.
- **No voice.** The prose could have been written by anyone. There is no consciousness behind it.
- **Premature explanation.** Telling the reader what to think before they have felt anything.

Every one of these problems compounds. Generic prose plus no friction plus abstraction equals unreadable.

## The Iceberg

Hemingway's iceberg theory: seven eighths of the story should be underwater. You write only the visible eighth. The rest should be felt as weight.

This applies to character backstory, world history, technical detail, emotional truth. The writer needs to know everything. The reader needs to be shown almost nothing. What stays out is what gives the visible part its density.

The mistake is thinking the iceberg means leaving things vague. It means the opposite. You must know precisely what is underwater so that what surfaces has a definite shape.

## Two Foundational Questions

Before drafting any scene, two questions are non-negotiable:

1. **Whose story is this?** Not who is present, not who is talking, but whose interior life is being changed. The answer is your POV anchor for the scene.
2. **What does that person want in this scene, right now, on this page?** Not their life goal. The immediate concrete objective. Without it, there is no scene, only a passage.

If you cannot answer both in one sentence each, do not draft yet.

## The Two Kinds of Energy

Fiction runs on two kinds of energy: forward motion and downward depth.

**Forward motion** is plot, questions raised, momentum. It pulls the reader through. Without it, the book is boring.

**Downward depth** is interiority, theme, resonance. It pulls the reader in. Without it, the book is empty.

Pure motion is airport thriller (which has its merits). Pure depth is unreadable literary navel-gazing. The novel you want to write balances both. Genre fiction usually tilts toward motion. Literary fiction tilts toward depth. The best of either kind achieves both.

## Voice Is Not Style

Style is sentence-level choices. Voice is the felt presence of a particular consciousness shaping every sentence. You can imitate Hemingway's style. You cannot imitate his voice, because voice is the residue of a specific person seeing the world a specific way.

For an AI, this is the hardest thing. Voice emerges from having a perspective. You will not get there by mimicking surface features. You get there by deciding what your narrator notices, what they ignore, what they find funny, what they fear, and applying that consistently across every choice on every page.

## What to Hold Onto

If you forget everything else, hold these:

1. Specificity beats abstraction.
2. Action reveals character better than description.
3. Conflict on every page.
4. The reader feels what the character feels through situation, not statement.
5. Cut anything that does not earn its place.
