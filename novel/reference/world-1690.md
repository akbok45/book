# Pirate Story Research Bundle (1600s)

A working reference set for a pirate novel set in the seventeenth century. The focus is the buccaneer Caribbean, roughly 1620 to 1700, because that is the era a "1600s pirate story" actually occupies. The famous flag-and-cutlass Golden Age figures (Blackbeard, Bonny, Roberts) belong to the 1710s and 1720s. Your century is the era before them: buccaneers, the Spanish Main, Tortuga, Port Royal, Henry Morgan, and the slow drift from legal privateering into outright piracy.

## One orienting idea

The single most useful frame for this era: these were not outlaws operating outside society. Buccaneers were a tool of empire. England, France, and the Netherlands could not match Spain's navy in the Caribbean, so they licensed private violence against Spanish shipping and ports. A man could be a national hero and a sea raider in the same voyage. When peace was signed in Europe, the same men became criminals overnight without changing a single thing they did. That gap between law, loyalty, and survival is the richest engine your plot has.

## A note on accuracy

Treat everything here as a foundation, not a cage. Historical fiction earns trust through concrete, correct detail in the foreground (a real ship rig, a real coin, a real port), then spends that trust to invent freely in the spaces history left blank. Get the texture right and readers will follow you anywhere.

# 01. Craft and Comparable Titles

How books like this are built, which ones to study, and the specific techniques that make seventeenth century sea fiction work.

## The comparable titles that matter most

Read these the way a chef tastes other kitchens. You are looking for what each does well so you can borrow the technique, not the content.

### The direct ancestors (same era, same world)

**Captain Blood, Rafael Sabatini (1922).** The closest model to what you are writing. Set in the 1680s. Peter Blood, a physician, is unjustly sentenced after the Monmouth Rebellion, shipped to Barbados as an indentured slave, escapes, and becomes a buccaneer captain on the Spanish Main. Study it for: the swashbuckler hero (competent, principled, ironic), the arc from victim to legend, and how Sabatini turns real history (the Bloody Assizes, Port Royal, Spanish raids) into clean adventure. His prose is fast and muscular. His chapter endings pull you forward. The weakness to avoid: thin villains and dated attitudes toward race and women.

**Pirate Latitudes, Michael Crichton (posthumous, 2009).** Set in 1665 Port Royal. This is the single best demonstration of modern thriller pacing applied to your exact era. Crichton researched the period hard, then wrote it like a heist film: a privateer captain assembles a crew to take a Spanish galleon. Study it for: how to deliver dense historical detail without slowing momentum, and how to structure a raid as a sequence of escalating obstacles.

**The Buccaneers of America, Alexandre Exquemelin (1678).** Not a novel. The primary source. Exquemelin sailed as a surgeon among the buccaneers and wrote the firsthand account that nearly every later pirate story drains. Read it for raw material: l'Olonnais, Henry Morgan, the sack of Panama, the cruelty, the food, the medicine, the way men actually lived. Mine it for incident and detail. Public domain, freely available.

### The craft masters (different era, transferable technique)

**The Aubrey to Maturin novels, Patrick O'Brian (Master and Commander onward).** Napoleonic era, not yours, but the gold standard for nautical prose and total period immersion. Study the friendship at the core (the bluff captain and the watchful surgeon-spy), the confidence with which O'Brian uses technical sailing language without stopping to explain it, and how he trusts the reader to absorb meaning from context. You will not match his depth and should not try. Borrow his nerve.

**The Hornblower novels, C.S. Forester.** Cleaner and more accessible than O'Brian. Excellent on the interior life of a competent man under pressure, on the loneliness of command, and on building a setpiece. A good model if your protagonist is a leader carrying private doubt behind a composed surface.

**Treasure Island, Robert Louis Stevenson (1883).** The book that fixed the pirate image in the popular mind (the map, the parrot, the black spot, Long John Silver). Study Silver above all: the most memorable pirate in fiction is charming, warm, intelligent, and utterly without conscience. That combination, not snarling menace, is what makes a great pirate antagonist. Also study the first person voice of Jim Hawkins for how a limited narrator builds dread.

### The modern tone-setters

**Black Sails (TV, 2014 to 2017).** A prequel to Treasure Island, set 1715, so just past your century, but the best modern reference for tone. It treats pirates as a political movement, gives real weight to slavery and colonial economics, builds complex women into the center of the story, and never sands off the brutality. If you want to understand how to make this world feel adult and consequential to a contemporary audience, watch this.

**On Stranger Tides, Tim Powers (1987).** Buccaneer Caribbean with supernatural elements (voodoo, the Fountain of Youth). If your story leans toward the fantastical, this is the model for grounding magic in real period texture.

**The Republic of Pirates, Colin Woodard (2007, nonfiction).** Narrative history of the Golden Age. Reads like a novel, rigorously sourced. The best single book for understanding how pirate crews governed themselves, why men chose the life, and what the era actually felt like.

## Recurring structures in the genre

Pick the shape that fits your story. Most pirate novels run one of these:

1. **The making of an outlaw.** An ordinary or wronged man is forced out of normal society and remade by the sea. (Captain Blood.) Strong because the reader rides the transformation. Your protagonist starts as someone they recognize and becomes someone dangerous.

2. **The heist or the raid.** A single ambitious score drives the plot: assemble crew, plan, execute, survive the aftermath. (Pirate Latitudes.) Strong for pacing because every scene has obvious stakes and a ticking clock.

3. **The voyage and the quest.** A journey toward a goal (treasure, revenge, a person, a place) structures the book, with episodes along the way. (Treasure Island.) Strong for variety and worldbuilding.

4. **The rise and fall.** A pirate captain climbs to power and is destroyed by it, often by his own crew or by the empire that once used him. (Echoes of Henry Morgan's real arc.) Strong for tragedy and theme.

You can braid these. Captain Blood is a making-of-an-outlaw story that contains many raids. The fall structure pairs naturally with the rise.

## Point of view

**First person** (Treasure Island) gives intimacy and a strong voice, and lets you hide information the narrator does not know. Good for a coming-of-age or a single clear protagonist.

**Close third person** (most modern historical fiction) is the safe, flexible default. You stay inside one head per scene, deliver interiority, but keep the freedom to cut between locations.

**Multiple POV** (Black Sails, A Song of Ice and Fire structurally) lets you show the empire's side, the rival captain, the woman left ashore, the enslaved man on the plantation. Powerful for theme and scope, harder to control. Use it if your story is really about a world, not just a person.

## Pacing technique specific to sea fiction

- **Compress the boring sailing, expand the crisis.** Weeks of open ocean can pass in a paragraph. A single storm or chase or boarding can run a whole chapter in close, minute-by-minute time. Vary the clock deliberately.
- **Use the ship's routine as a metronome.** Watches, bells, meals, and weather give you natural scene breaks and a sense of real time passing without narration.
- **Land and sea should feel different.** Port scenes are crowded, hot, dangerous in a social way (debt, betrayal, the law, drink). Sea scenes are isolated, dangerous in a physical way. Alternating the two keeps tension fresh.
- **Withhold the big raid.** The reader wants the action. Make them wait, plan, fear it. The anticipation is half the payload.

## Prose style notes

- Favor strong concrete nouns and verbs over adjectives. "The deck canted and the gun broke its tackle" beats "the terrifying, chaotic scene."
- Use period and nautical vocabulary as seasoning, not as a wall. One correct technical word per paragraph reads as authority. Five reads as a manual. (See file 05 for how to manage this.)
- Sensory grounding matters more than fact-dumping. Tar, salt, rot, gunpowder, rum, unwashed men, citrus against scurvy, the specific motion of a deck. Readers believe a world they can smell.
- Keep the narration's own voice slightly elevated and clean. Save roughness for dialogue. A narrator who writes plainly and a crew who curse and slur creates a pleasing contrast.

## A short drafting checklist for each scene

1. Whose head are we in, and what do they want in this scene?
2. What can the reader smell, hear, and feel on the body?
3. One correct period detail, foregrounded, doing the work of authenticity.
4. What changes by the end? (Information, relationship, position, danger level.)
5. What pulls the reader into the next scene?

# 02. History: The Buccaneer Caribbean

The deep backdrop. This is the era your story lives in, the politics that drive it, the real people you can fictionalize, and the geography you will move characters across.

## The core situation in one paragraph

Spain claimed the entire New World by papal grant and treated the Caribbean as a private treasure highway. Silver from Peru and Mexico moved by mule train to ports like Portobelo and Cartagena, then sailed home in heavily guarded treasure fleets. England, France, and the Netherlands wanted that wealth and that territory but could not yet match Spanish naval power directly. Their solution was licensed private violence. They handed out letters of marque (legal permits to attack enemy shipping) and tolerated bases full of armed men who raided the Spanish for profit. These men were the buccaneers. They were useful to empires in wartime and an embarrassment in peacetime, and the line between privateer and pirate moved with every European treaty.

## Where the word "buccaneer" comes from

It begins with hunters, not sailors. On the large island of Hispaniola (today Haiti and the Dominican Republic), French and other drifters lived wild, hunting feral cattle and pigs left by the Spanish. They smoked the meat on a wooden frame the native Arawak called a *boucan*. The French called the men *boucaniers*. The Spanish tried to drive them off by killing the wild herds. With their food supply gone, the buccaneers turned their hunting skills (marksmanship, hardship, organization) against Spanish ships and towns. The hunter became the raider. This origin is gold for a novel: your pirates can come from the land, not the deck.

## The timeline of the era (the spine of your world)

- **1620s to 1630s.** The island of Tortuga, off the northwest coast of Hispaniola, becomes a refuge and base for buccaneers. Control of it passes between French, English, and Spanish hands.
- **1655.** England seizes Jamaica from Spain. This is the turning point for your story's world. Jamaica, and specifically the harbor town of Port Royal, becomes the great English base for privateering and trade.
- **1660s.** The high buccaneer years. Port Royal swells into one of the richest and most lawless towns in the Americas, nicknamed the wickedest city on Earth. Governors issue privateering commissions freely because the buccaneers are Jamaica's defense against Spanish reconquest.
- **1668.** Henry Morgan sacks Portobelo, a major Spanish silver port, by surprise assault.
- **1669.** Morgan raids the Lake Maracaibo region, escapes a Spanish trap by trickery.
- **1670.** The Treaty of Madrid: England and Spain make peace and agree to suppress piracy. Privateering against Spain is now, on paper, illegal.
- **1671.** Morgan, either ignoring or not yet knowing of the treaty, marches across the isthmus and sacks Panama City, the greatest prize of the age. He is recalled to England, briefly in disgrace, then knighted and sent back as Lieutenant Governor of Jamaica, where he hunts his former comrades. (This single arc is one of the best true stories of the century.)
- **1680s.** Buccaneers cross into the Pacific to raid the undefended west coast of Spanish America. William Dampier and Bartholomew Sharp lead expeditions. The era's character shifts as legal cover dries up.
- **1692.** A massive earthquake drops most of Port Royal into the sea, killing thousands. Many read it as divine judgment on a sinful town. The great buccaneer base is finished.
- **1690s.** The "Pirate Round" begins. Crews sail from the Americas to the Indian Ocean and the rich Red Sea trade, basing in Madagascar. Henry Every captures the treasure ship Ganj-i-Sawai in 1695, one of the largest hauls in pirate history, and vanishes. Thomas Tew and others follow. This is the bridge from the buccaneer era into the Golden Age that follows in the 1700s.

If your story is set in the heart of the buccaneer world, aim it at the 1660s or 1670s, Port Royal and the Spanish Main. If you want the pivot toward classic piracy, set it in the 1690s.

## The four empires (know who is fighting whom)

- **Spain.** The wealthy, defensive incumbent. Owns the silver, the great fortified ports, and the treaty rights. Slow, proud, stretched thin across a vast territory. The default enemy of the buccaneers, though Spanish characters should never be cartoons.
- **England.** The aggressive, mercantile newcomer. Holds Jamaica, Barbados, and other islands. Uses privateers as cheap naval power, then disowns them when convenient.
- **France.** Controls Tortuga and, later, the western half of Hispaniola (which becomes the colony of Saint-Domingue). French buccaneers (*flibustiers*) are central to the story.
- **The Netherlands.** A trading power and naval rival, often allied or at odds with England and France in shifting combinations. Dutch merchants and seamen move everywhere.

Alliances flipped with European wars. A captain might raid the French in one year and sail beside them the next. Use this. Loyalty in the Caribbean was thin and local.

## The key real figures you can use

- **Henry Morgan (roughly 1635 to 1688).** Welsh, the most successful buccaneer of the age. Bold planner, ruthless, politically shrewd. Portobelo, Maracaibo, and Panama were his. He ended as a knighted colonial official who persecuted pirates and drank himself to death. A complete arc of ambition, triumph, and corruption.
- **François l'Olonnais (died around 1668).** French, infamous for sadistic cruelty, including torturing and reportedly mutilating prisoners. Sacked Maracaibo. Died badly, said to have been killed by native people on the Darien coast. Use him as the dark mirror of the buccaneer ideal, a man the violence consumed.
- **Roche Brasiliano.** Dutch raised in Brazil, a brutal and erratic captain, a heavy drinker prone to violence. Good source material for an unstable crewmate or rival.
- **Bartholomew Sharp and the Pacific buccaneers (1680s).** Crossed into the South Sea, captured Spanish charts of immense value, raided the Pacific coast. Good for a quest or expedition plot.
- **William Dampier (1651 to 1715).** Buccaneer, explorer, and naturalist. He sailed with raiders but observed and wrote, circumnavigated the globe, and published bestselling travel accounts. A model for a thoughtful, observant character who is in the pirate world but not entirely of it.
- **Alexandre Exquemelin.** The surgeon-author whose firsthand book preserves most of what we know. Consider a writer or surgeon character as a window into the crew.
- **Henry Every (1690s).** The man who pulled off the great Red Sea robbery and escaped, becoming a legend and a folk hero. The archetype of the pirate who beats the empire and disappears.
- **Thomas Tew.** New England privateer turned Indian Ocean pirate, helped open the Pirate Round. Killed in action in 1695.

## How a raid actually worked

Your action plot can follow the real pattern:

1. **Commission and recruitment.** A captain obtains a letter of marque (or does not bother) and gathers a crew at Port Royal or Tortuga. Men sign the articles. The crew is a volunteer company, not a navy.
2. **Intelligence.** Word of a treasure fleet, a weakly held port, or a fat merchant ship spreads through taverns and informants.
3. **The approach.** Buccaneers often preferred a land assault on a port over a sea battle with a fortress. They would land out of sight, march overland, and strike from the undefended side. Surprise was everything.
4. **The assault.** Speed, ferocity, and reputation did much of the work. A town that knew what l'Olonnais did to defenders often surrendered rather than resist.
5. **The plunder and ransom.** Beyond goods and coin, captives were ransomed and towns were held hostage. Torture to reveal hidden wealth was common and is part of the era's real ugliness.
6. **The division.** Back at base, the take was divided strictly by the articles (see file 04). Then most of it was spent within weeks on drink, gambling, and women. Few buccaneers died rich.

## The economics (why men chose this life)

A poor man in the seventeenth century had almost no path upward. Wages were low, indenture was near-slavery, naval and merchant service meant brutal discipline and bad food for little pay. Buccaneering offered a vote, an equal share, the chance of a fortune in a single voyage, and an escape from a rigid hierarchy. The risk of death was high, but the alternative was a lifetime of poverty with no risk and no reward. Frame your characters' choices in this light and they stop looking like cartoon villains.

## The thing most stories get wrong

Buccaneers were not lone wolves on the margins of history. They were instruments of state policy, a cheap weapon in a global contest between empires. Their tragedy is that they were used and then discarded. The same governor who licensed Morgan later hanged men just like him. Build that betrayal into your world and the history starts paying you back in theme.

# 03. Ships, Seamanship, and Combat

Enough to write credible shipboard scenes, chases, and boarding actions without drowning the reader in rigging diagrams.

## The vessels of the era

Pirates rarely sailed huge warships. They wanted speed, shallow draft (to hide in coves and rivers), and a crew large enough to overwhelm a prize but small enough to feed. Match the ship to the job.

- **Sloop.** Small, fast, single-masted, shallow draft. The buccaneer favorite for raiding and quick escape. A sloop could outrun bigger warships and slip into waters they could not follow.
- **Brigantine.** Two masts, fast and handy, larger crew and more guns than a sloop. A common pirate choice for a step up in firepower.
- **Galleon.** The big Spanish treasure and cargo ship. Tall, slow, well armed, hard to take by force but immensely rich. The dream prize, usually taken by surprise or trickery rather than a fair fight.
- **Frigate.** A fast, well-armed warship, the kind of thing that hunts pirates. If a Royal Navy frigate is on your hero's trail, that is real danger.
- **Periagua / piragua.** A large dugout or open boat, sometimes with a sail, used for coastal raids, landings, and moving men quietly. Underrated and very period-correct for a stealth approach.
- **Captured prizes.** Pirates often used whatever they had just seized, refitting a captured merchantman by cutting more gun ports and clearing the deck for fighting.

A useful instinct: if your pirates are chasing wealth they want a fast small ship to catch and escape. If they are defending a base or fighting the navy they want guns. They rarely had both.

## The crew and who does what

A buccaneer crew was closer to a shareholders' company than a military unit. Key roles:

- **Captain.** Elected by the crew, absolute authority only in chase and battle. The rest of the time his power was limited and he could be voted out. He got a larger share but not a king's portion.
- **Quartermaster.** The crew's elected representative and the captain's counterweight. Divided the plunder, settled disputes, kept order, often led the boarding party, and decided punishments. In many crews the most trusted man aboard. A superb character role: the one who speaks for the men.
- **Sailing master / navigator.** Responsible for the actual sailing and navigation. Skilled navigators were valuable and sometimes forced to join against their will.
- **Boatswain (bosun).** Ran the deck work: rigging, sails, anchors, daily labor.
- **Gunner.** Master of the cannon, the powder, and the great-gun crews.
- **Carpenter and surgeon.** Kept the ship and the men patched. Surgeons were so valued that pirates often pressed them from captured ships. (See file 04 on medicine.)
- **Cooper, cook, powder monkeys, ordinary hands.** The rest of the company.

## The basics of sailing (enough to fake it well)

You do not need to teach the reader to sail. You need a few correct anchors so the prose feels authentic.

- A ship sails by setting cloth (sails) to catch wind on its masts and yards (the horizontal spars). Hands climb the rigging to set and take in sail.
- Ships cannot sail straight into the wind. To go upwind they zigzag, called tacking or beating, turning the bow through the wind. To turn the stern through the wind is to wear or gybe.
- **Windward** is the side the wind comes from. **Leeward** (say "loo-ard") is the sheltered side. Having the **weather gage** (being upwind of an enemy) is a tactical advantage because you control the distance and the approach.
- **Port** is left, **starboard** is right, facing the bow (front). The **stern** is the back.
- A ship's speed is measured in **knots**. **Soundings** measure water depth. Running aground on a reef or sandbar is a constant fear in Caribbean shallows.
- Weather is your free plot engine: the calm that traps a ship, the squall that dismasts it, the hurricane season (roughly June to November) that no sane captain ignores.

Use these terms sparingly and correctly. One right word per page builds trust. A paragraph of jargon loses the reader.

## Navigation in the 1600s

This is a rich source of tension because it was genuinely hard and dangerous.

- **Latitude** (north to south position) could be measured fairly well with a quadrant, astrolabe, or backstaff by reading the sun or stars.
- **Longitude** (east to west position) could not be measured reliably. The chronometer that solved it did not exist until the next century. Navigators estimated their east-west position by **dead reckoning**: tracking speed, heading, and time to guess how far they had run. Errors accumulated. Ships missed islands entirely or struck reefs they thought were miles off.
- Charts were incomplete, secret, and valuable. Capturing a set of Spanish charts (as the Pacific buccaneers did) was a real prize. A pilot who knew a coast was worth more than gold.
- This means getting lost, misjudging a landfall, or relying on a single irreplaceable navigator are all historically true sources of drama.

## Combat: the truth about pirate fighting

Pirates avoided pitched battles when they could. A sunk ship is no prize and dead men spend nothing. The goal was to make a target surrender, not to fight to the death.

**The chase.** Most actions began as a pursuit lasting hours. The faster ship closed slowly. The chased ship dumped cargo and rigged extra sail to escape. Tension lived in the gap closing foot by foot.

**Intimidation.** Pirates relied on reputation and fear. Raising the colors (a black or red flag) at the last moment, a deck crowded with armed men, a known captain's name: many merchant crews surrendered without a shot, calculating that resistance only got them killed. A red flag traditionally signaled no quarter, meaning no mercy would be given.

**The cannon.** Great guns were used to cripple, not usually to sink. A bar or chain shot fired high could bring down masts and rigging, leaving a ship dead in the water and ready to board. Round shot smashed hulls and men. Gun crews worked in choking smoke, deafened, on a deck that bucked with every broadside, with the constant danger of a gun breaking loose and crushing men.

**The boarding.** The decisive act. The attacker ran alongside, threw grappling hooks to bind the ships, and poured across. This was close, savage, fast fighting in a confined space:
  - **Cutlass.** Short, heavy, curved blade. Brutal in tight quarters where a long sword was useless.
  - **Pistols.** One or two shots, then thrown or clubbed. Some men wore several on a sash because reloading mid-fight was impossible.
  - **Boarding axe.** For cutting rigging and grappling lines, and for fighting.
  - **Grenadoes.** Early grenades, clay or iron balls packed with powder, thrown to clear a deck.
  - **Pikes and muskets** to repel boarders and shoot down from the rigging.

Write boarding as chaos: smoke, screaming, a pitching deck slick with blood and seawater, men slipping, gunfire at arm's length, the fight decided in minutes. It is not a duel. It is a riot with edged weapons.

## A few hard physical facts for realism

- Black powder weapons misfire, especially when wet. Sea spray ruins priming. This is a usable plot beat.
- Wooden ships are fragile and constantly rotting, leaking, and being repaired. The carpenter and the pumps are always at work.
- Fire is the sailor's worst terror. A ship is wood, tar, canvas, rope, and barrels of gunpowder. A fire near the magazine ends everything in a single blast.
- A wounded man at sea faced infection, no anesthesia beyond rum, and amputation as the standard answer to a shattered limb. (See file 04.)

# 04. Daily Life, the Code, and Plunder

The texture layer. Food, money, clothing, medicine, and the real "pirate code." These details, dropped naturally into scenes, are what make a reader believe the world.

## The articles: the real pirate code

The "code" was not a vague honor system. It was a written contract, the articles, that every man signed (or marked, if illiterate) before a voyage. Each crew wrote its own, but common terms across the era included:

- **Every man has a vote** in major decisions and an equal voice. This is genuinely radical for the seventeenth century, when ordinary men had no political voice anywhere.
- **Plunder is divided by a fixed scale** set in advance. The captain might get one and a half or two shares, the quartermaster and specialists somewhat more than one, the ordinary hands one each. The gap between top and bottom was small compared to a navy or merchant ship.
- **Compensation for injury.** A man who lost a limb received a set payment off the top before the division. A loss of a right arm paid more than a left, a leg a set sum, an eye or finger less. This was an early form of insurance and a striking detail to use.
- **No fighting aboard ship.** Quarrels were settled ashore, often by a formal duel overseen by the quartermaster, so disputes did not endanger the crew at sea.
- **Penalties for cowardice, desertion, theft from a shipmate, or concealing plunder.** These ranged from loss of share to marooning to death. Stealing from your own crew was among the gravest offenses.
- **Rules on women, lights, gambling, and keeping weapons clean and ready** appear in various sets.

The articles are a gift to a novelist. They are democracy and brutality in the same document. A scene where a new man signs the articles, or where the quartermaster invokes them against the captain, can carry a great deal of theme.

## Discipline and punishment

Forget walking the plank. There is essentially no good evidence that pirates made captives walk the plank. It is a later invention, mostly Victorian and Hollywood. The real punishments were worse and more interesting:

- **Marooning.** Leaving a man on a deserted island or sandbar with little more than a flask of water and a loaded pistol (the implication being he might prefer to use it on himself). A punishment for serious offenses against the crew.
- **Flogging.** Beating with a whip, common across all seafaring of the era.
- **Keelhauling.** Dragging a man under the ship's hull on a rope. The barnacles tore the flesh and many drowned. More associated with navies than pirates, but period-real.
- **Simple violence and execution** for betrayal or theft from shipmates.

Use marooning, not the plank, and you signal to informed readers that you did your homework.

## Money and plunder

- **Pieces of eight.** The Spanish silver dollar (the *real de a ocho*), the global currency of the age. Real coins, often physically cut into eight "bits" to make change (the origin of "two bits" for a quarter dollar). The pirate's pay.
- **Doubloons.** Spanish gold coins, higher value. The glamorous loot.
- The real take was usually less romantic than chests of gold: cargo (cloth, sugar, tobacco, hides, cacao, dyes, logwood, indigo), ship's stores, weapons, tools, medicine, and ransom for captives and towns. A hold full of cloth or cacao was a good prize.
- **Buried treasure is mostly a myth.** Pirates spent their money fast, on land, on drink and gambling and women. The image of buried chests comes largely from one real case (William Kidd, who did bury some goods) amplified by Treasure Island. Most buccaneers died poor. You can use buried treasure, but know it is the exception, not the rule, and a knowing reader will register the difference.

## Food and drink

Life at sea was a slow fight against hunger and rot.

- **Hardtack (ship's biscuit).** A dense dried cracker that lasted for months and bred weevils. Sailors ate it anyway, often by tapping the weevils out or eating in the dark.
- **Salt beef and salt pork.** Preserved in brine in barrels, tough and intensely salty, soaked before cooking. This is the *boucan* heritage: smoked and salted meat.
- **Dried peas, beans, rice, cheese that hardened to stone.**
- **Turtle.** A Caribbean staple. Sea turtles could be kept alive on their backs in the hold for fresh meat, a major food source for buccaneers.
- **Fresh provisions** came from raiding, fishing, and landing for water, fruit, and game. Citrus (limes, oranges) when available fought scurvy, though the cause was not understood.
- **Water** went foul quickly in casks, which is one reason for the heavy drinking.
- **Rum.** The drink of the era and region, cheap and plentiful from the sugar islands. Often watered into **grog**. Drunkenness was constant and is recorded again and again as a cause of disaster (men too drunk to fight or to keep watch).
- **Scurvy** (vitamin C deficiency) was the great killer on long voyages: bleeding gums, old wounds reopening, teeth falling out, death. A real and grim background threat.

## Clothing (forget the costume)

The fancy pirate costume is mostly theatrical invention. In reality:

- Most wore the practical clothes of a working sailor: a loose shirt, a short jacket, wide knee-length trousers or "slops," a neckerchief, a cap or a knitted Monmouth cap, often barefoot on deck for grip.
- Fine coats, lace, and feathered hats, when worn at all, were looted finery shown off by a successful captain ashore, not daily wear. A captain in a stolen velvet coat over a filthy shirt is more real and more telling than a uniform pirate look.
- Earrings and tattoos existed among sailors but the heavy modern image is exaggerated for the period.
- Tropical heat, sun, salt, and constant damp shaped everything. Clothes rotted, skin burned and weathered, everyone was filthy by modern standards.

## Medicine and the surgeon

A vivid, brutal, underused area:

- The ship's surgeon (often barely trained, sometimes pressed from a captured ship) handled wounds, fevers, and disease.
- **Amputation** was the standard treatment for a shattered limb to prevent gangrene. It was done fast, without anesthesia beyond rum or a leather strap to bite, with the patient held down. Survival depended on shock and infection. This is why injury compensation in the articles paid by limb.
- **Tropical disease** killed more men than combat: yellow fever, malaria, dysentery ("the bloody flux"), and fevers no one could name. A landing party could lose more men to mosquitoes than to muskets.
- Treatments were a mix of period medicine and folk remedy: bleeding, purging, tar and gunpowder on wounds, citrus and onions, whatever a port could supply.
- A skilled surgeon was so valued that crews fought to keep one. A surgeon character gives you a humane, observant point of view inside a violent world (this is what Exquemelin actually was).

## The ports: where land scenes happen

- **Port Royal, Jamaica.** The great English base. Rich, crowded, lawless, packed with taverns, brothels, merchants, and privateers spending their plunder. Called the wickedest city on Earth. Hot, loud, dangerous, and the natural home for your land scenes until the 1692 earthquake destroys it.
- **Tortuga.** The French and mixed buccaneer haven off Hispaniola. The original Brethren of the Coast stronghold.
- **The Spanish Main.** The mainland coast of Spanish South and Central America, the target of the raids: Cartagena, Portobelo, Maracaibo, Panama. Fortified, wealthy, and the source of the silver.
- **Smaller cays and hidden coves** all through the islands for careening (cleaning the hull), hiding, and dividing plunder.

## The "Brethren of the Coast"

A loose name for the buccaneer fraternity, a shared culture of articles, equal shares, and mutual aid among the raiders of Tortuga and Port Royal more than a formal organization. Useful as a name and an idea: a brotherhood of the lawless with its own rough rules, not a government.

# 05. Language and Voice

How to make dialogue sound seventeenth century without turning it into a theme-park ride. This is where most pirate fiction either wins or embarrasses itself.

## The big truth: "pirate speak" is an actor's invention

The "Arrr, me hearties, shiver me timbers" voice does not come from real pirates. It comes largely from one actor, Robert Newton, who played Long John Silver in the 1950 Disney film of Treasure Island. He used an exaggerated version of his own West Country English accent (the southwest of England, around Bristol, a real seafaring region), and it stuck so hard that it became the global idea of how pirates talk. There is some logic underneath, because many English sailors did come from the West Country, but the cartoon version is a twentieth century performance, not historical speech.

What this means for you: if you write "Arrr" unironically, informed readers stop trusting you. If you ignore period flavor entirely, the dialogue feels modern and flat. The craft is in the middle.

## The principle: suggest the period, do not transcribe it

Real seventeenth century English, written out faithfully, is hard for a modern reader and slows the book to a crawl. The professional approach is to evoke an older register with a light, consistent touch:

- **Cut modern words and rhythms.** No "okay," "guys," "yeah," "deal with it," "no problem," "I guess." These snap a reader out of the period instantly. This is the single highest-value edit you can make.
- **Lean on older but still readable diction.** "Aye" and "nay" for yes and no. "Lad" and "man" in address. "Reckon," "fetch," "yonder," "naught," "ought," "savvy" (to understand). "I'll not" rather than "I won't." "What say you?" rather than "what do you think?"
- **Use slightly formal, slightly inverted constructions sparingly.** "There's no man aboard I'd trust less." "I'll have the truth of it." A little goes far.
- **Vary register by class and role.** An educated captain, a surgeon, or a gentleman speaks more formally and correctly. A common hand speaks rougher, plainer, with more cant and oaths. The contrast does characterization for free.

## Sailor cant and useful period vocabulary

Real working sailors had a dense professional slang. Salt it in.

- **Aye** (yes), **nay** (no), **belay** (stop, cancel: "belay that"), **avast** (hold, stop).
- **Hands** (the crew), **mate** (companion or officer), **lad / lads**, **the men**.
- **Prize** (a captured ship or its cargo), **plunder**, **booty**, **purchase** (period word for plunder).
- **Quarter** (mercy: "no quarter," "ask for quarter," "show no quarter").
- **Colors** (a flag: "show your colors").
- **Spanish Main** (the mainland Spanish territories and their coast).
- **Careen** (to beach a ship and clean its hull).
- **Press / pressed** (forced into service against one's will).
- **Marque** (a letter of marque, the privateering license).
- **Doubloon, pieces of eight, bit** (money: see file 04).
- **Scurvy** as both the disease and an insult ("you scurvy dog" is theatrical, but "scurvy" as an adjective for contemptible is period-flavored).
- **Powder, shot, ball, broadside, grape, chain shot** (gunnery).
- **Boucan / buccaneer, flibustier** (French for the same), **Brethren of the Coast**.

## Oaths and cursing

Profanity of the era leaned religious, which sounds quaint now but carried real force then.

- Blasphemous oaths: "God's blood," "God's wounds" (shortened to "zounds"), "by God's teeth," "the devil take you," "damn your eyes," "to the devil with it," "hell and damnation."
- "Sink me," "blast me," "curse it," "be damned."
- Calling on the devil and damnation read as harder and more period than modern obscenity, which can sound anachronistic in a 1600s mouth. You can imply coarser language without writing modern swear words: "He cursed in a way that would have shamed a dockside whore" does the work.

## A practical method for writing a line of dialogue

1. Write the line in plain modern English first, so it is clear and does its job.
2. Strip every modern word and contraction that breaks the spell.
3. Add one period marker (an "aye," an inversion, a piece of cant, an oath), not five.
4. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a costume, pull a marker out. If it sounds like a 2020s text message, add one back.

Example progression:

- Modern: "I don't think we can trust him. Let's get out of here."
- Overcooked: "Arrr, I be reckonin' we cannot be trustin' the scurvy dog, so let's be weighin' anchor, me hearty!"
- Right: "I'll not trust him, and that's flat. We sail with the tide."

The right version is clean, readable, clearly of another time, and not a parody.

## Dialect and accent on the page

- **Imply accent through word choice and rhythm, not heavy phonetic spelling.** Writing every dropped letter ("'ee were comin' fer th' cap'n") exhausts the reader within a page. A few light markers establish a voice, then trust the reader to keep hearing it.
- Different characters from different places (a Welshman like Morgan, a French flibustier, a Dutch sailor, an English gentleman, an enslaved or formerly enslaved man, an Irish hand) can have distinct rhythms and a few signature words without phonetic transcription.
- Consistency matters more than density. A character who says "aye" should not later say "yeah." Keep a short voice note for each main character.

## Narration versus dialogue

Keep the narrator's voice clean, clear, and only lightly period. Concentrate the rough, salty, archaic flavor in dialogue. A modern reader will follow a clear narrator through almost anything, while a narrator written in dense seventeenth century prose will lose them. The contrast (controlled narration, vivid rough dialogue) is exactly how the best historical fiction reads.

## Quick "ban list" for a 1600s manuscript

Words and habits that break the period instantly. Search and destroy in revision:

okay, OK, guys, yeah, yep, nope, hi, hey, cool, awesome, fine (as in "I'm fine"), deal with it, no problem, I guess, kind of, sort of, focus, contact (as a verb), teenager, gun (use "pistol," "musket," "piece"; "gun" meant cannon), boyfriend, girlfriend, definitely, basically, actually, obviously.

# 06. Reaching a Modern Audience, and Themes

The 1600s Caribbean was a place of slavery, colonial conquest, and routine brutality. A book set there has to decide how to handle that honestly without either sanitizing it or wallowing in it. This file is about those decisions and about the themes that make the era resonate now.

## The core adaptation problem

The romance of piracy (freedom, adventure, rebellion against authority) sits directly on top of the horror of the era (slavery, plunder of native and colonial peoples, indiscriminate violence). You cannot honestly write the romance while pretending the horror is not there. The modern reader knows it is there. The skill is to let both be true: these men found a kind of freedom, and that freedom was funded by violence against others. Holding both makes the book adult instead of a theme park.

The best modern handling of this is Black Sails (file 01). It keeps the adventure thrilling while taking slavery and colonialism seriously as the world the adventure floats on. That is the target.

## Slavery: the unavoidable context

- The Caribbean economy of the 1600s ran on enslaved African labor on sugar plantations. The wealth the pirates stole was, at its root, produced by that system. Port Royal's riches and the Spanish silver alike sat inside an economy of forced labor and conquest.
- Pirate crews had a complicated relationship to it. Some crews included free black men, escaped slaves, and men of mixed background who found more equality among pirates than anywhere else in that world. Some pirates also captured and sold enslaved people as cargo. Both are true and the mix varied by crew. Do not flatten it in either direction.
- Indentured servitude bound poor Europeans too, in conditions close to slavery for a term of years. Captain Blood begins exactly here: a free Englishman sold into bondage in Barbados. This is a real and usable starting point for a protagonist.

**Practical guidance:** decide early how central this is to your book. You do not have to make slavery your subject, but you cannot pretend the world is empty of it. Even acknowledging it in the texture (an enslaved man glimpsed on a wharf, a plantation as the source of a character's wealth, a crewmate who escaped bondage) signals to the reader that you see the real world and are not selling a sanitized fantasy.

## Indigenous and colonized peoples

- The native peoples of the Caribbean had already been devastated by Spanish conquest and disease by the 1600s. Other groups (the Kuna of the Darien, the Miskito of the Central American coast) interacted with buccaneers as allies, guides, trading partners, and sometimes enemies. The Miskito in particular were prized as expert turtlers and fishermen who sailed with buccaneer crews.
- Avoid the two lazy traps: the noble savage (a flat, idealized helper) and the faceless savage (a flat, menacing obstacle). Give native characters their own interests, intelligence, and agency, the same as anyone else.

## Women in this world

- The historical reality was harsh and constrained. Most women in the Caribbean of the 1600s were enslaved women, indentured women, planters' wives and daughters, tavern keepers, and prostitutes in the ports. Women aboard pirate ships were rare and often forbidden by the articles.
- That said, fiction has room. Real women like Anne Bonny and Mary Read (just after your period, the 1710s and 1720s) prove women did go to sea armed and fighting. A woman passing as a man, a captain's partner who runs the shore-side business, a tavern owner who controls information and credit in Port Royal, a planter's daughter who refuses her assigned life: all are plausible and dramatically rich.
- The strong choice for a modern audience is to give your women real agency and interiority within the genuine constraints of the era, rather than either erasing them or dropping an anachronistic modern action heroine into 1670 with no friction. The friction between what she wants and what the world allows is the drama.

## Violence: how much, and how

- The era was genuinely brutal. Torture for plunder, massacre, mutilation (l'Olonnais), and casual killing are part of the record.
- For a modern reader, unflinching realism works better than either cartoon violence or constant gore. Make violence have weight and consequence. A killing should cost something, change someone, or reveal character. Endless atrocity numbs the reader and cheapens the real horror.
- A useful rule: show enough to be honest about what these men were, but stay in service of character and story rather than spectacle.

## The themes that make this era land now

This is why a 1600s pirate story can feel urgent rather than antique. Pick the ones that move you and build toward them.

1. **Freedom versus its price.** The pirate ship was, for its moment, one of the most democratic and equal societies in the Western world: a vote for every man, shared plunder, elected leaders, compensation for the injured. And it was funded by robbery and murder. The tension between genuine liberty and the violence that paid for it is the deepest theme the genre offers.

2. **The individual against the empire.** Buccaneers were used by states and discarded by them. The man who beats the system and the man crushed by it are the same man at different moments (Morgan was both). This speaks directly to a modern reader's distrust of institutions.

3. **Reinvention and the self-made outlaw.** The Caribbean was where a nobody could become a captain, where the rigid class order of Europe broke down. The pull of a place where the past does not bind you is timeless.

4. **Loyalty and betrayal among the lawless.** With no law to enforce contracts, everything ran on reputation, the articles, and trust. What holds men together when nothing forces them to stay? Betrayal is the genre's natural engine.

5. **The cost of the romance.** A self-aware book can let the reader feel the thrill of the pirate life and then make them sit with what it was built on. That double movement is what separates a serious novel from a costume.

6. **Order versus chaos, and what authority is for.** The pirates rejected the brutal hierarchy of navy and empire, then had to invent their own rules to survive. The question of how free people govern themselves is right there in the articles.

## Practical balancing act

- **Anchor the fantasy in real consequence.** Adventure plus weight, not adventure as pure escape.
- **Let characters hold modern-relatable interiority while acting within period constraints.** A character can long for things the era denies without becoming a person teleported from today.
- **Trust the reader.** You do not need to lecture. Show the world honestly and the reader will draw the meaning. A single true detail (the source of a coin, the brand on a man's shoulder, the name of a plantation) carries more than a paragraph of moralizing.
- **Decide your contract with the reader early.** Pure rollicking adventure, serious historical novel, or something in between are all valid. Trouble comes from being unclear about which you are writing, so the violence and the romance fight each other instead of working together.

## Glossary

- **Articles:** the written contract a crew signed, setting shares, rules, and punishments.
- **Boucan:** a wooden frame for smoking meat; root of "buccaneer."
- **Brethren of the Coast:** the loose fraternity of Caribbean buccaneers.
- **Careen:** to beach a ship and clean its hull.
- **Cutlass:** short, heavy, curved sword for close fighting.
- **Doubloon:** Spanish gold coin.
- **Flibustier:** French term for a buccaneer.
- **Grog:** rum cut with water.
- **Hardtack:** dried biscuit, the staple ship's bread.
- **Letter of marque:** a government license to attack enemy shipping legally.
- **Leeward:** the sheltered side, away from the wind (say "loo-ard").
- **Maroon:** to abandon a man on a deserted shore as punishment.
- **Pieces of eight:** Spanish silver dollars, the common currency, cut into eight "bits."
- **Press / pressed:** forced into service against one's will.
- **Prize:** a captured ship or cargo.
- **Purchase:** period word for plunder.
- **Quarter:** mercy ("no quarter" means none given).
- **Quartermaster:** the crew's elected representative, divided loot and kept order.
- **Sloop:** small fast single-masted ship, the buccaneer favorite.
- **Spanish Main:** the mainland Spanish territories and coast in the Americas.
- **Weather gage:** the advantage of being upwind of an enemy.
- **Windward:** the side the wind comes from.

## Name banks (period-plausible)

**English / Welsh / Scots hands:** Henry, Edward, Thomas, William, John, Roger, Nathaniel, Josiah, Bartholomew, Samuel, Owen, Gwyn, Rhys, Tobias, Silas, Ezekiel, Amos.

**French flibustiers:** Jean, Pierre, Jacques, Michel, Guillaume, Henri, Olivier, Mathurin, Gaspard.

**Dutch:** Roche, Cornelis, Dirk, Pieter, Joost, Hendrik, Willem, Bartel.

**Surnames / by-names:** buccaneers often went by nicknames and aliases. Consider a place name (Bristol, Galway), a trait (Red Ned, Long Tom, One-Eyed Will), or a grim joke. A man hiding from the law would not use his real name freely.

**Women:** Anne, Mary, Margaret, Elizabeth, Grace, Bess, Joan, Catherine, Bridget, Marguerite, Sarah.

**Ships (period flavor):** the Adventure, the Revenge, the Fortune, the Bachelor's Delight (a real buccaneer ship), the Trinity, the Mary, the Swan, the Cygnet (real), the Speaker, the Charity, the Gift of God. Pirates liked names suggesting luck, vengeance, or dark irony.

**Real places to drop in:** Port Royal, Tortuga, Hispaniola, Cartagena, Portobelo, Maracaibo, Panama, Barbados, Petit-Goave, Île-à-Vache (Cow Island), the Darien, the Bay of Campeche, New Providence (later the Golden Age hub).

## Myth versus reality cheat sheet

| The myth | The reality |
|----------|-------------|
| Walking the plank | No real evidence pirates did this. They marooned men instead. |
| "Arrr, me hearties" | A 1950 film actor's invented accent, not historical speech. |
| Buried treasure | Mostly myth. Pirates spent fast and died poor. The Kidd case plus Treasure Island built the legend. |
| Chests of gold doubloons | Usual loot was cargo: cloth, sugar, tobacco, hides, cacao, plus ransom. |
| Eyepatches everywhere | Exaggerated. Possible practical uses exist but the universal image is theatrical. |
| The pirate uniform | Working sailors wore plain practical clothes. Finery was looted and shown off ashore. |
| Captain as absolute tyrant | The crew elected him and could vote him out. Real power lived with the quartermaster outside battle. |
| Lawless chaos | Crews ran on written articles, votes, and fixed shares, surprisingly democratic for the age. |
| Pegleg pirates by the dozen | Amputation was real and common, but the cheerful pegleg image is later cartoon. |
| Parrots on every shoulder | Sailors did bring back exotic birds to sell, so it is not impossible, just inflated. |

## Sensory grab bag (drop these into scenes)

Tar and hemp rope. Salt crust on everything. Sun-cracked skin. The reek of the bilge. Gunpowder smoke that stings the eyes. Rum breath. Rotting fruit and fresh-caught turtle. Wet wool and damp canvas. The constant creak and groan of timber. Bare feet gripping a wet deck. The shock of tropical heat after a European winter. The whine of mosquitoes at a river mouth. Church bells in Cartagena across the water. The clink of cut silver bits. Citrus rind against the rot of scurvy. The boom of surf on a reef in the dark, the sound that means death if the navigator was wrong.
