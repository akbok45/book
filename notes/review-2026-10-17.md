Your current outline actually answers essentially all of the editor’s questions. The issue is mostly where the information appears: the editor encountered names, motivations, and world rules before getting the context needed to understand them.

I’d send something like this back. I’d keep it conversational rather than trying to defend the outline point by point:

These are really helpful questions. A lot of this is answered later in my notes, but I can see from your comments that I need to establish some of it much earlier and more clearly.

Who is Balam?

Balam was Crumble’s teacher and essentially a father figure to him. He was a Maya resistance fighter and keeper of old knowledge who died about sixteen months before the book begins. Crumble trained under him for roughly three years, learning fighting, stealth, Maya language and history, and the day count that eventually becomes important to the codex.

The Luke/Yoda comparison is actually pretty close, except Crumble’s relationship with Balam is more personal and less purely instructional. Crumble idolizes him and has built a lot of his identity around becoming the kind of man he thought Balam was. Balam died while pursuing the codex, so Crumble sees finishing that work as a debt he owes him. Part of Crumble’s arc is realizing that loving and respecting Balam does not mean he has to become him.

Who is Cárdenas?

Don Mateo de Cárdenas is the main antagonist. He is a Spanish colonial officer in his fifties who has spent most of his life in the conquest of Peru and the Maya country and has recently turned his attention toward suppressing the buccaneers. The Spanish fort Jigsaw, Crumble and Bonesy unsuccessfully rob at the beginning belongs to him.

He has also spent decades collecting and studying the sacred objects, histories and strange artifacts of the peoples Spain conquered. He isn’t opposed to magic itself. In fact, he uses it. What he cannot tolerate is anything powerful or strange that exists outside an order he controls. That is ultimately why he wants the codex.

How does magic work, and what is Tortuga?

Tortuga is not a magical realm, and nothing happens to the world when they sail into it. It is the real Caribbean of roughly 1670, with one major difference: some of the supernatural beliefs, curses and objects of the Americas are real.

I’m aiming much closer to magical realism than Harry Potter. There are no wizards, magical schools or people casting spells. The strange tends to exist in objects, places and cursed beings. A walking skeleton can sit at a tavern table. A mermaid might be kept behind a curtain for people to pay to see. Men might gamble with a cursed skull without knowing exactly where it came from. Most people treat these things the way sailors might treat unusual weather, foreign merchandise or superstition: sometimes with fear, sometimes as something useful, and often without thinking very deeply about it.

The strange is rooted primarily in the Americas and is strongest in native places and traditions. Pirates have acquired pieces of it through trade, friendship, theft and conquest. Tortuga is simply where a lot of it has accumulated because Tortuga is where pirates from across the Caribbean accumulate.

Cárdenas’s collection is the darker version of the same process. He has spent forty years taking objects and knowledge from the peoples Spain conquered, cataloguing them and learning how they work. His obsession is classification and control.

What does Cárdenas want?

He wants the codex because he believes it can restore order to the Caribbean by ending all the uncontrolled curses and strange things that exist outside Spanish authority.

The eventual reveal is that he has misunderstood what “restoration” really means. The codex does not simply destroy magic. It puts things back the way they were before they changed. That sounds almost benevolent at first, but for someone like Bonesy, who has been dead for centuries, being restored means becoming the remains of a man who died centuries ago. For other cursed people, their conditions have become so intertwined with their lives that “restoring” them can effectively unmake them.

What kind of school did Jigsaw attend?

It was not a fighting school. It was a good Spanish colonial school for the sons of families with property, the kind of education that could lead into the navy. Jigsaw was extremely good academically and also learned formal fencing there, along with fighting in illegal back-room duels.

The problem was that he was always slightly outside the world he was being trained to enter. His father hid his Barbary background, and Jigsaw eventually discovers that ability matters less than family, blood and money. One of his classmates, Rodrigo, had worse marks but the right family and eventually receives the commission and status Jigsaw thought he had earned.

How does Jigsaw take his anger out on the “soft-handed” Spanish?

Once he becomes a buccaneer, his violence falls disproportionately on Spanish officers, wealthy men and men of status—the type of people he associates with the system that rejected him. He raids Spanish ships and forts and tells himself, at least implicitly, that these men deserve what happens to them.

The book does not agree with him. One of the important moments later is a flashback to a Spanish merchant ship surrendering. The danger is over, but Jigsaw keeps killing. Crumble sees the moment his violence stops being necessary and becomes rage. Jigsaw has legitimate reasons to hate the system he came from, but he has turned individual Spaniards who had nothing to do with what happened to him into substitutes for the people who did.

Who is Ixmal?

Ixmal is a woman, not a place. She and Crumble were together when they were young. Crumble left her at sixteen because he wanted to go to sea and prove he was destined for something larger. When he returned three years later, she was happily married to an ordinary man and had a child.

That matters because Crumble’s wound is almost the reverse of Jigsaw’s. Jigsaw believes the world took the life he deserved away from him. Crumble voluntarily walked away from a life and a person who loved him because he thought he was meant for something greater, then discovered that she did perfectly well without him.

Are Jigsaw and Crumble brothers?

Not biologically. I use “brothers” in the outline as shorthand for their relationship and in the older buccaneer sense of the “brethren.”

They met on Tortuga when Jigsaw was seventeen and Crumble was sixteen. They sailed together for about three years, spent roughly three years apart, and eventually came back together. By the beginning of the book, they are effectively family.

That is also why their pasts are intentionally different. Jigsaw is haunted by being rejected and left behind. Crumble is haunted by the things and people he chose to leave. Jigsaw responds to loss with anger and radical independence. Crumble responds to his own choices by becoming almost pathologically loyal—to Balam, to his friends, and to obligations he believes he owes.

What is the actual external plot? Why are they doing any of this?

This is probably the biggest thing I need to make clearer upfront.

Jigsaw, Crumble and Bonesy are buccaneers. They take jobs for money, raid, steal and generally answer to nobody.

Immediately before the book begins, they were hired to steal a saint’s finger bone from a Spanish fort near Veracruz. Jigsaw sees a major storm coming and calls off the search, so they leave believing the job failed. Two days later, while they are sheltering from that storm in the ruined Maya temple where chapter one begins, Bonesy casually reveals that he stole the finger anyway.

While they are trapped there, Bonesy also tells them about an old Maya book he once heard priests discussing. The priests deliberately broke the book into pieces and hid them in sequence, with each piece pointing toward the next. According to what Bonesy heard, the completed book could “put things back how they were, before.” The priests also supposedly buried gold alongside its pieces.

Crumble recognizes what Bonesy is describing as the codex Balam had been searching for when he died.

They sell the finger in chapter two and use the money to outfit themselves for the hunt.

So at first the external motivation is pretty straightforward: there is gold along the route, the clues point toward an extraordinary lost object, and they now have enough money to go after it. Each man also has a private reason for wanting to continue. Crumble wants to finish Balam’s work. Jigsaw wants the gold but is also drawn toward a place from his own past that the first clue points toward. Bonesy mostly wants to go wherever his friends go.

Then they discover Cárdenas is hunting the same thing.

That gradually changes the nature of the quest. At first it is a treasure hunt and a race. Once they understand what Cárdenas intends to do with the codex—and eventually what the codex would actually do to Bonesy and other cursed people—the goal changes from find it before Cárdenas to make sure nobody can use it.

So the basic external spine I’m working with is:

Failed robbery → Bonesy reveals the stolen finger and the codex → they sell the finger and finance the hunt → treasure hunt → race against Cárdenas → discover what the codex really does → stop Cárdenas → choose to break the codex again rather than let anyone decide what the world should be restored to.

The inner story sits underneath that. Jigsaw and Crumble begin the book believing, in different ways, that freedom means owing nothing to anyone. The treasure hunt gradually forces them to choose people over that kind of independence. Ideally, the external plot works as an adventure even if a reader never consciously articulates that theme.

I think the most important feedback hidden in the editor’s questions is the last one. They shouldn’t need your notes to understand the outer engine of the novel. The premise is actually pretty clean once stripped down:

Three buccaneers learn of a broken Maya codex whose pieces lead through buried gold to something that can “put things back how they were.” They finance the hunt with the proceeds of a robbery, only to discover that the Spanish officer they robbed is hunting the same book—and intends to use it to impose his version of order on the Caribbean.

That’s a strong adventure engine. Your existing document obscures it somewhat because it introduces the thematic reasons for the quest before making the treasure hunt → race → prevention mission progression completely obvious.

The other major signal is that Balam, Cárdenas, Ixmal, Tortuga, and the codex are all clear in your head but arrive as proper nouns faster than a first reader can categorize them. You don’t necessarily need more exposition in the novel. You mostly need each important name’s first appearance to give the reader one immediate hook: Balam, Crumble’s dead Maya teacher; Cárdenas, the Spanish officer whose fort they just robbed; Ixmal, the woman Crumble left behind. After that, you can complicate them.
