Yeah. I went through the transcript and the current “Book One: The Story.” The bones are really strong now, but I would not lock Claude’s version yet. It successfully captured almost all your individual notes, but there are a few places where it made them too clean, too explicit, or solved a problem without asking whether that solution creates a better payoff later.
The biggest thing I’d change is this:
The final battle should not primarily test whether they became better fighters. It should test whether they became different men.
Everything from Chapters 1–8 is setting that up.
1. The core story is clearer than Claude makes it sound
I think the cleanest statement of Book One is:
Jigsaw and Crumble begin the book believing freedom means owing nothing to anyone. By the end, they learn that choosing people is not the opposite of freedom. It is what makes freedom worth having.
But they learn different versions of that.
Jigsaw begins with rage at a society that decided his worth for him. His response has been basically: fuck everyone, I need nobody. His father’s life looks like surrender to him. His arc isn’t learning to obey society. It’s realizing his father chose family, and choice is what mattered.
Crumble has almost the inverse problem. He thinks he’s independent, but he’s actually living according to a dead man’s rules. Balam is practically his religion. Crumble’s arc is realizing that loving Balam does not mean becoming Balam.
Bonesy is the counterpoint to both of them. He already understands something they don’t: he doesn’t care what he’s supposed to be. He’s dead, cursed, vulgar, curious, ridiculous, and completely himself.
That’s why Bonesy being the one who finally says some version of:
“That was their choice. What’s yours?”
is so good.
Claude has this material, but I would make this the organizing principle of the entire back half, not simply one theme among several.
2. Cárdenas needs one important adjustment
I really like:
Driven by control. Anxious since a rough childhood.
And I like the sister/curse idea.
But I would not make him simply anti-magic.
That’s too easy.
He has spent forty years collecting magic. He surrounds himself with it. He studies it. Uses it. Labels it. Hoards it.
That’s fascinating because he’s a hypocrite—but not a stupid hypocrite.
His belief should be more like:
Magic isn’t evil. Uncontrolled magic is evil.
Same with people.
Same with cultures.
Same with pirates.
Same with Jigsaw.
Same with Crumble.
Cárdenas doesn’t really want a world without strange things because he hates strange things. He wants a world where nothing exists outside a system he controls.
That makes the cabin vastly more important.
His collection isn’t just trophies.
It is his worldview rendered physically.
Every supernatural thing has:

* a shelf
* a label
* an origin
* a known effect
* a known cost
* a place

Then Bonesy walks in.
Bonesy is something Cárdenas can’t psychologically tolerate: a creature that is ancient, absurd, uncontrollable, happy, and completely uninterested in being understood.
That gives Cárdenas and Bonesy a surprisingly great opposition.
And I’d change the sister slightly
Don’t make it:
sister gets cursed → Cárdenas concludes magic must disappear.
Make it more tragic.
His father loots something he doesn’t understand. His sister becomes cursed. Every authority gives Mateo a different explanation—priest, healer, Maya keeper, Spanish doctor. Nobody can tell him what is true. She dies while adults argue.
His trauma isn’t magic. It’s uncertainty.
Then Balam is perhaps the first person who can actually explain what happened.
Young Mateo becomes obsessed with knowledge because:
If I understand everything, nothing can hurt me unexpectedly again.
Knowledge becomes collection.
Collection becomes classification.
Classification becomes control.
Control becomes empire.
That’s a much stronger villain trajectory.
3. Balam should remain morally ambiguous
Claude is close here, but I’d resist making Balam too easy to forgive.
The parallel between Jigsaw’s father and Balam is excellent:
both sons thought compromise meant weakness; both fathers compromised because they loved somebody.
But the outcomes shouldn’t be identical.
Jigsaw’s father made a deal and got his son.
Balam made a deal and eventually helped create Cárdenas.
That’s much messier.
So when Crumble asks:
“Do you regret it?”
Balam shouldn’t give him an inspirational answer.
Something closer in meaning to:
I regret what he became.
I don’t regret saving her.
That leaves Crumble with an adult problem:
A good decision can have terrible consequences and still have been the right decision at the time.
That’s much more interesting than “Balam wasn’t perfect.”
4. Balam’s daughter needs a bigger secret/payoff
This is probably the biggest thing Claude hasn’t fully exploited.
You established:
Balam split his teaching between Crumble and his daughter.
Right now that mainly explains why she can read something Crumble can’t.
That’s not enough.
It should matter in the climax.
Balam gave Crumble the things he thought Crumble needed:

* fighting
* survival
* resistance
* discipline
* day count
* perhaps navigation/tradition

But he gave his daughter something completely different.
Maybe how the codex actually works.
Or even better:
Balam deliberately taught neither of them enough.
He knew Cárdenas.
He knew Cárdenas could break a man.
So he made sure no single person could give Cárdenas everything.
Crumble spent years interpreting this as:
Balam didn’t trust me enough.
But the truth becomes:
Balam trusted Crumble enough to make him one half.
That’s a beautiful resolution to Crumble’s insecurity.
And it makes the daughter indispensable without making her “the smart girl who translates glyphs.”
5. I would reconsider the weighted-cord fighting style
It’s visually interesting, but right now it feels slightly invented because the plot needs a cool new combat mechanic.
I’d retain it only if it embodies the theme.
For example, the old fighting method could be something that is weak alone and devastating with two people.
One fighter catches/binds.
The other strikes.
Or one creates tension in the line while another changes its direction.
Crumble originally learned only one side of it from Balam.
His daughter learned the other.
Suddenly the split teaching matters physically too.
And then Jigsaw figures out how to fight alongside them rather than trying to dominate the engagement himself.
That’s much stronger than:

Their new combat system literally requires trust and cooperation.

9. The cursed village should be less “X-Men village”
Claude’s three examples are fun, but I would be careful.
seed touch
fireproof woman
refrigeration shadow
Three clean superpowers in a row risks making the strange feel like a magic system.
Your world seems cooler if curses are weird, inconvenient, culturally integrated, and sometimes useful by accident.
For example:
A woman can’t feel heat. That’s dangerous—she burns herself without knowing—but it makes her extraordinarily useful around kilns.
A man’s shadow draws flies away from him. Miserable most of the time. Fishermen have figured out hanging the day’s catch beside him keeps flies off it.
A child causes seeds to sprout prematurely wherever she sleeps. So her family rotates her sleeping place through nursery plots.
Benefits emerge from adaptation.
Nobody was cursed to conveniently help agriculture.
That makes the village feel lived-in rather than designed.
10. The daughter needs a weakness beyond physical strength
“Five men overpower her” is realistic and should stay.
But that’s not really a character flaw.
Her real weakness is already hiding in your notes:
She has lived entirely for other people.
That’s her flaw.
She can command everybody except herself.
Ask her:
What do you want?

She knows:

* what her village needs
* what her father wanted
* what Crumble needs
* what the cursed need

But not herself.
That creates a great contrast with Jigsaw, who has spent his entire life asking what he wants.
They’re opposites.
And it explains why Jigsaw could be attracted to her while also ultimately understanding that Crumble is right for her.
Her arc should culminate in more than “I want to see the world.”
That’s a little generic.
I’d make it concrete.
Maybe she’s fascinated by maps because she’s never been farther than the river.
Or she wants to see snow because a sailor once described it and she didn’t believe him.
Or she wants to sail far enough north that the stars change.
Something strangely personal and almost childish.
That’s important because everyone else’s desires for her have been important.
Her first desire for herself should almost feel embarrassingly small.
That makes it human.

Prompt I’d give Claude
Go back through our entire conversation and revise Book One: The Story before we build the final battle. Do not radically replace the existing road. Keep the plot of Chapters 1–8 largely intact, but strengthen the underlying logic and setup/payoff.
The central character idea should be clearer: Jigsaw and Crumble have both confused independence with freedom in different ways. Jigsaw believes freedom means needing nobody and answering his injuries with rage. Crumble believes he forged his own path, but he is actually living according to Balam’s rules. Their fathers’ compromises should not teach them to submit or accept society. They should teach them that choosing people can itself be an act of freedom. Bonesy’s eventual “That was their choice. What’s yours?” is the cleanest expression of this, but the book itself should mostly show the idea rather than state it.
Strengthen Cárdenas. His deepest fear should be uncertainty, not simply magic. His sister was killed by a curse connected to something his father looted, but what traumatized young Mateo was that nobody could explain or control what was happening. Balam was perhaps the first person who could explain any of it. Cárdenas therefore learned that knowledge prevents helplessness. Knowledge became collection, collection became classification, classification became control, and control became his worldview. This explains why his cabin is meticulously labeled and why he uses magic while wanting eventually to eliminate uncontrolled magic. His belief is essentially that nothing dangerous should exist outside an ordered system he controls.
Keep Balam morally complicated. Jigsaw’s father and Balam both compromised with enemies to protect people they loved, but the outcomes were different. Jigsaw’s father’s compromise worked. Balam’s kindness and compromises helped create Cárdenas. Balam can regret what Cárdenas became without regretting saving his daughter or his people. Do not turn his lesson into a clean inspirational answer.
Make Balam’s split teaching much more important. Crumble and Balam’s daughter should each possess something the other genuinely lacks. Balam may have deliberately divided his knowledge because he knew Cárdenas could capture or break one person. This should eventually matter directly in Florida. Crumble initially interprets the missing knowledge as Balam not trusting him enough, but the deeper truth may be that Balam trusted him to be one necessary half rather than the sole heir.
Reconsider the weighted-cord fighting style so it expresses the same idea. It should not simply be a new, stronger weapon they learn during imprisonment. Ideally the old Maya method works best between two people: one binds, redirects or creates tension while the other strikes or moves. Crumble may know one part from Balam and his daughter another. They perfect it together. Jigsaw eventually learns how to fight with them instead of simply beside them. Their improvement should come from cooperation rather than a prison training montage.
Strengthen Balam’s daughter’s personal flaw. Five soldiers physically overpowering her is realistic but is not her character weakness. Her real weakness is that she has spent her entire life knowing what everyone else needs and almost never asking what she wants. She cared for Balam, then the village, then the cursed and different people around her. Her arc is learning to possess a desire that belongs only to her. Give that desire something specific and personal rather than merely “see the world.” It can be small, strange or almost childish. Her insistence on joining the crew remains motivated by protecting her people, but by the beach she should begin choosing the adventure for herself.
Keep the Jigsaw/Crumble/daughter romantic material restrained. Jigsaw can like her and recognize that she and Crumble love each other, but do not turn it into a prolonged YA triangle. One quiet moment where Jigsaw sees it and deliberately steps aside is stronger, especially if Crumble never fully realizes what Jigsaw gave up.
Deepen Don Rodrigo. He represents the life Jigsaw believes society stole from him: commission, rank, legitimacy, status and the woman Jigsaw once loved. However, complicate the old romance so Jigsaw was not simply an innocent victim. His anger and inability to imagine a quiet life may have contributed to losing her. Rodrigo should eventually receive a final confrontation in Florida, but strongly consider having Jigsaw defeat him and then refuse to kill him because Rodrigo no longer matters enough. That would complete Jigsaw’s movement away from rage better than revenge would.
Make the Chapter 5 raid flashback genuinely ugly. At first Jigsaw’s violence should look impressive and exciting. Then the danger ends and he keeps killing. Crumble sees the point where necessity becomes rage. Later, during the shared vision, Crumble discovers that Jigsaw did even worse things during the years they were apart.
Keep the cursed island village grounded. Avoid making every curse a clean superpower. The people should have strange, inconvenient conditions that the community has learned to incorporate into ordinary life. A woman unable to feel heat may work near kilns but also burn herself without noticing. A person’s strange shadow may happen to keep insects from fish rather than being a magical refrigeration power designed for fishing. The point is adaptation and community, not an island of superheroes.
Strengthen Domingo’s fort scene. He should create a moral problem for Crumble. Jigsaw’s father compromised for family. Balam compromised for people he loved. Domingo also betrayed the meeting because he was afraid for people he loved and for himself. That does not excuse him, but it makes it harder for Crumble to draw a clean line between heroic compromise and cowardice. Humanize Domingo without redeeming him.
Slow the opening of the fort chapter. The prisoners should initially be exhausted, angry and defeated. The fighting practice grows gradually out of weeks of confinement rather than immediately becoming a training montage. Cárdenas may knowingly allow them sticks because he believes giving broken men a little hope reveals them better than simply chaining them.
Keep Cárdenas absolutely true to his word when they accept his deal. That is more frightening than a betrayal. He releases them because he genuinely believes he has broken them and no longer needs the chains. Their confiscated weapons are not returned because that was never part of the bargain.
Make the Bonesy rescue deliberately contrast with the Chapter 3 victory. Chapter 3 is spectacular, violent and cocky. The rescue should be ugly and difficult: sticks, cords, exhaustion, injuries and winning one guard at a time. It should look less impressive while meaning much more. They are no longer fighting because they enjoy proving what they can do. They are fighting for one person they love.
Also strengthen the Cárdenas/Bonesy opposition. Cárdenas can understand Jigsaw’s rage and Crumble’s obligations because both can be studied and manipulated. Bonesy should remain unusually difficult for him to understand. Bonesy is cursed, crude, curious, happy, unpredictable and uninterested in being categorized. When Bonesy voluntarily enters the chest for his friends, Cárdenas should initially misunderstand the act because voluntary self-sacrifice does not fit the kind of leverage he expects.
Most importantly, define the codex mechanics before writing 9.3. We know that when made whole it can unmake curses and the strange, but establish what “made whole” actually means, what the Florida location does, why the codex was divided, and what someone must physically do to activate it. Avoid inventing these rules during the final battle.
Strongly consider making Florida the place where the codex can be activated rather than simply another place containing another piece. The earlier leaves can ultimately be instructions leading to the one place where the codex can alter the strange. The final puzzle should not simply be another glyph translation.
The final puzzle should instead pay off the entire crew. Cárdenas should fail to understand it because his worldview assumes knowledge and power should be concentrated in one controller. The solution should require different people doing different things: Jigsaw’s navigation and understanding of water, Crumble’s day count and Balam’s teaching, Balam’s daughter’s separate half of Balam’s knowledge, and something only Bonesy can physically do because he is dead/cursed. No individual is the chosen one. The crew is effectively the key, but nobody should state that theme aloud.
The Florida landscape should combine both brothers’ weaknesses. It is water, so Jigsaw initially expects to understand it, but mangrove estuaries have no stable horizon, channels change with tides, land and water blend together and his normal sea instincts fail. It is also unfamiliar to Crumble. Neither man is the expert. They have to learn it together.
When we build the final battle, Cárdenas must lose because of his own flaw. In Chapter 6 the heroes’ flaws help defeat them: Jigsaw’s confidence in his mastery and Crumble’s blind trust in Balam. In Chapter 9, Cárdenas’s obsession with control should create his defeat. Give him a point where victory requires trusting or relinquishing control to another person. He cannot do it. The protagonists win because they have learned something he cannot.
Do not automatically kill Cárdenas in Book One. He may be more valuable surviving after losing control of the situation, the codex and perhaps parts of his collection. For a man obsessed with certainty, ending the battle with him genuinely unable to predict what happens next may hurt more than death.
Also consider ending the codex conflict circularly. The book begins with a book deliberately broken apart. The protagonists may ultimately choose to break it apart again rather than simply destroy magic or permanently solve the problem. Do not commit to this until the activation rules and trilogy implications are tested.
Keep Morgan and Panama out of the Florida climax itself. January 1671 can instead function as an ending or Book Two stinger: after the intimate victory in Florida and return toward Tortuga, news arrives that Morgan is gathering men for Panama while England is supposedly suppressing the brethren.
