# Part one: the manuscript

## Chapter 1, The Temple

### Beatriz and the Book

Two days into the storm, Bonesy was getting nowhere with Beatriz.

He stood in front of her with what he had in place of a hand resting on her stone shoulder. He had drawn a heart in the dust between them on the first morning. He had drawn an arrow through it on the second. The arrow had wandered because his foot kept getting in the way, and now the arrow had two points and no shaft, which he had told us was a metaphor and we had not asked him for. Beatriz did not return his looks. Beatriz had not returned anyone’s looks in nine hundred years, but the fact of this had never been brought up to Bonesy in any way he would respect.

“You’ve been quiet today,” he said to her.

The wind hauled into the headland above the temple’s mouth, and a branch came down off the upper stones and skidded down the slope in pieces. The lamp Crumble had wedged between two broken slabs guttered. The shadows of the chamber moved.

“I noticed,” Bonesy said.

I had been on the windward end of what remained of a stone bench for an hour, working a knot out of a length of cord with my teeth, listening to the storm and to him. We were in the back room of an old Maya temple that had given up at the front a long time ago. The front had come down at the doorway in stones and the stones had grown over in vines, and the door was a mouth now. The chamber behind the mouth was lower than a man’s reach and four long paces deep, and at this hour of an afternoon, with the weather what it was, it was dark enough that Crumble had lit the lamp. The lamp gave us back a little of the day. It lit the carvings in the band along the walls well enough that a man could read them, if a man knew how, and Crumble did. It lit the three of us, and the three of us in that light looked like three men of dignity, which we were not.

Crumble was against the south wall with a leather pouch open in his lap. He had taken a tobacco leaf out of the pouch some time before and was working a corner of it slow in the back of his cheek, the way Balam had taught him, the way the people of this country had done before the Spanish ever came and dried the leaf and rolled it into sticks. He had a chart laid across his thighs to pass the time. He had been reading it for two days. He read charts the way a priest reads a scripture, in the small steady way of a man who is not in a hurry and does not feel he is being watched. He had closed his eyes some time back without leaving the chart there, which was his way of saying he did not want to be talked to. I knew the look. I had known it seven years.

I should say, on Bonesy.

Bonesy was a skeleton.

He had been a man before he had been a skeleton. By his own account he had been a Maya man, from a town he could no longer find on a map and no longer wanted to. He had not been anyone of importance, in his time. He had worked a patch of corn ground. He had not been a priest. He had not been the son of a priest. The priests of his town had not let him near anything they did not want everyone to see, and that had stayed with him ever since. He could not say how long ago that had been, exactly. He had been a skeleton a long time. He had not aged in any way I could see, which was the part of him that took some getting used to, and the part of him that, once you were used to it, made him hard to think about clearly. He stood a little under my height. He had nothing on him except a leather cord around his neck. He had been with Crumble and me about a year, attached the way a stray attaches to a fire, and we had stopped trying to put him anywhere else.

He talked to Beatriz the way a man talks to a wife he has been married to too long and not long enough.

“I made a list,” he said. “Things I appreciate about you. Things I find difficult.”

Crumble, without opening his eyes, said, “She’s a corn god.”

“She is what I say she is.”

“He.”

“She is a goddess.”

“He’s a god of corn. He has the husk on his brow.”

“That’s hair.”

“It’s a husk.”

“She has lovely hair.”

“He is bald under a husk.”

“Beatriz,” Bonesy said, “tell him.”

Beatriz did not.

The wind eased a notch and the lamp steadied. The lords of the underworld watched from the band along the upper wall, where they had been watching for nine hundred years. There were thirteen of them, by Crumble’s count. They had their hands folded in front of them. They had not blinked, in the time of any man in this chamber but one, and that one not by much.

Two and a half days back we had walked out of a Spanish fort at the edge of Veracruz with nothing for our trouble. The hire wanted a finger. Not any finger. The finger of a small Castilian saint who had died half a century back at a mission in Yucatán, kept now in a reliquary of gilt silver in the lower vaults, which we had taken to be one vault and had found, on getting in, to be several. There was time to crack one. Not several. I had read the sky off the high wall on the way in. The sky was wrong by an hour, in the direction that does not let you bargain with it. Crumble wanted to go down anyway. I said no. He said yes, and then he said yes once more, more quietly, which was him telling me he had heard me. The quartermaster on the south wall had given his face a longer look than I wanted to think about, and his corporal a longer one. We had not run on the way out. We had walked. We had put the sloop three hours west with the first edge of the squall already on our shoulders and tucked her into a pass we had kept in our pocket for years, between two outflung arms of stone where the surge runs up the outer arm and breaks on it and never makes the inner. The cove behind the arms was the reason the cove existed, and the reason we knew of it. She had been holding for two days. She was holding now. She was also coming out from under cover, by the hour, as the storm let up. The two were one fact looked at from two sides.

Bonesy turned his skull a quarter to look at me. He turned it the way a man does not turn his head. There is a way the head sits on a man, with the muscles of the neck holding it, and it does not move that way on its own. Bonesy’s slid round on the spine the way a stone slides on a table when the table tips. He had a kind of decency about it. He did it slowly. He gave you time to take it in.

“Jigsaw.”

“Yes.”

“Tell him she’s a rain goddess.”

“She’s a rain goddess.”

“There,” he said to Crumble. “Quorum.”

“You bribed him.”

“He volunteered.”

“He always volunteers.”

“He is loyal in his way.”

“He is bored in his way.”

I let them go. I was listening. The squall had been blowing two days out of the east-southeast and was tired now. A gale wears itself out the way a man does. In pulls and pauses. Getting more honest at the end about how it cannot keep this up forever. I could hear the headland breaking the worst of it. I could hear the cove sounding fine in the easing.

The wind drew in a long breath. Held it. Let it out a touch softer than the breath before.

There.

“It’s hauling round,” I said.

Crumble opened his eyes.

“How long.”

“Few hours. Teeth come out, then it’ll quiet by first light.”

He nodded. He closed his eyes. He took another small piece off the leaf.

“A shame,” Bonesy said. “We were making progress.”

“You were making progress,” Crumble said.

“It is a partnership.”

“It is a wall.”

“Hard women are like that.”

“She is a wall.”

“They take time.”

Crumble’s mouth almost moved. He held it. He took another piece of the leaf.

Bonesy bent down. The bones of his knees made the sound of a man cracking walnuts. He picked up a small piece of broken stone from the floor, considered it, set it on the heart in the dust as if he were laying a wreath, and stood up again. The same sound on the way up.

“So,” he said. He was looking at the wall. He was talking to the wall, which is what he did when he was talking to us. “The old priests used to talk about a book.”

I did not stop working the cord. Crumble’s jaw did.

“They used to sit in their cellars,” Bonesy said. “Not the cellars they cleaned for the festivals. The ones in the back. They didn’t want me down there. They never said so. They just looked at me a certain way and shifted their bones around. I went in anyway. I’d sit on a barrel. I’d ask questions. They’d pretend they didn’t hear me. They’d talk to each other about a book that wasn’t a book anymore because they had taken it apart on purpose. They’d argue about whose grandfather had touched a corner of it. Whose grandfather’s grandfather had. They could never agree. They’d argue all night. They could never get rid of me. It was the only thing they could never get rid of.”

He picked up another small piece of stone. He set it next to the first one.

“It was an interesting book,” he said. “By the sound of it.”

“What did it do,” I said.

Bonesy turned the little stone over.

“Never said. Not plain.”

He set it down by the others.

“The young ones said gold. A share laid in with every piece.”

“And the old ones.”

“Dropped their voices for that part.” A pause. “Even with only me in the room.”

“And you never heard.”

“One word, once.” He tipped his skull at the wall. “Before.”

The wind eased another notch.

Crumble had not opened his eyes. He had stopped chewing. That was all I needed to know.

“Balam used to talk about it,” he said.

Bonesy did not turn round.

“Did he.”

“Yes.”

“Did he have one of the pieces.”

“He was working on one. Before he went.”

“Which one.”

Crumble was a long time answering. He chewed the leaf and looked at the lamp.

“He said it was off a half-moon island,” he said. “He said the cave was hid by a thing the old priests couldn’t put a name to. He said it works the way certain weather does. A man on the shore wouldn’t see the cave. He’d see a pool.”

“He’d see a pool,” Bonesy said.

“Yes.”

I let the cord lie still in my hands.

“Tarpon,” I said.

Crumble’s eyes opened.

“What.”

“There’s tarpon in a pool on the windward side of a half-moon island west of the Bay of Honduras. Six and seven feet of them. They slide past each other on a current the pool has got no business having. Water no deeper than my waist. They don’t turn off when a man comes up on them. I crouched on the stone five years back and put my hand in the water. They went past my hand. They went past it slow.”

The chamber went quiet the way a chamber does when the wind that has been working at it for two days finally lets up.

“Jigsaw.”

“I know, Crumble.”

“Jigsaw.”

“I’ve been there.”

He closed his eyes again. He did not look surprised. He looked like a man who had been holding his breath without knowing it and had only just been able to let it out.

I had stood at the edge of that pool five years back. I had been twenty. The water behind the tarpon had not stopped moving the way a small shallow pool’s water stops, when the wind quits and there is no reason for it to keep on. The water had been pulling somewhere I could not see. I had stood and watched it the better part of an hour. I had got into the dinghy and gone home. I had not spoken of it to Crumble. I had not spoken of it to anyone. I had been telling myself for five years that someday I would go back.

Bonesy made a noise. We looked at him.

“What.”

“Nothing. Carry on.”

“Bonesy.”

“I am very moved. Don’t mind me. Beatriz is moved. We are both moved. Carry on.”

“Bonesy.”

“What.”

“Sit down.”

He sat down in front of Beatriz. He took her, gently, off her base, and set her in his lap, and stared at the wall.

Outside, the storm came down off its peak and the wind hauled another quarter. The air in the chamber had changed. There is a way air changes in a small room when three men in it have been quiet for two days and one of them has finally said the thing the other two were waiting for. The lamp does not light it any differently. It sits a little heavier on the shoulders, is all.

“We can’t leave it,” Crumble said. He was saying it to himself.

“No,” I said. “We can’t.”

The lamp guttered. The lords of the underworld in their row watched, as they had been, and would.

Bonesy stepped on the heart in the dust by accident, and stepped off it carefully, the way a man does when he doesn’t want to admit he’s done damage.

“Beatriz,” he said. “I am about to have an adventure. Try not to take it personally.”

Beatriz did not.

### The Finger

The storm went the way I said it would. The teeth came out of it past midnight, a few hard hours throwing what it had left against the headland, and then by the grey before first light it had spent itself and gone north to be some other coast’s trouble. I had slept the way I sleep on a job, in pieces, with one ear out, and when the chamber stopped flinching I came all the way awake and lay listening to the cove. The surge had come down. The pass was talking quietly to itself the way it does on a falling sea. She had taken the whole blow without complaint, which is the most you can ask of a boat and more than you can ask of most men.

Crumble was already up, sitting against the south wall with his hands loose on his knees, looking at nothing, which was him being ready to go an hour before there was anything to go to. He had not slept much. I could see it and I left it. There are men you ask after and men you leave to find their own bottom, and Crumble has been the second kind since we were boys.

Bonesy had Beatriz in his lap still. He had not put her back on her base.

I got up and stretched the night out of my back. The chamber in the grey light was a smaller, poorer place than it had been by the lamp. The lords on the wall had gone flat, just scratches in old stone, and Beatriz in his lap was a corn god with a chip out of his brow, and the heart in the dust had been walked through so many times in the night it was only dust again. That is the way of places. They are large in the dark and keep their size only as long as you are afraid of them.

“We’ll want to be off her by full light,” I said. “She’s been under cover two days and the cover’s gone. Anybody who came up out of the south after the blow with a glass and an hour of clear sky finds a hull where there’s meant to be none.”

“You think the fort sent word,” Crumble said.

“I think the quartermaster looked at your face longer than a man looks at a face he means to forget. I don’t think past that. But I don’t want to be the hull he finds.”

Crumble nodded and got himself up the wall to standing, and stood with his hand flat over his coat where the chart was, and I knew he had the half-moon island in his hand the way I had it in mine. West and a little south. Six days with the wind in its right quarter. The pool. The door I stood at when I was twenty and did not go through.

Here is a thing about me I will set down because it matters to what came after. I wanted to go. Not the way Crumble wanted to go. Crumble wanted it because of Balam, because a dead man set it in his hands and dying men’s wishes are the heaviest things there are. I wanted it because I have spent my life on the top of the water wondering what the water was keeping from me, and here at last was a door down into it, and I have wanted to go through that door since before I had a word for wanting. The gold was real and I’ll not pretend it wasn’t. But under the gold was the door, and under the door the thing I have never been able to name and have been chasing up and down these islands since I was a boy, and I knew if I let it, the wanting would put us on the wrong reef with the wrong gear and end us.

So I was the one who said it.

“We can’t sail for it like this.”

He looked at me.

“We’ve a finger we didn’t get. No pay for it, because there’s no pay till it’s in the man’s hand. Two days of water in the hold. We go through that door like this and we come up the way Balam came up. Old, broke, and not finished. I’d rather go a month late and go right.”

His jaw set. Something moved behind his eyes and was put away, and I knew the shape of it, because I’d handed it to him. I’d as good as said he’d run us at the thing half-cocked if I let him, that the man Balam made couldn’t be trusted to wait. He didn’t answer it. He let it sit in him. That is the way of us. There are things we hand each other that neither one says out loud, and they go in, and they stay in, and you find them again years later still where you put them.

“So Petit-Goâve,” he said. “It’s the wrong heading.”

“It’s the wrong heading and the right order. East before west. We get the finger to the man, we get paid, we fit her out, then we point her at your old man’s island with a full hold and a clean name. We don’t go down empty.”

“We’ve nothing to put in front of him,” Crumble said. “We walked out of that fort with our hands as bare as we walked in. There’s no pay for empty hands.”

“No,” I said. “There isn’t.”

And there it was, the plan dead on the floor before it could stand, because the man only pays for the finger, and the finger was in a vault under a Spanish courtyard three hours east of us with a lid on it.

Which is when Bonesy set Beatriz down off his lap, gentle, and stood.

“I’ve been waiting two days for one of you to get to that part,” he said.

He reached to the cord at his neck. There was a thinner cord run through the thick one, that I had not marked in a year of looking at him, and he drew it up over the knobs of his spine and held the end of it out into the grey coming through the vines.

A finger hung off it. Bone, yellow-brown, longer in the joint than my own, worn smooth at the knuckle the way a thing gets worn by a great many hands that thought touching it would do them good.

Neither of us moved. I have been in a great many rooms when the thing in them changed, and I knew the room had changed, and I was half a step behind it, which does not happen to me often and which I did not enjoy.

“That’s the finger,” Crumble said, the way you say a word in a dream. “That’s the saint. That’s the pay.”

“That’s the pay,” said Bonesy.

“You’ve had it.”

“Since the cellar. While you two were up in the courtyard reading the sky at each other, I went and had a look at the part of the fort you were so sure we’d no time for.” He turned the bone on its cord. “Nobody minds me on a stair. Nobody shoots at a thing they take for a trick of the lamp. I went down where the dead ones were kept. There was a little gold house, very fine, and her shut up inside it in the dark where nobody could so much as say good morning to her.” He looked at the finger, and for a moment he was not playing to us. “I’ve been in a gold house. I didn’t care for it. So I took her out and left them the box.”

“Two days,” Crumble said. “We sat in this rock two days sick over it. You watched us.”

“You were very sad. It was educational.” He tilted his skull. “And had I shown you in the courtyard you’d have wanted the box as well, and we’d all three be living in the box now.”

The laugh came up out of Crumble before he could stop it, low and cracked and not rightly knowing if it was a laugh or the other thing, and he sat back against the wall with it and put the heel of his hand to his eye. I felt it climb in me too, the foulness and the relief together, two days of mourning turned in one stroke into two days of a skeleton wearing our pay round his neck and watching us grieve. There is a kind of luck so rotten and so good at once that the only thing a man can do with it is laugh.

But the anger was still in Crumble under it, the two days of it, and it had its say once the laugh thinned.

“You let us sit in it, you bony bastard. Two whole days.”

“I did. I’d have done three.” He was not sorry. He does not get sorry. “You learn things about men, watching them think they’ve lost.”

“He’s right, though,” Crumble said to me, and the laugh was gone out of his voice now, just the plain fact of it left. “Not about the three days. About the rest. We’d not have got within a wall of that cellar and you know it. They’d have had us on the stair. He walks down there and he’s a shadow, a ball goes through him and it’s a hole in the air, a sea that drowns us is weather to him. He goes where we can’t and he doesn’t even have to be brave about it. He just goes.” He looked at Bonesy and said it level, the way a man says a thing he means and wants heard: “We’ve no hope of that island without you. None.”

I watched Bonesy take it.

He went still in a way he does not usually go still, the performing gone clean off him for a breath, the skull level, the empty of him turned full on Crumble. He has been a long time among people who’d not have cared if he lay down in a ditch and stopped, and you don’t lose that the way you lose a smell off your clothes. Then he gathered himself back up into something he could carry.

“Yes,” he said. “Well. One of us has to be of use.”

But he’d heard it. I saw him hear it.

“So we get her to the man,” I said, to give him somewhere to put it. “He’s at Petit-Goâve, or his people are. We hand her over, we get paid, we fit her out, and we go.”

Bonesy looked down at the finger against his front.

“We hand her over,” he said.

“That’s the job.”

“He’ll house her again. Your man. He didn’t pay to set a saint loose in the air. He paid to have her in a box of his own instead of a box of theirs.” He turned her on the cord, slow. “Out of one gold house and into the next. That’s the whole of what we’ve done for her.”

“It’s the whole of what we were paid to do,” I said.

“I know it.” And he did. He stopped turning her and let her rest against the front of him, and he did not argue it, and that was the thing I marked, because Bonesy argues everything, it is the one labor he never tires of, and he did not argue this. He just looked at her a moment longer than the joke wanted. Then he ran the thin cord back down inside the thick one and settled her out of sight, and the matter was closed, and he was the practical thing again.

We left the way you leave a place you don’t mean to see again and don’t want to make a thing of. Crumble stopped at the mouth and said something low in the old tongue to the wall, that I didn’t have and wasn’t meant to. Bonesy stopped by Beatriz. He stood over her a moment, set her square on her base facing out the way she’d faced nine hundred years, and stepped back. He drew no heart. He made no joke.

“Goodbye,” he said to her, and went out ahead of us into the morning, and did not look back at her, and did not touch the cord at his neck, though I half thought he would.

The storm had scrubbed the morning clean. The sky had gone that hard washed blue it goes after a blow, and the cove was so clear over the white sand you could count the links of her chain from the rocks. She sat in the pass between the two stone arms with her single mast raked back and her hull the colour of a thing built not to be seen, riding to her scope, waiting on us like she’d known all along we’d be back and had better things to think about in the meantime.

I stood on the rock above her and looked past her, out the pass, south and east, the road to Petit-Goâve.

It was empty.

I looked at it a while, the way you look at a thing because its being empty is a kind of luck you have learned not to trust. Then I went down to take us off.

## Chapter 2, Petit-Goâve

### Nine Days East

It is six days from that island to Petit-Goâve with the wind in its right quarter, and the wind was not in its right quarter, so it was nine. Nine days is a long while to be penned on a small boat with two men carrying things they will not set down, and a third who sets nothing down because he carries nothing, and fills the quiet with whatever comes into his skull.

It was on the third day that Crumble named whose fort it had been.

We were close-hauled and beating, and he had the tiller, and I was forward working an eye into a line that had chafed through in the blow.

“That garrison was Cárdenas’s,” he said, to the horizon.

I did not stop working the line. I did not need to ask which Cárdenas. There is only the one, for us. We had crossed his people before and carried it on us still, Crumble in a seam of scar under his ribs and me in the habit of counting a Spanish crew twice before I believed the number. Don Mateo de Cárdenas.

“We robbed a cellar of his,” I said.

“We robbed an empty box out of a cellar of his,” Crumble said. “He’ll have found it by now.”

“A man like that. Finding a box in his own house with the lid up and nothing in it.”

“A man like that,” Crumble agreed, and shifted the tiller a hair to feel the wind, and that was the whole of what we said, because there was nothing to do about it from a small boat six days from anywhere, and because the thing we were not talking about was still louder than Cárdenas, who is a hard thing to be louder than.

We raised Petit-Goâve on the ninth evening, the hills behind it black against a sky going the colour of a bruise on the mend, and came into the road under the last light with the canvas down to nothing, the way you come into a place where everyone watching owns a glass and a grudge.

### The Hat

The sale went easy, the way it goes when you have the saint and the man wants the saint and neither of you is fool enough to pretend otherwise. The hire kept an agent above a cooperage on the harbour front, a round Portuguese named Vaz who smelled of cloves. He took the finger in a square of black cloth and held it to a candle and turned it, and for one breath the cloves and the soft round manner went off him and there was the man underneath who handled saints for a living, and then it came back, and he paid us. He paid us well, the way you pay men you would like to use again and would rather not have annoyed at you. We went down the cooperage stair heavier in the coat than we had been in a year, free of the job, and clean.

Which meant it was time to spend some of it, because a man does not go through a door at the bottom of the sea on the gear he had spare.

We have our own pieces. Those a man does not buy in a port, because a man’s own gear is half him by the time it is worth anything, and you do not buy half yourself off a table.

Crumble carries a blade Balam had made for him the year he finished teaching him, a thing shaped older than a cutlass and shorter, straight and broad and squared at the tip, after a pattern the old people used, with a grip wound in dyed leather and a guard worked with the small blocky figures that mean things to Crumble and nothing to me. He wears the old people on him on purpose, panels of worked cloth and plate over the European coat, a sash dyed the deep red they made from a bug and a root, so that he walks into a Spanish room looking like the thing forty years of Cárdenas has been spent trying to scrub off the earth, and he does it knowing exactly what he looks like, which is the bravest small thing I know a man to do daily.

I wear hides, but not the way a man pictures when I say it. Not skins thrown over the shoulder like some hunter come down out of the hills. I wear a pirate’s coat and a pirate’s rig, cut and stitched the way any man’s is on this water, only I built mine, and I built it out of what the sea and the hunt gave me. Hide worked into the facings where the salt eats wool to nothing. Hide at the shoulders and the forearms where a blade looks to land. Hide sewn down over the canvas in panels, oiled dark, so it turns water where canvas drinks it and breathes where wool boils a man in his own sweat. It is mine, every piece of it off an animal I knew the death of, and it has kept the parts of me a man wants kept more times than I can count. To a stranger it reads as a coat that has been somewhere. That is the whole of what I want it to read as.

And I do not carry a cutlass. I carry two Spanish blades, lighter than shipboard iron and quicker in a crowd. They are Toledo in shape, but the steel is older than that in its making: crucible steel, wootz by another name, high-carbon and watered in the Arab fashion, with dark lines running through it when the light catches right.

I learned the Spanish sword before I even knew what Tortuga was, in rooms with swept floors and boys whose fathers had paid for them to stand straight. They called it form. Form is useful until someone is trying to kill you. I learned the rest in back rooms with dirt on the floor and coin underfoot, where steel went where it could reach and no one called it unfair until he was opened.

Needless to say me and Crumble were not going to be buying new swords.

The gear we wanted was the plain kind a man replaces, and we went to get it. Powder, dry, off a Dutchman who kept it dry and charged for the keeping, and a fresh paper of flints to spark it. Cartridge wadding and a ball-mould and bar lead to cast our own. A coil of good hemp and a bolt of canvas for the sailmaker’s work that never ends. Pitch and oakum for her seams. Salt pork in the cask and turtle we’d take live and keep on its back in the bilge for fresh meat, and hard biscuit, and a netted sack of limes and another of oranges against the rot that loosens a man’s teeth and then takes the rest of him. A breaker of water sweetened with a little rum so it would keep. Crumble went off on his own account and came back with the things no Dutchman stocks, got from the Mayans who keep to the back lanes of any port on this coast. Ground cacao pressed into hard tablets, bitter as bilge and worth more than its weight to a man on a long sail. A twist of dried chiles. Pom, the tree-blood incense the old people burn to their dead, which he did not explain and I did not ask after. Supplies are supplies, and a man’s reasons are his own.

And at a slop-seller’s stall with the lamps coming gold along the front, we bought Bonesy a hat. We thought nothing of it. A good one, black felt, broad, the brim stiff, a band of plain dark ribbon, the kind of thing any of the three of us might have wanted off the table, and Crumble paid for it the same as he’d paid for the limes.

Bonesy took it and turned it once in what he has for hands and put it on. Then he stood under it a moment longer than the moment wanted, and put his hand up and touched the brim, just touched it, settling it, the way a man does with a thing that is his.

I had been about to say something idle and did not. In all the time he had been with us, Bonesy had worn nothing, a cord at his neck and that the whole of it, and I had taken it for one of his ways, like the not sleeping, like the talking. It was not a way. A hermit crab will go bare and pinched its whole life before it climbs into a shell that is not its own, and the bare ones do not last. He had been the bare kind. He had stopped looking for a shell so long ago he had forgotten it was a thing a creature could have, and not one of us, in a year, had thought to hand him one.

He did not say anything about it, and neither did I. Some things you carry better unsaid.

We got him a belt too, broad, to take a blade, and hung a short sword on it that he swore he had no use for and kept anyway. He drew the line there. No coat, no boots. A hat, a belt, a blade. Enough that a man across a room would take him for a man and not a haunting, which was further across that line than he had ever stood, and far enough for one night.

Crumble was already pulling his collar up, though it was warm. “I’m away up to find Domingo,” he said. “He’ll know if the old man left word with anyone but me. He keeps the ears of half the coast and lends them cheap to people he likes.” He looked at me. “He knew Balam. Boys together, before any of it. He goes by Domingo down here because a Maya name in a Spanish port is a thing men collect you for. To me he’s not Domingo.” He said the other name, soft, with the finish I do not have. “Don’t follow me up. He won’t open with strangers by, and the two of you are a great deal of strange.”

“We’ll find a tavern and lower rum,” I said. “You’ll know us by the wreckage.”

He went up the hill into the dark of the town, and I watched him go until I couldn’t, the way I always do.

### The Clean Face

Bonesy and I went the other way, down toward the water.

There is not much to Petit-Goâve and there is not meant to be. One front street along the harbour, a scatter of shacks and storehouses climbing the hill behind it, the fort up top, and the whole of it small enough that a man can stand at the seawall and see every place worth going. It is an outpost more than a town, a place men come to spend what they took and take on what they need before they are somewhere else. There is no one kind of man in it. There is the Welshman and the Dutchman and the Frenchman who would cut each other’s throats in any harbour that flew a flag worth the name, and the freed man and the run man and the man with no country left to claim him, and the outpost asks none of them where they came from or what they did to need it. That is the whole of its welcome and the whole of its strength. A man walks into Petit-Goâve off any deck under any colours and by the second cup he is no stranger, because the outpost was built to take in exactly the men no other place would.

The French hold it, after a fashion. A governor sits up in the fort under their colours and takes his cut of everything that crosses the harbour, the casks and the captives and every prize sold on the front, and what he gives back for it I have never been able to find. He does not keep the peace; the brethren keep their own. He does not feed the place or defend it past his own wall. He takes his share and signs his papers and flies his flag, and the men on the front let him, the way you let the gulls have the offal, because it is easier than minding them and they were always going to take it anyway.

The heat does not let up after dark here. It only changes its smell. By day the front reeks of tar and gutted fish and the hot stone of the seawall; by night it goes sweeter and worse, rum and woodsmoke and the sugar-rot drifting off the cane barges, and under all of it the green wet breath of the island itself, which never dries. We passed the slave pens behind the customs house, where the Company keeps its cargo between ships, and a man does well not to look too long into the dark of them, and I did not, and Bonesy did, the way he always does, because the dead and the soon-to-be-dead are the only company he has ever truly belonged to and he has never once pretended otherwise.

The tavern sat where the front street gives out, behind a chandlery, and it was the tavern, the one the whole outpost drinks in, because an outpost this size has room for one and no more. It was a Spanish building, from before the brethren made this coast unfit for Spaniards, coral-rock walls two feet thick and beams of black hardwood overhead, and it had stood through more weather than the men in it had years between them. The hurricane that took the church roof three streets over had taken half the front street with it and left this building standing as if the storm had looked at it and thought better of the argument. Storms come through Petit-Goâve every season and knock down what the careless built, and every season this one is still here, and a man stops asking why and just drinks under it.

It was full, and loud, and the right sort of place, which is to say no one looked at Bonesy twice. That is the gift of the outpost and the whole reason a man like him can drink at all. Anywhere else a walking skeleton in a black hat is the end of days come walking in the door. Here he was maybe the fourth strangest thing on the floor, and falling.

Because the strange washes up in a place like this the way sea grass piles on a windward beach, a little more every tide, and the town has long since stopped stepping around it and learned to step over it instead. At a table near the back a ring of men were at cards, and the stake in the middle of them was not coin. It was a skull, small, yellow as old ivory, sitting on the boards in its own little cleared space the way a thing sits when men are half afraid to touch it. I have seen a dozen like it in a dozen such rooms. They all carry the same weight, that is the strange part, a heaviness that is not in the bone, a feeling when you hold one that you are holding something that knows you are holding it. Men will swear they have seen them burn with their own light at the dark of the moon. I have held them at the dark of the moon and seen nothing, and felt that weight the whole while, and put them down no surer than I picked them up. I have known good men go thin and strange chasing what a skull like that was supposed to do, sell their share and then their boat and then themselves to the want of it, and die no nearer an answer than the day they first held one. Whatever sits inside a thing like that, it has buried more men than it has ever answered.

And behind a rail and a curtain at the back, past a man you paid a coin to get by, was the other trade, the one the front of every port is built to feed. They had a woman back there who was not all the way a woman, scales from the hip down gone grey and dull for want of the sea, got off some Dutchman who’d kept her in a tank on his foredeck. The men in the line did not stand in it because she was a wonder out of the oldest story sailors tell. They stood in it because she was strange and could not get up and leave, and there is a kind of man who will always pay the most for the thing that cannot walk away from him. The town had been handed a creature out of the deep mystery of the sea and found, without having to think about it, the one use it has for everything, which is to put a curtain in front of it and a coin on the other side of the curtain. That, more than the skull or the dice or Bonesy himself, is the truest strange thing in any of these places. Not that the sea throws up wonders. That men can stand in front of one and see only a thing to spend.

Bonesy considered the curtain as we crossed to our corner.

“Tail and all,” he said, the scrape of it warm with the pleasure of his own joke, “and they’ve still found the one end of her that turns a coin. You have to admire a town that practical. A man could drown back there and die happy and they’d charge his shipmates for the water.”

“You’re a foul thing.”

“I’m an honest thing. It’s worse.” He dropped onto the bench with his new hat and his new belt and the blade he had no use for, well pleased with himself. “Buy me a cup I can’t drink, Jigsaw. I like to hold a thing while I watch other men make poor decisions.”

So I bought a bottle of something brown that had been other things before it was rum, and a cup he could not drink from, and we took the corner.

We had not been in the corner long when I marked the one face in the room that did not belong to it.

Two tables off, a man sat alone with a cup he was not drinking. His face was too clean for the room. Not washed. Clean. The clean of skin kept soft on purpose, a jaw shaved that morning by careful hands, the face of a man who has never once hauled a wet line or stood a night watch in spray. You do not come by that face honestly in such a place. He had dressed it down, a sailor’s coat, a sailor’s slouch, the costume right in every part, but the face would not come down with the cloth, the way a ladyfish will throw itself clear of the water like a tarpon and fool you for half a breath, until you see the size of it and know exactly what you’re looking at.

And when his eye crossed Bonesy it did a thing he could not stop, the way a lizard throws its throat at any shape that passes, no thought in it, only the old machinery underneath. Disgust, up out of him before he could sit on it, the look a devout man keeps for the thing his book has told him to hate. Then he caught it and smoothed it back down and was a tired sailor again, a man waiting on a tide.

A clean Spanish face in a French outpost. That alone would have been enough.

I set my cup down slow, and the trouble I worked out sitting there was not the trouble a man would guess. He had heard little. A skeleton going on about dead priests, which in that room is no more than the noise the room is made of, and somewhere in the easy run of it, that the other one of us had gone up the hill to see a Maya man about an old book. That was the whole of it. To him, a few random marks on a chart, no course in them yet. He was a set of ears and nothing more, sent to carry back whatever he caught for a cleverer head to read. It was no fault of Bonesy’s. A man talks in a tavern. That is what a tavern is for. In any other port the words would have gone up the chimney with the smoke.

The trouble was the head he carried them to.

I have stood off Cárdenas’s people twice, and the second time I went and learned what I could of the man they answered to, because a man who has nearly killed you twice is a man worth the reading. And what I learned is that he does not break a people and sail on. He keeps them. He keeps their books, the few the fires missed, and their gods in his cabinets, and their histories in his head, the whole shape of a thing he has spent a life unmaking, because you cannot truly finish a people until you understand them better than they understood themselves, and he means to finish them, so he understands them. He is the one Spaniard alive who could take a broken Maya book, and an old man newly dead who knew of it, and one of that old man’s pupils gone up a hill to a Maya keeper, and lay those few stray marks on the chart and see the course run clean between them at a glance.

The spy had heard almost nothing. Cárdenas would see everything.

I leaned to Bonesy, who had gone quiet the moment my cup touched the wood, because for all that he never stops, he misses nothing.

“Clean Spanish face, two tables, your left,” I said, low. “Don’t turn. It’s not what he heard. It’s who he carries it to.”

Bonesy did not turn his head.

“Cárdenas,” he said. The scrape of it dropped almost to nothing.

“Cárdenas.”

“And in this town we can’t put a knife in him for it.”

“No. We can’t.”

“I’ve never had much use for that law,” Bonesy said, slow.

“It’s the only roof these people have. We don’t pull it down over one clean face. Sit.”

He sat. And across the room the man rose, unhurried, the way they are taught, and laid a coin on his table too good for the rum, and went out into the night up the same hill Crumble had gone up, where a clean Spanish face goes when it has something worth sending fast to an old master forty years at his trade.

And up that same hill, ahead of him, my brother was walking to the one man left on the earth who was a piece of Balam, not knowing he had grown a shadow the moment he turned off the front.

There was not one thing in the world the law would let me do about any of it.

### The Escape

I sat with it a while. A man goes up a hill. Another man goes up after him. I sit in a corner with a bottle of brown and the law of this place in my lap.

Bonesy let it run a good while. For him.

“You’ve gone quiet.”

“I have.”

He turned the cup he could not drink from, once, on the boards.

“You going up.”

“No.”

“Why.”

“Because he told me not to. Because I’d get there last.” The bottle had gone warm in my hand and I had not drunk from it. “And because I said an hour ago that the law was the only roof these people have.”

“That’s three.”

“It is.”

“Men with one reason go,” Bonesy said.

I did not answer that. I took the bottle out to the step, where I could see the hill.

There were lights coming down it. Four, evenly apart, holding the spacing where men walking home would have bunched and straggled. They had come out of the fort gate.

Bonesy came out behind me. He does not breathe, so there is never any warning.

“Soldiers.”

“Soldiers.”

“In this town.” He said it slow. “Coming down.”

“Cárdenas has bought the garrison,” I said. “And the law with it.”

Bonesy was quiet a moment. “That fast.”

“The clean face went up an hour ago.”

“I said I never had much use for that law.”

“You did.”

“I’ve a use for it now.”

Above the lanterns the hill went black where the trees start. I stood and looked at the black for longer than I had to spare. Bonesy stood beside me and said nothing, which he can keep up about as long as a man can hold his breath.

“He’ll come down to the water,” he said.

“He’ll come down to the water.”

“Then we want to be on it.”

We went down to the water at a walk. A man running on that street turns every head on it, and I did not want those heads going up the hill yet.

She lay where we had left her, bow out, with the last of the evening’s buying still on the stones beside her. The pork was aboard. The powder was aboard, because powder goes aboard first. What stood on the stones was the breaker of water, and the netted sack of oranges, and a coil of good hemp none of us had got to.

A full breaker wants two men at the stones and a third at the rail, and we had no third.

“I can get it aboard,” Bonesy said.

“How long.”

He looked at it a moment. He did not lie to me.

“Longer than you’ll want to give me.”

“Then leave it.”

He left it. He put the oranges over the rail and left the water standing on the stones, and I have thought about that breaker more since than I have thought about most men I have killed.

They came off the hill and onto the head of the front street and stopped.

Men were already coming out behind them. Out of the tavern, off the decks along the quay, out of the chandlery with the door still going. Nobody drew. They came out with their cups in their hands and stood on the stones and looked up the street, and there got to be a great many of them, and the four lights stayed where they were.

One of the Frenchmen lifted his lantern to put it on a face and lowered it again.

They did not come past the chandlery.

We got her lines singled and I took the tiller and we did not go.

The tide was making. I had until it turned and a little after, and past that the gap out by the point goes foul and stays foul till morning.

I worked it out three times. The answer did not move. I worked it out again.

“He’s coming,” Bonesy said.

I did not answer.

“Jigsaw.”

“I heard you.”

He let it sit. Then, quieter than he goes: “You’ve done the sum.”

“I’ve done it.”

“How long.”

I told him. Bonesy, who has never once run out of talk, did not say anything.

I gave the tide away. I gave it all the way away, past the sum and out the other side of it.

He did not come down the street. I had been watching the street.

He came along the water from the west, out of the dark past the last of the storehouses. He was wet to the thigh. He had Balam’s blade still in his hand and nothing else in it.

I put my hand out and took his wrist and got him over the rail, and I did not let go of the wrist as quick as I might have.

Bonesy looked past him, back up the water the way he had come, for a good while after there was any use in looking.

Crumble went forward and put his hand on the cable.

“Let her go,” he said.

We went out on the last of it. The gap by the point let us through with water under her. The breaker stood on the quay where we had left it, pale on the dark stones, and it was standing there still when the town went behind the point.

Above the town the lights had gone back up the hill and into the fort, in the same order they came down in.

## Chapter 3, The Cay

### Running Dark

It was a long while before anyone spoke. The outpost shrank astern to a scatter of lamps and then to nothing.

“Domingo,” I said. Not a question.

“In the trees above the town. He’d not meet me anywhere with walls.” Crumble had his eyes on the water ahead. “Garrison came straight up the path. Somebody sold the meeting before it happened.”

“And you got out.”

“Put a smoke down and went into the green. Domingo stood up out of it so they’d have a man to take.” His hands stayed easy on the tiller. “Last I saw, they had him on his knees and the Spaniard in the clean coat was looking at the trees. Right at them.” He let a breath out. “So I came down to the boat.”

I did not tell him he was right to leave. He knew he was right to. There is nothing crueler you can tell a man than that he was right to leave his friend on his knees in the dark, and nothing truer, so I said neither, and let the wind have it.

“You get what you went for?” Bonesy said. He’d come aft from the cable, the new hat a black shape against the stars.

“Part of it. It’s real, and it’s broken, and the pieces run in a line, like the marks on a chart. The cave off Jigsaw’s island is the first mark. Find it right and it sets you the bearing to the next.”

“And he gave you that bearing?”

“He was getting to it in the trees when the garrison came up the path.”

So we had the first mark and no leg drawn past it, and the one man who could have laid the rest of the course was on his knees in Petit-Goâve. Bonesy looked back the way we’d come, north and east, where the lamps had been.

“They’ll not sit on that long,” he said. “A man like the one you’ve drawn, he’ll have a hull behind us by the morning watch.”

“Let them,” Crumble said. “Nobody on this sea catches her with a clean bottom and a fair wind.”

“They will if it blows,” I said. “We run from weather. The kind of ship he’ll send—”

“Sails straight through it,” Bonesy said. He said it lightly, the way he says the things that aren’t, and took the weight of it off me, which is a thing he does more often than I let on.

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer. It went into the boat with the rest of the night’s weight and we left it there.

“We’ll be thirsty before any of it,” I said, to put us back on a thing we could mend. “We left the water on the quay. A day aboard, maybe less.”

“There’s the cacao,” Crumble said.

The pressed tablets the Mayans carried, that he’d had the sense to lay in. It would keep us standing if it kept us nothing else, and tasted, he allowed, like the scrapings off a wet deck. But cacao is dry, and a man cannot drink it dry.

“We water before the island. Off the run, nowhere they’ll watch.” I ran the cays in my head, which held and which went dry. “There’s a string of low ones west and south. Most are sand and nothing. But there’s one the reef wraps the whole windward face of, and that one I never made, the gap looked mean and I had water enough at the time not to chance it.”

“The one with the palms standing in a hook,” Bonesy said.

I looked at him.

“I’ve been to it.”

He has been to nearly every scrap of land in this sea and remembers almost none of them. The ones he keeps are not the ones where men took him in, because men never did. A walking dead thing is the first over the side when a boat runs short, and he has been put over the side of more boats than I’ve sailed. What he keeps is the stone. A face in a wall that did not decide he was expendable, that did not leave.

“There’s a spring behind the rocks at the back,” he said. “And a face the old people cut over it. Ixchel. I sat with her a season once.” A beat. “Worse company than Beatriz, and Beatriz is a post.”

“Then the water’s clean,” Crumble said. “The Mayans cut a face over a spring to mark it safe.”

So between us we had it. My reckoning of the reef and his of the stone. One gap in that windward face, the surge standing in it like a wall, a boat through at first light or not at all. She’d go through. I’d put her through. I kept the rest in my head.

I took the first watch. Crumble would not sleep and Bonesy never does, and so we went on three men awake on a small boat, the way we’d come in, lighter by all our water and heavier by everything else, and one eye, all of us now, on the dark astern.

### The Girl

Bonesy lasted until the lamps of Petit-Goâve were down under the sea behind us, and not a breath past it.

He folded onto the thwart across from Crumble and tipped the new hat back off his brow with one finger, easy, a man settling in to enjoy himself.

“You’re good at that, aren’t you,” he said. “Leaving a fellow behind.”

“I know what I did.”

“Not scolding. Some men can’t do it at all. You don’t even slow down.”

Crumble worked the leaf and said nothing, which in him is an answer.

A man taken in the dark is a small thing to Bonesy, and you sit with him a while before you see why. He has watched the short-lived get hauled off and drowned and hung for nine hundred years, and from where he stands we all fetch up in the same harbor soon enough, the only question the hour of it. He is not standing close enough to the loss to feel its size. The rest of us have no choice but to.

“He’d not meet me anywhere with walls,” Crumble said. “His choosing.”

“Sure it was. Who’d you leave the first time.”

He said nothing.

Bonesy knew most of it already. A year in a small boat and he had all our headlines whether we handed them over or not. He was not after the story. He likes to lay a finger on a sore place and feel it jump, the way a boy presses a bruise to know it’s still his.

“Come on. The girl.”

“You know about the girl.”

“I like to hear you say her.”

Crumble took a turn off the sheet that did not need taking and made it fast. “Ixmal,” he said. The old name, the one Balam’s people gave a child born in a hard season, that means something near enough to bear. “A town. I was sixteen and a crew would have me.”

“And you went.”

“And I went.”

“Quick about it, were you.”

“Quick about everything, then.” He turned the leaf. That was all he gave it, and it was more than he gives most.

What he did not say, I knew, and it sat in the boat under the little he did say, the way the deep part of a channel sits under the bright of it. The sea was the largest thing a boy of sixteen had ever stood beside, and a girl in a town is a small steady light against a whole horizon, and he chose the horizon, the way he was always going to. She did not go to ruin after. She married and grew children and a good life in the exact place he had stood, closed over the hole of him without a mark, and that is the wound. Not that he hurt her. That she could do without him so clean. Mine runs the other way and comes to the same water. We have never set the two side by side and named them the one thing, and they are no more the same than the water in a spring and the water in the sea, and no less, and a man dying of thirst in a boat learns the difference is the whole of everything.

“So now you put nothing down,” Bonesy said, light still, but in. “Promises. Dead men’s errands.”

“That’s loyalty.”

“That’s paying.”

Crumble took the chart from under his coat and held it without unrolling it, which is the most of an answer Bonesy was going to get, and we all knew it, and it went into the boat with the rest of the night’s weight and we left it.

At first light Crumble turned east and said the day’s name under his breath in the old tongue, as he has every dawn since Balam died.

The thirst was bad by full light and the hunger under it worse. Want makes a man quiet. It sits in him and sands him down, and by midmorning the only one of us in any humour was the one with no belly to ache.

“Try wanting nothing,” Bonesy said, watching us not eat. “Gave it up ages back. Light as a gull.”

“You want a hat.”

“Got the hat.”

I put a handline over and trolled it on the quarter while the cay came up green and low, and short of noon a little tunny took it. Crumble bled him over the side and we ate him raw and warm. It gives a man back a little but never the thing he’s after, the salt of a fish only sharpening the want of clean water down the line. It steadied us. Enough to think with.

Bonesy watched us eat, the way he watches everything we do with our mouths, like a man at the border of a country he’ll never hold papers for.

“You went back,” he said to Crumble. Not a question.

Crumble’s hands kept on the fish.

“Once.”

“And.”

“There was no room in her life the size of me.” He flicked the bones over. “Came home.”

That was the whole of it, four words doing the work of a year of standing in the trees, and Bonesy let them, because even he knows when a man has handed over the true thing and there’s no call to press it flatter.

Home was our crew, and then it was not. Our last year aboard, on a raid with the deck going up in flames, he came out of the smoke with a Maya girl over his shoulder. I did not see what came before. She told him her father would make something of him if he had it in him. After the town and the room too small for him, he went and found out. Three years under Balam. He came back with the blade and a way of standing still I have never learned, and a saying of the old man’s for most weathers.

### The Gate and the Spring

Which was when I looked astern, the way I had since Petit-Goâve without rightly stopping, and at the hard rim of the sea where there had been nothing all morning, there was a sail.

I watched it to be sure. A tired eye builds ships out of haze. But it held, too much canvas for the easy wind, a hull driven and not sailed, standing off no honest business of its own.

We had known he sent it. That much we carried out of Petit-Goâve for certain, that the man does not wait on weather the way we do, that a hull built to cut right through a storm the way a sword tears through flesh.

“There he is,” Bonesy said, almost fond, the way he greets what the rest of us dread.

And dead ahead, low and green and ringed all round with bright shoal water, the cay came on, and the one mean gate in it, and behind the gate the only water for sixty miles that would not kill us.

The gate did not look like a gate. It looked like a wide bright road in. That is the trick of clear water, and it has drowned more men than rough water ever has. Rough water frightens a man honest. Clear water lies to him sweet. I read the reef off the colour of it, the dark green that is depth and the brown green that is a coral head a foot down. There was one way into that cove, the way there is one gap in a fence a fox can take, and we barely made it.

The cay sat low and green ahead. A hook of palms, and behind them other trees climbing higher, all of them competing for the sun, a jungle island shouldering up out of the sea. A beach under the palms the colour of bone. No track on it, no fire-black, no mark of a boat ever drawn up.

As we rounded into the cove we dropped the anchor. The water was warm as blood, and we went over the side and swam in.

Bonesy went up the sand ahead of us, skipping, a choirboy let out of the cathedral early.

“Mind the wet rocks,” he sang out.

Behind the rocks the spring came out of a low grey face of stone, and over it the Mayans had cut a woman, worn near to nothing, only the line of the nose and a closed and patient eye. Bonesy stopped and caressed her face.

“I spent many years with this one,” he said.

“She’s a rock, Bonesy.”

“It’s almost a good thing she can’t talk.”

We got down to the stone and put our faces to the water and drank like a Petit-Goâve cur drinks from the gutter after it rains. No shame in it.

When I sat back I looked out past the reef. She was there. She had come up while we drank, topsails to the mast, riding easy. Not coming on. Not firing. Just waiting, the way a barracuda waits, hung still in the water with everything in it pointed at you, patient because it knows it is faster than the thing it means to have.

“Two days dry and you forget there’s a King’s ship come to take you off in irons,” Bonesy said, watching us watch her.

Crumble looked at me. I looked at him, and both of us were in the same place at once. Twenty men against the two of us, three years back, in a cove much like this one. Only two remained to tell the tale.

The smile got away from me before I could stop it. Crumble’s mouth did the same.

“What,” Bonesy said. “What. I miss things, being dead. What is it.”

“High ground,” I said, and got up.

The high ground was a shelf of broken rock above the spring, screened by scrub, with a clean line out past the reef to the deep blue where she lay. We went up on our bellies the last of it and lay in the scrub and watched.

She was busy now. The easy riding was gone out of her, the sails hauled up and hanging slack against her masts as the men worked the falls, and the long shape of a boat swung out on her davits, slow against the bright water.

“Rowboat,” Crumble said.

“Aye.”

A crew at the oars, armed to the teeth. The reef that wrecked a hull was an open invitation to a boat drawing two feet.

That was the trouble, and it sat us both back on our heels. Three years gone, the pirate had brought his whole ship in after us, fat and sure as a pig walking itself to the butcher knife, run her into the cove on his own arrogance, and we had only to be there and let the ground do the rest. It had been easy. This was a different man.

Bonesy watched our faces and smiled, the way he smiled the last time me and Crumble ran aground.

“You weren’t expecting him to drop a rowboat in, were you,” he said.

Neither of us answered.

But a day from now there would be two more ships among us. After a day, a whole fleet. The hunt had gone out the moment the finger left the box, and every hour we sat here it drew its net a little tighter.

Crumble looked at me. I looked at him. The only way that ship came onto the rocks was if the man at her helm stopped thinking. You have to take it from him. You have to make him feel he has no choice. You have to take the choice from him the way you take it from a slave, leave him nothing in his hands but the one road you’ve left open, and watch him walk it thinking it was his own feet that chose.

### The Boat

The boat came in on the slack water with the oars dipping easy, ten men in her, the morning sun bright on the steel they’d brought to take three tired pirates off a rock. They came the way a dog comes for a thrown bone, sure of the having, eyes on nothing but the prize. They did not look up.

The cove was ringed in palms that lean out over the water, and a man in the crook of one is a shadow among shadows to an eye fixed on the beach, and I had been in mine since before they cleared the slot. I watched the boat come under me. The bald spot on the bow oar, the soft clean neck of the one in the stern sheets who wore his coat better than the rest. I let them slide in under the tree. I let the bow grate on the sand.

Then I came down on them out of the green.

I took the steersman first. I came down with the whole weight of the drop behind the upper sword and drove it in at the join of his neck and shoulder, down through the meat of him, and felt it grate and stop on bone deep in his chest. He folded over it. The blade was stuck and I put my boot on his shoulder and tore it free and the wound opened and gave up its dark wash down his front, and he went into the bilge thrashing, heels drumming the boards.

There is no art to it, in a boat. A boat is a pen. Men in a boat cannot run, cannot get round you, cannot bring their numbers to bear. They can only stand up into the thing killing them, one and one and one, and a man standing up in a rocking boat is a man handing you his belly. The next one came up with his cutlass half drawn and I took the hand off at the wrist before the steel cleared the scabbard, clean, the hand dropping with the hilt still in its grip, and he stood a moment staring at the stump pumping bright into the sun before I put the point under his jaw and up. He bit down on the blade as it went through. I felt his teeth shut on the steel.

The man behind him slipped in his fellow’s blood and went down to one knee in the bilge, and I brought the pommel of the lower sword across his temple and the bone gave with a wet crack and his eye started from the socket, and he pitched forward over the thwart and lay with his legs still in the dead steersman’s lap, twitching. Another came at me low with a bayonet and laid the back of my forearm open, and I let him have the arm and took his throat with the short blade, sawed it, felt the gristle of the windpipe part under the edge, and turned him loose to drown in the sound he was making.

Crumble came in over the bow with Balam’s blade. He took a man through the kidney from behind and ripped sideways, the way you gut a fish, and the man came apart at the middle and sat down in what came out of him, holding it, trying to gather it back. Another swung an oar at Crumble’s head and Crumble ducked it and opened his thigh to the bone and left him to fall, and he fell screaming, both hands jammed in the wound and the blood coming through his fingers in ropes.

“BEAUTIFUL,” Bonesy was howling from the rocks, up on his feet, clapping his hard hands together with a sound like stones knocked. “Oh, the LEFT one, Crumble, mind the left, there’s a — yes! YES! Down he goes! Did you see it!”

It was a butchering. I will set it down as that, because I have heard men call these things fights after, and they lie to make themselves easier to live with. We closed the boat from both ends toward the middle and killed them in it with the sun on the water, and one we did not finish clean, a young one, gut-cut and folded over the thwart still moving, and I took him by the collar and the belt and pitched him over the side into the lagoon to bleed out where he wouldn’t be underfoot, and he went down in a slow red bloom with the little fish already rising to him. The cove that had not heard a human sound in a hundred years heard all of it.

And we kept two.

That was the cold part, the part we’d settled on the rock with a look and no words. The soft-necked officer. And one big slow oarsman who’d dropped his blade and thrown his hands up, which on another morning might have bought him something. A dead man goes down and is a number. A live one works at the surface a long while, and the man with the glass to his eye cannot put it down. We bound them.

Bonesy came down off the rocks and crouched in front of the two of them, his skull a hand’s width from their faces, and looked them over the way you’d look over fish on a market slab.

“Hello, fellas.”

The rest we hauled up. Crumble took the shoulders and I took the feet and we carried the dead up off the sand to the high shelf one by one, seven of them, and set them in a row along the lip where the line was clean to the sea.

“Then let’s give him something to watch,” said Crumble.

We put them in the water. Not together. One, and a count, and the next, the way you pay out a line you mean a man to watch running. Each went off the lip and struck and went under and came up turning. The current along the face took them out slow. By the fourth there was a string of them standing away from the rock in good order, and a man a half mile off with a glass to his eye would have no trouble at all counting what was left of the boat he’d sent.

Then the two.

I went to the oarsman first. He was crying. I cut the back of both his knees and stood him up and put my boot to him, and he went off the lip with his arms going.

The officer did not cry. He watched me come the way a fish holds still when a heron’s shadow crosses the water above it, still because still is all that is left to it. I cut the back of both his knees and stood him up and put my boot to him.

He went in screaming. The coat filled with water and dragged him and he came up anyway, both hands beating at the surface, the ruined legs trailing dark behind him. He got his chin up once. Went under. Came up again with less in it. The coat took him on the third.

I heard him stop. The oarsman went on a while after that, out past the string of the dead, and then he did not.

I was already cleaning the blade.

### The Reef

She took the bait. We had made it ugly enough that the captain could not stay out in deep water and watch. His whole boat crew was in the water below the rock now, drifting in a red bloom, the small fish already rising to them and the bigger fish under those. He brought her in.

She came in fast, too fast for water like that. She found the deep channel and ran it a while, then lost it. Her keel went up onto the coral and stuck, and she hung there with her bow into the cove and her stern swinging slow on the swell, caught in the reef the way a fish hangs in a weir.

Bonesy had already gone back to the sloop. He got to the one gun and laid it while she was still settling, and when she came to rest he had her whole deck open below him. The first ball went in at the bow and walked the length of her, the rail going to splinters, men going down.

That was our door.

Every face aboard turned forward to the smoke and the wreck of the bow, and that was when Crumble and I came off the cliff.

The masthead stood level with the rock where we crouched, near enough to touch. Crumble went first. He caught the topmast shroud where it ran up to the cap and went down it hand under hand, quiet, his feet barely touching the rope. I came after. We came down the rigging into the smoke with the whole crew’s backs to us, four pistols apiece across our chests, and dropped onto the deck behind them.

No one had looked up. The bow was smoke and splinters, and every eye was on it.

A barracuda does its waiting before the rush. After that there is only the flash of it, and the water opening red behind.

We had waited long enough.

I set the first pistol to the nearest back and fired. The shot went through him and found the sailor in front of him, and both dropped into the smoke. Crumble fired twice beside me. Two more went down before the deck understood the trouble had come from behind.

Then they turned.

Badly. Some toward me, some toward Crumble, some back toward the bow as if the first trouble were still the only one. Smoke had them by the eyes. Fire had them by the backs. The reef had the ship by the belly. We were in the one place no one had guarded.

I drew both blades.

The first sailor came at me wide, cutlass high. I stepped inside the swing and put one blade under his arm, the other across the back of his knee as I passed. He folded wrong, one half of him still trying to stand and the other half finished with the matter.

The next had a pistol halfway out. I cut across his hand before he cleared it. The pistol dropped with three fingers still curled around it. He looked down, slow with shock, and I put the other blade under his jaw. His teeth shut on the steel. I felt it in the grip.

Crumble was on the other side of the smoke with Balam’s blade low in his hand. He does not fight pretty. He fights like a snare taking shut. A sailor swung an axe at him. Crumble stepped inside it, caught the wrist on the way down, and opened him from hip to ribs. The axe hit the boards after its owner did.

After that the deck went slick.

Blood does not stay where it falls on a ship. It runs with the tilt, finds seams, gets under your feet. The smoke rolled back from the bow in hot sheets, and the killing went half blind. Shapes came at us and we cut them down. One slipped before he reached me and rose on one knee, which was high enough. Another backed into the broken rail and put his hand into a nest of hot splinters. He looked at the hand. I opened him.

Someone called for the captain. Someone else called for God.

Neither came quick enough.

We pushed them toward the middle of the deck. Crumble from one side, me from the other. Not clean work. Deck work never is. Too close, too loud, too much footing to lose. A sailor would come out of smoke with his mouth open and be dead before he had shaped a word. Another would swing at where I had been and find Crumble instead. The ship bucked under us on the reef, each swell lifting her and setting her down harder, so the wounded slid with the blood and the dead shifted as if they had been called back to their stations.

A sailor came out of the smoke with a boarding pike. I caught the shaft between both blades, turned it, and drove my shoulder into him. He went back against the rail. I put one blade through his belly and pinned him there long enough to take the pike from his hands.

The smoke thinned.

The deck opened in front of me, and the captain was there.

He stood at the break of the quarterdeck with his coat off and his sleeves rolled back, a long Spanish blade low at his side. He had not rushed with the rest. He had watched them die and used the time well.

I hated him for that first.

He had the look of the boys from the clean rooms. Not soft now. The sea had taken some of that off him. But he had been soft once. Fed once. Named once. The kind of face that had never had to ask the world for room.

Then he lifted the blade, and I knew the school in his wrist.

He came in without hurry.

The first touch told me he was good. The second told me I could die there.

He did not spend himself. That was the worst of it. Anger gives you handles. Fear gives you gaps. Pride makes a fool reach too far. He gave me none of that. His point stayed in the middle of me, patient as a hook in deep water, and his feet took the deck a little at a time.

So I gave ground.

Past the mast. Past a body rolling under my heel. Past the smoke blowing hot from the bow. I had both blades moving and still he made me work harder than I liked. Every time I tried to turn him, he was there. Every time I tried to crowd him, his point was waiting in the road.

Crumble had his own work. I saw him only in pieces. The red sash through smoke. Balam’s blade rising and falling. A man going down with both hands at his throat. Then the captain took the sight from me again.

He pressed me back toward the broken rail.

The cove was below. Fire to my left. Smoke to my right. His blade in front, already measuring the place under my breastbone.

Then his mouth changed.

Not a smile. He knew better than to smile. Only the smallest easing at the corner, as if the deck had righted under him, as if the dirty sailor in hides had remembered where he belonged.

“Don Rodrigo taught me this,” he said, and touched my blade with his. “He gave us our instructions on the pair of you. Keep the Englishman in the sash on your left. Never let him in close.” His point came back to my breastbone. “You, he said, were the easy one. Push you and you forget the rules.”

My left hand had gone tight on the grip. I made it loose.

That was when I knew what I hated in him.

A proper master would have been proud of him. That was his trouble. Proper masters teach a man to beat another proper man. Same floors. Same rules. Same clean lies.

I let my left blade catch his.

Not hard. Not desperate. Just enough to look like a mistake. He felt the bind and took it, because it was the right thing to take. His strength came forward. His eye went with it. For one breath, all of him belonged to my left hand.

My right went under.

No flourish. No lesson. Just the short ugly thrust from rooms where nobody was trying to become a gentleman.

The blade went up beneath his jaw and stopped deep.

The captain froze.

His sword fell out of his hand and struck the deck once.

He looked surprised. Not hurt yet. Not afraid. Only surprised, as if the world had broken a promise it had been keeping with him all his life.

I held him there a moment.

I wanted him to know it had not been reef, or smoke, or luck.

Me.

Then I drew the blade out and let him down.

The fight went out of the ship with him. There were still bodies moving. Still breath catching wet where it should not. Still Crumble standing in the smoke with Balam’s blade dark to the grip. But the ship knew. You can feel it when a crew is finished. The noise changes. The living start listening for permission to stay that way.

Out past the rail, the cove had gone red. The fish were rising through it.

## Chapter 4, The Half Moon

### Open Water

The sharks were in the cove by the middle of the morning. They had come in through the gap on the flood and they did not leave. By the second hour we had stopped looking.

She was bilged forward and settled by the head, and her stern swung a little on every swell, working herself on the coral. She was going to come apart there whatever anybody did. So we took what would come off her, and we took it fast.

Powder in the dry cask. Shot for the one gun. Biscuit, a cask of salt beef gone over but not gone, cordage, canvas, a spare set of blocks, and a copper kettle Bonesy took a liking to that nobody argued about. Her glass, which was better than ours by a long way. Her charts, which were Spanish and careful and worth more than the rest of it together.

We took the water last and we took it slow. The spring under Ixchel came out of the rock the thickness of a man's finger and would not be hurried, and we stood a man at it in turn through the afternoon and into the dark, filling what we had.

“He'll know where we were,” Crumble said. He was on the stones with the chart across his knees and had not unrolled it yet.

“He'll know we took her with three.”

Crumble looked out at the wreck a while.

“Then the next one won't come in a boat.”

We went out through the gap on the morning tide with the cove behind us going about its business, and we did not look at that either.

It was eleven days west and south.

Eleven days is long enough that a boat stops being a boat and turns into a room three men cannot leave. Crumble had the tiller more than his share of it. Bonesy talked. I read the Spanish charts, which were better than anything I had ever had under my hand, and found on the third day that I had read the same soundings four times and could not have said what they were.

Crumble watched me start a fifth time.

“Balam said a man who hurries the hunt feeds the jaguar.”

“Balam never stood a watch.”

“No.” He kept his eyes on the luff. “He waited. His whole life, he waited.”

I did not say that he had waited his whole life and died with the work undone. I rolled the chart and put it away.

“Your pool,” Bonesy said.

“What about it.”

He let that sit a while.

“You never went in.”

“No.”

“Why.”

I did not give him one. He let it go, which he does about as often as a squall turns back.

What I had not said in the temple, and did not say on that deck, is that the pool had been mine for five years because I had not gone into it. I could take it out on a bad watch and look at it and put it back the way it was. I had been living off it the way a crew lives off a cask nobody has broached. Now Domingo was in a Spanish room somewhere with the rest of the course in his head, and the pool was the only road the three of us had.

Crumble did not ask for days. Then he asked.

“Jigsaw.”

“I know.”

“I've not said anything.”

“You've been going to since the cay.”

He shifted the tiller a hair to feel the wind.

“How sure are you of the water pulling.”

“Sure.”

“Sure the way a man is sure. Or sure the way you're sure of a current.”

“The second.”

He nodded and let it alone. That was the whole of what either of us said about the fact that everything we had left was a pool I had got out of at twenty and rowed away from.

Every dawn Crumble gave the east its day under his breath. On the sixth morning Bonesy gave it one too, a different one, and swore to it. Crumble let him be wrong.

On the ninth day Bonesy asked whether there was stone on it. I told him there was not, that I had seen none. He took it the way a man takes bad weather he has no say in, and did not ask again.

On the eleventh we raised it. It came up off the bow low and long and bent at the middle, green at the two ends and pale in the bight of it, the shape a man's thumb and finger make when he holds something small he does not want to crush.

I had it in my hands for the better part of an hour before Crumble said anything about the glass.

### The Pool

There is no landing on the windward side. The swell comes the whole width of the sea to get at it and it arrives the way it has been arriving since before anybody was here to watch. We brought her round into the lee and let the anchor go on sand in three fathoms and walked over.

It is not far. A half mile of low scrub that takes the skin off a man's forearms and does not apologise, and then the ground lifts into grey rock scoured bare, and the noise of the other side comes up over it before you see any of it.

Bonesy went up the last of the rock first because he does not tire and it costs him nothing to be told to wait. He stood on the top of it with the wind taking the brim of his hat flat back off his skull and looked down the far side a long moment.

“Oh,” he said.

The pool sits in the rock about a cable in from the break, in a shelf the sea only gets into on the worst days. It is thirty feet across and no shape that means anything. The rock around it is scoured pale and there is nothing living on it, no weed, no crab, nothing that wants to be there. The water is so clear that the first time a man looks he takes it for empty.

Then it moves.

They were in it. Six of them, and one over seven feet that I remembered and had half talked myself out of remembering. They went round the pool one behind the other on the current, slow, not working at it. The great plate scales of them went from grey to white to nothing as they turned through the light. A tarpon the length of a man does not look like a fish in three feet of water. It looks like something that has come up out of where it belongs to see what is being said about it.

Crumble came up beside me and stopped.

“You said six and seven feet.”

“I did.”

“You undersold it.”

I went down the rock and crouched at the edge where I had crouched before, and put my hand in the water to the wrist.

They came round. The big one came first and did not turn off. He went past my hand close enough that I felt the water he pushed. He went past it slow. The eye of him came round as he came and stayed on me the whole length of his body, and then the tail went by and the water closed and he was round the far side and coming again.

“He's had a look at you,” Bonesy said from up on the rock.

“He has.”

“What did he make of it.”

“He's not said.”

I kept my hand in the water. I had done exactly this at twenty and had got up afterwards and walked back over the scrub and rowed away, and I had spent five years telling the story to myself and to nobody else. Now Bonesy was up on the rock with his hat in his fist so the wind would not have it, and Crumble was standing over me with Balam's blade on his hip and a Spanish chart in his coat, and it was not mine any more.

Crumble crouched. He did not put his hand in. He watched the water for a while instead, and I let him do it, because there is nobody alive I would rather have read it after me.

“It's pulling,” he said.

“It is.”

“Pool this size in bare rock. No weed in it, no crab, and the water going round like that.” He put two fingers in at last, just the ends of them, and took them out. “There's no wind on it. There's no wave getting in. Nothing turns water in a hole in a rock for nothing.”

“No.”

He looked across at the low side of the shelf, where the rock goes down under the pool and the light stops.

“Where does it go.”

“I never found out,” I said.

### The Night Before

We went back over the scrub as the light went. It took the skin off us a second time. The sloop lay where we had left her in the lee, swinging to her cable over the sand. We could hear the sea on the windward rock from the deck. There is nowhere on that island you cannot hear it.

Crumble boiled the last of the salt beef with biscuit broken into it, in the copper kettle Bonesy had claimed off the wreck. Bonesy allowed it. A man shares his kettle.

“First light,” Crumble said.

“Slack water’s an hour after.”

“Then an hour after first light.” He scraped the kettle with his spoon. “Sleep.”

“I’ve the watch.”

“There’s nobody for forty leagues.”

“Then it’ll be a short one.”

He looked at me over the kettle. Then he put his back to the mast and shut his eyes. With Crumble that is the end of an argument, not the end of the thinking.

Bonesy went forward and sat on the bitts, his heels knocking against the planking. He took off his hat and turned it over in his hands, examining it as though he had found something new about it.

I sat aft with my arm over the tiller. The captain’s voice was still in my ear, the way he had said Rodrigo’s name with his point on my chest. Somewhere between one swell and the next I slept.

The fencing room was at the far end of the academy, beyond the chapel and the master’s offices. Its ceiling was high, with dark beams crossing above us, and tall windows let the afternoon sun fall in long strips across the boards. Portraits of Spanish admirals hung between the windows. At the far end, beneath the royal arms, the fencing master sat with his cane across his knees.

Twenty boys stood along the walls for the last bout of the term. Most wore their best coats and waistcoats, with clean stockings and buckled shoes. The richer boys had lace at their cuffs and buttons of silver or polished brass. Rodrigo wore a dark blue coat that fitted him perfectly. Mine had been let out at the shoulders twice, and the cuffs had begun to fray.

The swords stood in racks along the wall. They were iron, blunt on both edges, with a rounded button of metal at the tip to keep a thrust from running a boy through. They weighed what real blades weighed, and every year they broke somebody’s fingers.

A circle had been drawn in chalk across the middle of the floor.

I stood at its edge.

I had spent most of my life learning where I was allowed to stand. There were houses I could enter through the front door and houses where I was expected to wait at the back. There were boys whose fathers would see them commissioned regardless of how they performed, and boys like me, who were told that excellence might be enough.

The chalk was another line. But inside it, at least, I knew what I was worth.

Rodrigo came up beside me.

“My father has my commission,” he said quietly. “Who’s writing yours?”

I looked at him.

He stepped over the chalk and saluted the master. Then he turned and smiled at the boys along the wall.

I followed him in.

I was sixteen. I had beaten every boy in that room, Rodrigo included, and I knew I could beat him again.

Rodrigo was good. Strong, patient, and careful with his feet. He had an excellent guard and rarely reached farther than he needed to. When we practiced together, he was one of the few boys who could make me work for a touch.

We saluted.

The master lowered his hand.

Rodrigo came forward with a cut toward my shoulder. I caught it on the stronger part of my blade and answered toward his chest. He turned my point aside and pressed me back a step.

I let him come.

He struck again, this time low, and I moved my foot just beyond his reach. His blade passed in front of my knee. Before he could recover, I turned my wrist and brought my point beneath his arm.

“One to Jigsaw,” the master called.

Rodrigo lowered his sword and rolled the shoulder where I had struck him.

We returned to the edge.

“She came to supper on Sunday,” he said. “At our house.”

I knew who he meant.

Her family lived in the house with the blue shutters beside the cathedral steps. For a year I had walked past those shutters on my way home, sometimes slowing when I thought I saw movement behind them.

I looked at Rodrigo.

He was examining the tip of his sword as though he had said nothing worth remembering.

The master called us forward.

I attacked first. Rodrigo caught my blade and turned it aside. I struck again, harder, and he gave ground toward the chalk.

I thought I had him.

He let his guard open just enough to show me his shoulder. I lunged for it, and he stepped across my line, bringing his point against the outside of my thigh.

The blow stung through my breeches.

“One each,” the master said.

Rodrigo stepped back and saluted.

I could hear two boys whispering along the wall. I knew they had seen me fall for the opening. I also knew Rodrigo had left it deliberately.

He had scored fairly.

That did not make me feel any better.

We returned to the edge.

“She’s a fine girl,” he said.

I tightened my grip.

The master gave the signal.

This time I waited.

Rodrigo advanced, testing my guard with two quick cuts. I caught the first and stepped away from the second. He followed, keeping his point toward my chest, trying to draw me into another mistake.

I let him think it was working.

When he came forward again, I stepped into him instead of away. Our blades met near the hilts. I turned mine over his, pushed his point aside, and struck him cleanly across the chest.

“Two to Jigsaw.”

It was the best touch of the bout.

Rodrigo looked down at the place where I had struck him. He had not expected me to come inside his guard.

I smiled.

My heart was hammering, and a wonderful heat had spread through my chest. I could feel it in my arms, in my fingertips, even in the soles of my feet. The room seemed brighter, every sound sharper. The sting in my thigh was still there, but now I liked it. I liked the way it reminded me that I was in a fight.

I wanted another touch. Then another. I wanted to see that look on Rodrigo’s face again, the moment he realized I had beaten him.

I had spent years training for the satisfaction of winning. This was different. Winning gave me a rush that lasted only a moment, and already I could feel it slipping away. I wanted it back before it had even gone.

Rodrigo saw me smiling.

He did not smile back.

We walked back to the chalk.

“My mother asked about your father,” he said. “Where he came from. Before.”

I turned my head.

“Nobody at the table could say.”

My jaw tightened. I felt my teeth press together, the hard scrape of one against another.

He knew where my father had come from. Everyone at the academy knew enough to whisper about it. Rodrigo had heard the stories as often as I had, and he knew which parts of them I hated.

He stood opposite me in his fine coat, with his commission already promised and his father’s name behind him. He had never earned any of it. He had been born, and the world had made room.

I felt my pulse in my neck.

The master called us forward.

I went at Rodrigo hard.

He caught my first cut, then my second. I pressed him backward, striking high and low, trying to break through his guard by force. Each time our blades met, the shock traveled into my wrist.

He was near the edge.

I saw his shoulder open and went for it.

He had shown me the same opening before.

I knew that even as I moved.

He stepped aside and drove his point into my ribs.

The pain went through me like a knife. My breath stopped. I staggered sideways and nearly crossed the chalk before catching myself.

“Two each,” the master called.

I bent forward, one hand against my side.

For several moments I could not draw a proper breath. My ribs seemed to close around something sharp. When I finally managed to inhale, the pain came again.

I heard Rodrigo moving back toward the edge.

I straightened.

My heart was pounding so hard that I felt it in my throat. Heat had climbed from my chest into my neck and face. Sweat ran beneath my collar. My sword hand trembled, and I closed it harder around the grip.

The room had begun to sound strange. I could hear the scrape of Rodrigo’s shoes, the rustle of his coat, the air going in and out of my own nose. The boys along the walls seemed very far away.

I looked at Rodrigo.

He was watching me carefully.

I wanted to hit him.

Not score against him. Not prove I was the better swordsman.

I wanted to hurt him badly enough that he would remember it.

We returned to the edge for the last touch.

“Her father had a letter from mine this week,” Rodrigo said. “About her.”

He spoke softly. I almost missed the words.

Then I understood them.

The heat in my face seemed to spread behind my eyes. My jaw clenched until it hurt. I could feel my pulse in my fingertips, and for a moment I thought I might be sick.

I looked at Rodrigo’s mouth.

I imagined him sitting at her family’s table, speaking to her father as though he already belonged there. I imagined the two families discussing their future while mine was being decided in rooms I would never be invited into.

His father had secured him a commission. Now he was arranging the girl as well.

Rodrigo stood across from me with his sword raised, waiting.

I wanted him to say something else.

Anything.

The master lifted his hand.

I watched it fall.

Rodrigo gave ground as soon as I came forward. He expected me to rush him, and I did.

He kept his blade between us and retreated toward the edge, waiting for me to reach too far. I struck toward his head. He caught it. I struck toward his shoulder, and he turned that aside as well.

Then he showed me an opening at his chest.

I saw it.

I saw the trap behind it too.

For a moment I wanted to laugh. He thought he was about to catch me the same way he had before.

I wanted him to believe it. I wanted him to see me take the opening, to feel that little moment of triumph when he thought he had finally outsmarted me.

And then I wanted to take it away.

I lunged.

Rodrigo thrust as I came in. I turned my body just enough that his point passed beside my ribs, close enough to catch the cloth of my coat. I stepped through and drove my own point into the hollow of his throat.

His head snapped backward.

He staggered, coughing, and one hand went to his neck.

“Three to Jigsaw,” the master called. “The bout is finished.”

I heard him.

Rodrigo lowered his sword.

I stood looking at him.

My breath was coming hard through my nose. My ribs ached. My heart was beating so violently that I could feel it behind my eyes.

I had won.

But the feeling was already going.

The heat in my chest had begun to fade, and I hated it. Rodrigo was standing there with his commission waiting for him, his father’s name, and the girl whose family had invited him to supper.

I had beaten him in front of everyone.

Tomorrow he would still have everything.

Rodrigo straightened slowly and raised his blade to salute.

I stepped forward and struck him across the side of the head.

The iron caught him above the ear.

He went down hard, one knee striking the boards before the rest of him followed. His sword clattered beside him.

Nobody moved.

For a moment I stood over him with my sword still raised.

I could feel the rush returning.

Then the master was on his feet.

“Enough!”

His cane struck the floor.

I looked toward him.

The room came back around me. The boys along the walls, the portraits, the afternoon sun. Rodrigo was on one knee, holding the side of his head. Blood had begun to run between his fingers.

I lowered my sword.

The master crossed the room and took it from my hand.

“Outside,” he said.

I looked at Rodrigo once more.

He was staring at me with an expression I had never seen on his face before.

Not fear. Not quite.

Disbelief.

He had beaten me twice that afternoon, and I had beaten him three times. We both knew the result.

But I had struck him when he wasn’t defending himself.

And I had enjoyed it.

I walked out past the boys along the wall.

Nobody spoke.

By the following morning, I was no longer a student at the academy.

“Jigsaw.”

I opened my eyes.

It was grey. The island lay black against the eastern sky, and dew had gathered along the rail.

My hand was wrapped around the tiller.

I had been gripping it so tightly that my fingers hurt. I had to loosen them one at a time.

Crumble was crouched beside me, his hand on my shoulder.

“Light’s coming,” he said.

“I see it.”

He took his hand away.

For a moment I could still feel the iron in my fist.

My ribs no longer hurt, of course. The bruise had gone before I left the academy. But I could remember exactly where Rodrigo had struck me, and the strange pleasure that had followed the pain.

Ten years had passed.

I could still remember the sound of the sword against his arm.

Bonesy sat on the cabin top with his hat resting on his knees.

“You were talking,” he said.

“Was I?”

“Said a name.” He turned the hat once in his hands. “Rodrigo.”

Crumble went still beside me.

“Who’s Rodrigo?” Bonesy asked.

“Nobody.”

“Nobody’s got a name.”

I said nothing.

Bonesy watched me for a moment, then settled his hat on his head.

“All right.”

Crumble stood and went forward to prepare the line for the pool.

I remained beside the tiller.

I had spent years telling myself Rodrigo had brought it on himself. He had known exactly what to say, and he had kept saying it until I could bear no more.

I still believed that, some mornings.

But I had heard the master call the final touch. I had seen Rodrigo lower his sword. I had known the bout was over.

And I remembered how badly I had wanted to hit him again.

Crumble called for help with the line.

I got up and went forward.

The sea was still breaking against the windward rock. In an hour the pool would stop pulling, and we would have our chance to go down.

I took the line from Crumble and began laying it out.

My hands were steady now.

### Into the Pool

We crossed the ridge with the line coiled over Crumble’s shoulder and the sun not yet clear of the sea.

The pool was grey in that light. The tarpon were in it, circling slowly, nose to tail. Every few turns the big one rolled up to the surface and gulped air with a loud pop, the way tarpon do, and sank back into its place in the circle. They were slower now. The pull was going out of the water as the tide came to the top. I could see it on the surface, where the small ripples off the rock had stopped leaning one way.

“How long till it stops pulling?” Crumble said.

“Half an hour. Less.”

He put the coil down on the rock and found the end of it.

Bonesy took off his hat. He held it out to me, and I took it. Then he stood at the edge of the pool while Crumble passed the line twice around his ribs and made it fast.

“Three pulls, you come up,” Crumble said. “One pull, you’re all right.”

“And no pulls?”

“Then we haul.”

Bonesy looked down at the knot on his ribs. He looked at the water.

“Fish don’t bite, do they?”

“Not you,” I said. “Nothing to bite.”

“That’s a comfort.”

He stepped off the edge and went straight down.

He did not swim. He sank like a stone, upright, with his arms loose at his sides. When his feet found the bottom, he walked.

The tarpon opened around him. The big one turned off and went around the far side of the pool before coming back past Bonesy, close enough to touch, with its eye on him. Bonesy put out a hand to it. It did not stop.

He walked toward the low side, where the rock went down under the pool and the light stopped. Crumble paid out the line hand over hand. I watched Bonesy’s skull go into the shadow beneath the rock, then his shoulders, and then I could not see him at all.

The line kept going out.

Crumble let it run through his palms. He did not take his eyes off the water. We had brought twenty fathoms of line. When a little under half of it was gone, it stopped.

Nothing happened.

I stood with the hat in my hands and counted. A living man can hold his breath for perhaps a hundred counts, if he is good at it and not frightened. I got to two hundred. Crumble held the line across his palm, feeling for any movement.

Then it twitched once.

Crumble let out his breath.

It was a long while before the line came back. Crumble took it in slowly and coiled it at his feet. Bonesy’s skull came out of the shadow first, then the rest of him, walking up the slope of the bottom toward us.

He climbed out of the pool and sat on the rock, letting the water drain between his ribs.

“Well?” Crumble said.

“There’s a wall.” Bonesy shook his head, and water came out of his eye holes. “Down under the low side. Goes in a way, then a wall.”

“Rock?” I said.

“That’s the funny part. Looks like rock. Feels like rock, too, until you put your hand in it.”

“You put your hand into solid rock?”

“Didn’t know it was going to let me.”

Neither of us said anything.

“Then I put the rest of me through. Other side’s air. Dark. Dry stone. Steps.” He looked at his hand. “Never knew rock could do that.”

“How far?” Crumble said. “From where the light stops to the air?”

Bonesy thought about it.

“For you?” He looked at me. “Two breaths. If you don’t stop to look.”

The surface of the pool had gone flat. The tarpon were barely moving now, hanging in the water with their fins working slowly. It was as near slack as it was going to get.

I gave Bonesy his hat back.

I took off my coat and shirt and boots and set them on the rock. I took off the belt with my swords on it and laid it on top of the coat. My hands did all of this on their own, the way they do the small work on a deck. While they did it, my stomach went tight and then tighter, until it felt like a fist beneath my ribs.

Crumble passed me the end of the line.

I tied it around my middle. I had tied that knot ten thousand times. It took me two tries.

Crumble watched me do it and said nothing.

“When you reach the air, give us one pull,” he said. “Then untie yourself. I’ll follow the line down.”

I nodded.

I went down to the edge of the pool and stood where I had stood at twenty.

The water was clear all the way down. I could see the bottom, pale and bare, and the low side where it went under the rock and turned black.

I knew how cold the water would be. I could see how the bottom fell away beneath the rock, and how quickly the daylight disappeared. Five years had passed since I first stood here, and I could still remember kneeling at the edge, my hand beneath the surface, trying to convince myself to go farther.

My feet would not move.

I had stood before men with loaded pistols and felt less afraid than I did looking down into that pool.

There was nothing stopping me from turning around. I could walk back over the ridge, put my coat on, and tell Crumble we had come far enough. I had found reasons to leave before. I could find another.

Behind me Crumble had the line in both hands.

He waited.

Nobody told me to go.

Then I felt a hand on my shoulder.

I turned my head. Bonesy had come down beside me, his hat back on his skull. His fingers rested against my skin, light and hard.

He said nothing.

I looked past him at Crumble, standing with the line in his hands. He had followed me across the sea and over this island without once asking me to explain why it mattered so much. Bonesy had gone into the dark without being asked, as though it were the most natural thing in the world.

I was not going down there alone.

The tightness in my stomach eased a little. The water was still black beneath the rock, and I was still afraid of it, but there were two men behind me who had trusted me with worse.

Bonesy gave my shoulder a small pat and took his hand away.

I took one breath and let it out. I took another, as deep as it would go, and went in.

The cold took my chest first.

I heard Bonesy hit the water behind me.

I kicked downward, and the tarpon parted around me, their great silver sides flashing in the pale water. One passed so close that the water shoved against my chest, and I felt the force of its tail beating through the pool.

The big one turned and came with me for a few strokes, its eye level with mine. Then it went away into the pale water, and I was alone, swimming down the slope of the bottom toward the black.

The light went green, then grey.

I could see the rock above me now. It sloped down until it nearly met the bottom, leaving a narrow passage beneath it. I pulled myself forward along the stone, feeling the line trail behind me.

The daylight faded.

I kicked harder.

Then the passage ended.

There was rock in front of me. A wall of it, grey and pitted, filling the whole space from floor to roof. It looked exactly like every other piece of stone on that island.

I put out my hand.

My fingers went into the rock.

I stopped kicking.

I could see my forearm ending at the stone. There was no hole, no opening, no break in its surface. My hand was simply gone.

I pulled it back.

There it was again, whole and ordinary.

I put it through a second time.

I felt nothing at all. No scrape of stone, no pressure, not even a change in the water. My hand disappeared, then my wrist, then my forearm.

Every part of me wanted to pull back.

I had been afraid of the water. I had been afraid of the dark beneath the rock. I had not expected to find something worse than either.

My chest was beginning to ache.

I kicked forward and put my face into the wall.

For a moment there was nothing to see. Not the grey of the stone or the green of the water behind me. Only black.

I pushed farther.

My shoulders passed through, then my chest. I could feel the line dragging against my middle, but nothing touched my skin.

I kicked again.

There was still no light.

My lungs had begun to burn. I reached forward and felt nothing. I kicked harder, my fingers spread wide, searching for stone, for an edge, for anything I could push against.

My hand struck something solid.

I recoiled, then felt along it. Stone, rough beneath my fingers. I followed it upward and found the edge of a step.

I pulled myself forward.

My hand broke the surface into air.

I came up after it too fast, struck my head against stone above me, and went under again.

I found the step with my feet and pushed upward, keeping one hand over my head.

My face broke the surface.

I breathed.

It was black. The air was cold and smelled of dry stone. Somewhere close by, water was dripping.

I stood on a step with water up to my waist and breathed until the burning in my chest began to ease.

My heart was beating so hard I could hear it.

“Took your time,” Bonesy said, out of the dark.

I nearly lost my footing.

“Christ, Bonesy.”

“What?”

“Give a man some warning.”

“I was waiting for you.”

I could hear water dripping off him onto the stone.

“How long have you been here?”

“Not long.” He paused. “Long enough.”

I reached down and found the knot at my waist. My fingers were clumsy with cold, but I managed to work it loose.

I gave the line one hard pull.

Then I laid it across the step so Crumble could follow it through.

For a moment I stood still, listening.

The water moved softly against the stone. Behind me, somewhere beyond the wall, the pool was full of daylight and fish. Here there was nothing but darkness and the sound of dripping water.

“Can you see anything?” I asked.

“Steps,” Bonesy said. “Go up a way.”

“Anything else?”

“Door.”

“What’s beyond it?”

“Didn’t go.”

I turned toward his voice.

“Why not?”

“Thought we’d all go together.”

I said nothing.

From the water behind me came a faint disturbance.

Then a splash.

Crumble had reached the wall.

I listened to him moving through the passage. There was a scraping sound, then silence, and then a hand came up out of the water and caught the edge of the step beside me.

Crumble’s head broke the surface with a gasp.

He came up blowing like a whale, shook the water from his eyes, and reached blindly for something to hold.

I caught his arm.

He found the step with his feet and stood beside me, breathing hard. For a while neither of us spoke.

“Well,” he said at last.

“Well.”

“That’s not rock.”

“No.”

He took another breath.

“Didn’t much care for that.”

“Neither did I.”

Behind us, under the water, the passage we had come through glowed faintly green with the morning outside. In that light I could just make out the stone steps rising in front of us, cut square and even, and above them the black mouth of a doorway.

Crumble let go of my arm.

The steps were old. Their edges had been worn smooth in places, though I could not imagine who had walked them or how long it had been since anyone had come this way.

I looked back toward the green light beneath the water.

Five years I had known about that pool. Five years I had turned away from it.

And all that time there had been a doorway on the other side.

“Well,” Crumble said again.

I looked up at the steps.

“You first.”

“It’s your island.”

“And you’re the one who likes ruins.”

Bonesy had already started up.

### Come On Through

The steps went up eleven and stopped at the doorway. Bonesy was standing in it.

“There’s light,” he said.

There was, a little. Beyond the doorway a passage ran straight back into the rock, farther than I could see. Thin beams of daylight fell into it from holes cut high in the roof, one after another down its whole length. Dust hung in each beam and turned slowly. Roots had pushed down through cracks in the ceiling and hung there grey and dry, and the air was cold and smelled of bats and old stone.

The floor was paved with square stones. Along the top of both walls ran a band of carved faces, dozens of them, every mouth open. They were all looking down at the floor.

Crumble stood in the doorway beside me and looked at it for a long time.

“Don’t go in yet,” he said.

I stopped.

He crouched and ran his fingers along the edge of the first paving stone.

“What is it?”

“Look at the floor.”

I looked. The stones were old and worn, their edges dark with dirt. I could see nothing unusual.

“Some of them sit higher than the others,” he said. “And there’s a gap around that one.”

He pointed to a stone several paces into the passage.

I looked more closely. There was a narrow black line around its edges, too even to have been left by chance.

Crumble stood and examined the walls.

“And those.”

Low down, at about the height of a man’s knee and again at his chest, there were holes in the stone. Small and round and evenly spaced, running the length of the passage on both sides, as far as the light went.

“Those aren’t for letting air in,” he said.

I looked from the holes to the loose stone.

“You think they’re connected?”

“I think somebody went to a great deal of trouble to make sure we don’t get to the other end.”

I had heard the stories. Every sailor has. A man goes into a king’s tomb after gold and the floor drops away under him. Or the walls shoot arrows, or the roof comes down. I had always taken them for stories men tell to make a dull voyage shorter.

I looked down the passage again.

We were both in our breeches and nothing else, barefoot and dripping. Our swords were on the rock outside with our coats. I had never felt so naked in my life.

“Right,” Crumble said quietly. “We go slow. Test every stone before we put weight on it. Keep to the middle.”

“Agreed.”

“Bonesy, you stay behind us.”

Bonesy was already twenty paces down the passage.

“Bonesy.”

“What?” He turned round in the circle of light. “It’s just a hall.”

The stone under his left foot sank half an inch with a sound like a cellar door closing.

Something hissed out of the wall.

I threw myself back against Crumble, and the two of us went down in the doorway in a heap. When I looked up, Bonesy was standing exactly where he had been. Six darts had gone through him. Two had passed between his ribs, one through the hole where his nose had been, and three through nothing at all. They lay scattered on the stones on the far side of him, black and long as a man’s hand.

Bonesy looked down at himself.

“Huh,” he said.

He bent and picked one up and smelled the point.

“Don’t,” Crumble said.

“It smells like a dead dog.”

“That’s the poison. Put it down.”

He put it down. He wiped his fingers on his ribs, which did nothing, and looked at the floor around his feet.

“That one’s loose,” he said, pointing at the stone he was standing on. “Mind that one.”

Crumble and I got up off the floor. Neither of us said anything for a while.

“You stay where you are,” I said.

“I’m all right where I am.”

“Don’t move.”

Bonesy looked at us.

“Why?”

“Because you nearly got us killed.”

“But I didn’t.”

He turned and went on down the passage, looking up at the faces carved along the walls.

“Bonesy,” Crumble called.

He kept walking.

We followed him slowly, testing each stone before putting our weight on it. Bonesy was already under the next shaft of light, examining the ceiling.

“Funny faces,” he said.

“Watch your feet,” I told him.

“I am.”

He wasn’t.

Halfway down the passage there was a wide stone set into the floor, carved with a face. It was a jaguar, I think, though it might have been a man wearing one. Bonesy stopped and looked down at it.

“Now that’s a face,” he said.

“Don’t step on it,” Crumble said.

“I wasn’t going to. She’s handsome.”

He crouched to look more closely, then stood and stepped carefully over the carving.

His foot came down on the plain stone beyond.

Something cracked above him.

I looked up.

A great block of stone dropped out of the ceiling and struck him squarely.

The sound was enormous.

Dust rolled down the passage in a grey cloud. I threw my arm over my face and stumbled backward, coughing, my ears ringing.

When the dust cleared, the block lay broken into three pieces across the floor.

Bonesy was underneath.

For a moment neither of us moved.

Then one of the pieces shifted.

A hand came out from beneath it and felt around on the stones. It found Bonesy’s hat, which had been flattened beneath the edge of the block.

The hand lifted it into the light.

“Oh, look at that,” Bonesy said.

He pushed the stones off himself one at a time and sat up.

His skull had come loose on his neck and was facing slightly the wrong way. He put both hands against it and turned it back with a grinding sound, the way you work a stuck stopper loose.

Then he looked at the hat.

“I’ve had this hat a month.”

“Are you hurt?” Crumble asked.

Bonesy looked at him.

Crumble closed his mouth.

Bonesy punched the crown of the hat back out from the inside. It came up lopsided, with a crease across the front. He put it on, pulled the brim straight, and looked at us.

“How’s that?”

“Looks all right,” I said.

“It doesn’t look all right. It looks sat on.”

He got to his feet and brushed the dust from his knees.

“All my life I wanted a hat. Now I’ve got one, and a dead priest drops a roof on it.”

He started walking again.

We followed him past the broken block. I stepped exactly where he had stepped. I did not look at the face on the floor.

The passage ended at a pit.

It ran from wall to wall and was two fathoms across, too far to jump from where we stood. On the far side the passage continued to a low doorway.

I went to the edge on my knees and looked down.

The pit was three fathoms deep and full of stakes. Long hardwood poles, blackened in a fire and sharpened at the top, stood so close together that a man could scarcely have put his hand between them. Their points came up to within a man’s height of the edge.

Down among the poles lay what was left of somebody who had tried to cross.

Crumble knelt beside me and looked down for a long time.

“Well,” he said. “That’s that.”

Bonesy walked up beside him, looked down, and stepped off the edge.

I grabbed for him and missed.

He dropped onto the points with both feet. He wobbled, flung his arms out, and found his balance. He weighed no more than a bundle of dry sticks, and the points held him.

He looked back up at us.

“Come on through,” he said.

“We’re not made like you,” I said.

“No.” He looked down at the stakes beneath his feet. “Suppose not.”

He started across, stepping from one point to the next with his arms out.

Halfway over he stopped.

“Oh,” he said. “Here’s a funny one.”

“What?”

He knocked his heel against the post beneath his foot.

It made a dull sound, nothing like the dry knock of wood.

“Stone,” he said.

He bent and ran his hand over it.

“Painted black like the rest. And flat on top.”

He looked around him.

“There’s another. And another.”

Crumble leaned out over the edge.

“Where?”

Bonesy pointed.

Once I knew what to look for, I could see them. Among the hundreds of sharpened wooden stakes stood a few dozen posts of stone, carved and painted to look like the rest. Their tops had been cut flat, each about the width of a plate.

They made a crooked path across the pit.

The first stood close beneath our side, near enough to reach with a foot while holding the edge. The others were spaced a careful step apart, with the occasional longer gap where a man would have to stretch.

In the middle the path turned back on itself. A man who guessed and went straight would step off it onto the points.

“The priests had to get in too,” Crumble said slowly.

“Course they did,” Bonesy said. “Can’t carry a book across a pit on a rope.”

He came back toward us, walking on the sharpened stakes until he reached the first stone post.

“I’ll go first,” I said.

Bonesy stood beside the post.

“I’ll show you which. Step where I point. You fall, I’ll catch you.”

“You weigh nothing. You can’t catch me.”

“I can try.”

I sat on the edge with my legs over the drop.

The first post stood just below my feet. I turned onto my stomach, held the edge with both hands, and lowered myself until one foot found the flat stone.

I put my weight on it slowly.

It held.

I brought my other foot down beside it and straightened.

The post was scarcely wider than my two feet together. Around it the sharpened stakes stood close enough to brush my legs.

“Good,” Bonesy said. He pointed. “That one next.”

The next post was a short step away.

I reached out with one foot and felt for the flat top. When I found it, I shifted my weight across and brought the other foot after it.

“Now that one.”

I went like that, one stone at a time.

Bonesy walked beside me on the points, showing me each post before I moved. I kept my eyes on his hand and the next patch of flat stone. The wood below me was black and sharp, and I could see the floor of the pit through the gaps.

At the fourth post I made the mistake of looking down.

There was a skull wedged between two stakes beneath me. Its jaw hung open, and one of the poles had gone through the hollow where its chest had been.

I looked away.

“Keep looking at me,” Bonesy said.

I did.

In the middle the path turned back toward the side we had come from. The next stone was behind me and to my left. There was no room to turn both feet together.

I shifted my weight onto one foot and began to turn.

My knee trembled.

I stopped.

The stone beneath me was dry, but the sole of my foot was still wet from the pool. I could feel it slipping against the smooth surface.

“Take your time,” Bonesy said.

I bent my knee a little and turned the rest of the way.

Then I reached back for the next stone.

My foot found it.

I moved my weight across.

The path turned again and carried us toward the far side. I could see the doorway now, low and dark beyond the edge of the pit.

The last post stood close enough to the far wall that I could reach the floor with both hands.

I leaned forward, caught the edge, and hauled myself up.

I sat down on the stone floor.

My legs were shaking.

Crumble came across behind me the same way, slower, with Bonesy beside him pointing out each stone. His mouth was set in a line, and he did not look down.

At the turn he stopped for so long that I thought he might not move again.

Bonesy stood beside him, waiting.

Then Crumble put out his foot, found the next stone, and went on.

When he finally climbed onto the floor beside me, he sat down too.

Bonesy climbed out of the pit after him and stood over us.

“See?” he said. “Nothing to it.”

Crumble looked at me.

I looked at Crumble.

We were sitting on the floor of a dead priest’s passage in our breeches, soaked through and grey with dust from the ceiling. We had walked the whole length of it on our toes, holding our breath. Bonesy had walked it like a man going down to the harbor for a drink.

Crumble laughed first.

It came up out of him quietly and then not quietly at all. Then I was laughing too, both of us bent over with our backs against the wall, until my ribs ached worse than when Rodrigo had hit them.

Bonesy watched us with his head on one side.

“What?” he said. “What is it?”

Neither of us could tell him.

He waited until we were done. Then he turned and looked at the low doorway at the end of the passage.

“There’s another room,” he said. “Dry. There’s a jar.”

We stopped laughing.

### The Leaf

The doorway at the end of the passage was so low that we had to go through it bent double.

On the other side the air changed. It was dry, drier than anything I had breathed since the storm, and warmer. A single shaft came down through the roof and laid a square of daylight on the floor, and in the square stood a table cut from one block of stone.

On the table was the jar.

It was the size of a man’s head, round and fat, made of red clay gone dark with age. Its mouth had been stopped with a clay lid and sealed all round with something black and hard.

Nothing else in the room. No gold, no bones, no carvings on the walls. Just the table and the jar and the light coming down on them.

I walked around the table, looking for something I might have missed. I even looked beneath it.

There was nothing.

I stood up and rubbed both hands over my face. My shoulders ached from the swim, and the soles of my feet were raw from the passage. I had spent five years thinking about what might be hidden beneath that pool. Now I was standing in front of a clay pot.

“All that,” I said.

Crumble had gone straight to the jar.

“All what?”

“The passage. The pit. Nearly getting ourselves killed.”

I looked at the jar.

“For a pot?”

“We haven’t opened it.”

“Unless they’ve folded a chest of gold inside, I don’t much like our chances.”

Bonesy came up beside me and looked at the jar.

“It’s a nice pot.”

“You can have it.”

“Already got Beatriz’s sister.”

I looked at him.

“Beatriz’s sister?”

“The lady in the floor.”

“The carved face?”

“She was handsome.”

“You nearly had your skull crushed standing beside her.”

He touched the crease in his hat.

“Wasn’t my skull I was worried about.”

Crumble put his hands on either side of the jar.

“Will you two be quiet?”

Bonesy looked at me.

“He’s cross.”

“So am I.”

The room was roughly round. On the far side from the doorway there was a well in the floor, a little wider than my shoulders, with a low stone lip around it. It was dark inside. I could hear water somewhere down in it, faint, the way you hear the sea in a shell.

I made myself look away from it.

Crumble stood with his palms flat against the clay, as if he could feel something through it.

“Balam died looking for this,” he said.

Nobody answered him.

He took the knife from his belt and worked the point round the black seal. It was wax, old and hard as horn. It came away in flakes. When he had gone all the way round he put the knife down and lifted the lid with both hands.

There was no smell. I had expected rot, or damp. There was nothing but the dry smell of the room.

He reached in and brought out a packet of oiled cloth, folded small, and laid it on the table in the light. He opened the cloth one fold at a time. Inside was a single leaf, folded twice, the colour of old bone.

He opened it.

It was bark, beaten thin and coated with white plaster so the paint would take. It was a little longer than my forearm. The colours had barely faded: red and black, a blue like the shallows over sand, a yellow like dried grass. There were lines across it and small figures, and rows of black dots and bars. Down one side ran a long wavering band of blue.

I looked at it for some time.

“That it?”

Crumble ignored me.

“No gold,” I said.

“Jigsaw.”

“Not even a little.”

“Would you let me look?”

I leaned against the table and folded my arms.

Bonesy peered over Crumble’s shoulder.

“Could sell the pot.”

“Thank you, Bonesy.”

“Welcome.”

“It’s a map,” Crumble said.

I straightened.

“Of what?”

“I don’t know yet.” He leaned over it. “It’s laid out the old way. Balam showed me. They didn’t put north at the top the way we do. They put east at the top, where the sun comes up, and they painted that edge red.”

He touched the red border along one end.

“These are roads.” His finger followed a line of tiny black footprints, each one no bigger than a grain of rice. “Footprints mean somebody walked it, or paddled it. And this is how they counted.”

He touched a row of dots and a black bar beside them.

“A dot is one. A bar is five.”

I looked at the blue band. It was crooked, full of small bays and points. Islands sat off it like crumbs.

“That’s a coast,” I said.

Crumble glanced at me.

“I can see that.”

“Which one?”

“I haven’t worked that out.”

I bent over the leaf.

The blue line turned sharply in two places, with a long shallow bend between them. There were three small islands near one end and a larger one farther along.

“I’ve sailed these waters long enough to know the coast,” I said. “That isn’t it.”

“It’s an old map. Shorelines change.”

“Not that much.”

“You’re looking at it the wrong way.”

“You said east was at the top.”

“Yes.”

“Then why would they draw the coast like that?”

“Because they weren’t making it for you.”

I looked at him.

He had bent over the leaf again.

“Balam could read these things,” he said. “I only know what he taught me.”

I looked back at the blue band.

Something about it troubled me. Not the shape of the coast itself, but the way the islands sat against it.

East at the top.

I reached out and turned the leaf on the table, so that the red edge faced away from me.

“Careful,” Crumble said.

The coast came round.

I stopped moving the leaf.

The long bend of the shore running south. The bight where the reef stands off. The three small cays in a line where I had watered once in a dry year.

I knew that coast.

A little out from the shore was a half moon, painted in yellow with a tiny blue spot in the middle of it.

The pool.

“Here,” I said.

I put my finger beside the half moon.

“That’s this island. And there’s the pool.”

Crumble leaned over the table.

“No.”

“Look at the reef.”

He followed my finger.

“And those three cays. They’re right where they ought to be.”

He said nothing.

“That’s the island we’re standing on. The pool’s marked in blue.”

Crumble bent closer. He looked from the half moon to the coast and back again.

Then he let out a breath.

“Yes,” he said.

The footprints began at the half moon. They went out across the blue in a line, north along the coast, and stopped beside a long island lying off the shore. Next to the line, in black, there was a bar and two dots.

“Seven,” Crumble said.

“Seven days?”

“By canoe. That’s how they’d have gone.”

I looked at the long island.

I knew it. Every sailor on that coast knows it, lying low and green off the shore with a channel inside it that the Spanish use when the wind is foul.

“Cozumel,” I said.

“Cuzamil,” Crumble said, the old way. “The island of swallows.”

He said it quietly, as if somebody might overhear.

Beside the island, at the end of the footprints, there was a sign painted in black and red. It was not a footprint and it was not a number. It looked like a face in profile with curls coming out of its open mouth. They might have been smoke, or breath, or wind.

Crumble looked at it for a long time.

“What does it say?” I asked.

“I don’t know.”

He said it flat.

He bent over the leaf until his nose was a finger’s width from it. Then he straightened and stood with his hands on the edge of the table.

“Balam taught me the counts,” he said. “He taught me the roads and the numbers and a few hundred of the old signs. Not that one.”

“Somebody can read it.”

“Somebody can.”

He did not go on.

Bonesy looked at him, then at me.

“Who?”

Crumble took a long breath.

“His daughter.”

I knew who he meant. I had seen her once, years ago. She was over his shoulder as he came out of the smoke on a burning deck. I had never heard him speak of her since.

“The girl from the raid,” I said.

“She’s not a girl now.”

He began folding the oiled cloth back over the leaf, slowly, a corner at a time.

“Balam taught her things he never taught me. If anyone can read that sign, she can.”

“Where is she?”

“On the mainland. Up a river, a long way past where any Spanish boat can go.”

“Then we ought to find her.”

Crumble stopped folding.

“No.”

I looked at him.

“No?”

“Not with Cárdenas looking for us.”

“He doesn’t know where we’re going.”

“He knows what we look like. He knows we’ve taken something from him. And if Domingo’s still alive, he may know more than that.”

I glanced toward the doorway.

“Then all the more reason to get somewhere he can’t follow.”

“You think because a Spanish ship can’t get up a river, the Spanish can’t get there?”

“I think we’re running short of places to hide.”

“And I think you’re forgetting what happens to the people who hide us.”

He folded the last corner over the leaf.

I said nothing.

“Her people have kept themselves alive by staying away from men like Cárdenas,” he said. “I’m not leading him to their door.”

“We don’t have to lead him anywhere. We can lose him before we go inland.”

“And how do you propose we do that?”

I had no answer ready.

I looked at the jar.

A few hours ago I had been certain there would be gold in it. Enough to buy a ship, perhaps, and men to sail her. Enough to stop running from one harbour to the next with the Spanish at our backs.

Instead we had a piece of painted bark and another place to go where we might not be welcome.

“Then what was the point of finding it?” I asked.

Crumble looked at me.

“Balam died for that leaf.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

I felt my jaw tighten.

I shoved off the table so hard the edge scraped my hip. I turned away from him and stood looking at the empty walls.

“I went through the same damned passage you did.”

For a moment I thought he would answer.

Instead he looked down at the folded packet.

I could hear the faint movement of water in the well. My hands were still trembling from the crossing, and I pressed them against my thighs to stop it.

Bonesy had been standing quietly beside the table.

“Well,” he said, “you did get to see me die twice.”

I looked at him.

“You didn’t die.”

“Then you got a very good bargain.”

Crumble made a small sound through his nose, though he kept his eyes on the leaf.

I wanted to be angry with Bonesy too, but the sight of his ruined hat made it difficult.

Crumble laid the packet back in the jar and pressed the lid into place.

“We’ll get away from Cárdenas first,” he said. “Then we’ll see.”

He took the end of the line from round his middle and bound the lid down, round and round, tight.

I watched him work.

“If she’ll have us,” he added.

Behind me, something changed.

It was a small sound.

I turned round.

The water in the well had begun to move. It was drawing back down into the dark, slowly at first and then faster. It made a soft sucking noise, like a man breathing in through his teeth.

Cold air came up out of the hole and touched my face.

The tide had turned.

I went to the edge of the well and looked down.

The water was going down and away from me into the rock, the way it went from the pool, only here I could see it go. It went down farther than the light reached. It did not stop.

Somewhere below me it was running on into the dark, the same as it had the day I knelt by the pool at twenty. It was running to a place I had never seen.

I stood at the lip and looked down for a long while.

“Jigsaw,” Crumble said.

I did not turn round.

“The pull’s coming back. If we don’t go now, we don’t get out through the wall.”

“I know.”

Bonesy came and stood beside me.

He looked down into the well too, with his ruined hat on the back of his skull.

He did not say anything.

After a while he put his hand on my arm, the way he had on the rock.

I looked down into the dark one more time.

Then I stepped back from the edge and went with them.


# Part two: the story summary

## Book One: The Story

2026-10-16 · Aydin

Three buccaneers, Jigsaw, Crumble and Bonesy, learn about an old Maya book that was broken into pieces on purpose. The pieces lead from one hidden place to the next, and gold is said to be buried with each one. They pay for the hunt with money from a robbery. Then they discover that the Spanish officer they robbed, Don Mateo de Cárdenas, is hunting the same book. He wants to use it to wipe magic out of the Caribbean, even though that would kill every person whose life depends on magic, including their friend Bonesy.

Chapters 1 to 3 and the first two scenes of chapter 4 are drafted. Everything after that is a proposed outline. Each proposed scene explains what happens, why the characters do it, and what changes because of it. Names marked suggested are placeholders.

### The outer story

The adventure should work on its own, even for a reader who never thinks about the themes.

1. **A failed robbery.** The three are hired to steal a saint's finger bone from a Spanish fort near Veracruz. Jigsaw sees a dangerous storm coming and calls off the search, so they leave with nothing.
2. **Two surprises in the temple.** While they shelter from the storm in a ruined Maya temple, Bonesy reveals that he stole the finger bone after all. He also remembers an old Maya book that priests broke apart and hid. Crumble realizes it is the codex his dead teacher, Balam, was searching for.
3. **The hunt is paid for.** They sell the finger bone and use the money to equip their boat. The first clue points to a tidal pool Jigsaw found years ago.
4. **A race.** The fort they robbed belonged to Cárdenas. He learns about the codex and starts hunting it too.
5. **The truth.** They learn what the codex really does, and what it would do to Bonesy and to other people changed by magic.
6. **Defeat.** Cárdenas captures them, breaks them, and gets what he needs from them. They give up.
7. **The choice.** Bonesy convinces them to go back. In Florida they stop Cárdenas and break the codex apart again so nobody can use it.

### The heart of the book

Jigsaw and Crumble both believe, in different ways, that freedom means owing nothing to anyone. By the end they learn that choosing people is not the opposite of freedom. It is what makes freedom worth having.

- **Jigsaw** wants proof that he mattered, and that the world was wrong to reject him. That is why he must be the best sailor, the best fighter, and the man who wins. He keeps trying to prove his value to a world he says he does not care about. By the end he understands that he gets to decide what is worth something. His father understood this before he did.
- **Crumble** believes love and respect have to be earned by becoming important. He left Anne because he thought he was not good enough for her yet. Then he did the same thing with Balam, turning his teacher into an impossible standard. By the end he understands that loving Balam does not mean he has to become Balam.
- **Amara** knows what everyone around her needs, but struggles to say what she wants for herself. By the end she makes a choice because she wants it.
- **Bonesy** already understands what the others are learning. He is dead, changed by magic, crude, curious, and completely comfortable being himself.
- **Cárdenas** is the opposite. A loss he could not control made him need to control everything.

The book shows these changes through what people do. Nobody stands up and explains them.

### Start here: what a first reader needs to know

#### The plot

**What was the failed job?** Just before the book begins, the three were hired to steal a saint's finger bone from a Spanish fort near Veracruz. Jigsaw saw a major storm coming and called off the search, so they left believing the job had failed. Two days later, while they shelter from that storm in a ruined Maya temple, Bonesy reveals that he stole the finger anyway.

**Why is the finger worth so much?** Because it really works. Magic is real in this book, and so is this relic. Suggested: the bone never decays, and pressed to a wound it stops the bleeding. The fort it came from belongs to Cárdenas, and the finger was part of his collection, locked away with a label in his handwriting. That is why a collector will pay a fortune for it, and why Cárdenas comes after the thieves so hard and so fast. The hire stays unnamed for now.

**Are they mercenaries?** Yes. They are buccaneers. They take jobs for money, raid Spanish ships and answer to nobody. They sell the finger in chapter 2 and use the money to equip their boat for the hunt.

**What is the codex?** An old Maya book. Years ago Bonesy sat on the cellar barrels of a group of priests and heard them talk about it. The priests broke it into pieces on purpose and hid the pieces so that each one points to the next. The young priests said gold was buried with every piece. The old priests lowered their voices when they talked about what the whole book does, and Bonesy caught only one word: before. Crumble recognizes it as the codex his teacher Balam was hunting when he died. What the book really does stays unknown until chapter 7.

**Why are they chasing it?** At first for simple reasons. There is gold along the route, and the finger money pays for the trip. Each man also has a private reason. Crumble wants to finish Balam's work. Jigsaw wants the gold, and the first clue points to a pool from his own past. Bonesy goes wherever his friends go. Then they learn that Cárdenas is hunting the same book, and it becomes a race. Once they learn what he wants to do with it, the goal changes from finding it first to making sure nobody can use it.

#### The world

**What kind of world is it?** The real Caribbean of 1670, except that magic is real. It is magical realism, not conventional fantasy. There are no wizards, no schools of magic and no spells. Magic lives in old objects, old places and people whose bodies it has changed.

**How common is magic?** Rare. To most people in the Caribbean, magic is still more story than fact: sailors' tales, saints' relics, old curses talked about in taverns. People do see certain pieces of it now and then, like a cursed skull at a card table or something odd hauled up in a net, but it is not part of daily life, and most of what gets talked about is never seen at all. Real magic survives mainly in isolated pockets, especially native communities that kept their old traditions and knowledge. Some magical objects have spread through trade, piracy, conquest and theft.

**Is Tortuga a magic place?** No. Tortuga is a pirate stronghold that accepts almost anyone. Pirates, fugitives, native sailors, unusual people, stolen sacred objects and people changed by magic all end up there. So more magic has piled up there than in most places. It is an accident, not a property of the island. That is why chapter 2 can show a skull staked at cards and a mermaid behind a curtain.

**Where is magic strongest?** In the hidden Maya community where Amara lives. It has stayed isolated, so magic there is much stronger and more common than anywhere else in the book.

**What does Cárdenas want?** To wipe magic out of the Caribbean. When he was young, magic killed his sister and nobody could explain or stop it. He decided that anything nobody can control should not exist. The codex, used correctly, restores everything magic has changed to what it was before. That sounds harmless until you think it through. Bonesy has been dead for centuries, so restored he would be old bones. Many people in Amara's community would die or be unmade. Cárdenas understands this and accepts the deaths.

#### The people

**Who is Balam?** Crumble's teacher, and close to a father to him. A Maya fighter and keeper of old knowledge, dead about sixteen months when the book begins. Crumble trained under him for about three years and learned fighting, stealth, the Maya language and history, and the Maya day count, which later matters to the codex. Balam died hunting the codex, so Crumble feels he owes him the finish. Years before, Balam also taught a young Spanish officer named Cárdenas. He came to regret it.

**Who is Cárdenas?** Don Mateo de Cárdenas, the villain. A Spanish naval officer in his early forties. He is intelligent, disciplined and very good at his job. He hates magic, yet he uses it all the time, but only magic he has studied and learned to control. The fort they rob at the start is his. When he has an important enemy he would rather break the man than kill him, but he kills when he needs to.

**Are Jigsaw and Crumble brothers?** Not by blood. "Brothers" means brothers in the old buccaneer sense. They met on Tortuga when Jigsaw was seventeen and Crumble sixteen, sailed together for about three years, spent about three years apart, and came back together. By the start of the book they are family.

**What school did Jigsaw attend?** A good Spanish colonial school for the sons of families with property, the kind that leads into the navy. He was the most gifted student there and learned formal fencing. But his father had hidden the family's Barbary roots, and Jigsaw never fully belonged. His classmate Rodrigo had a better family and got the commission and the life Jigsaw thought he had earned.

**Who does Jigsaw hate?** Spanish officers, wealthy men and men of rank, because they stand for the system that rejected him. He often takes it out on Spaniards who had nothing to do with what happened to him. The story knows this is wrong.

**Who is Anne?** The girl Crumble loved in England. At sixteen he left her and went to sea, because he thought he had to become somebody important before he deserved her. When he came home years later she was happily married to an ordinary man and had a child. Nobody betrayed him. She simply kept living after he chose to leave.

**Why are the two men so different?** Jigsaw was rejected and answers it with anger and fierce independence. Crumble left the people he loved and answers it with loyalty that goes too far: to Balam, to his friends and to debts he thinks he owes. Crumble is English by birth and Maya only by training. His childhood comes out in pieces, and that is enough.

### The world and the cast

The Caribbean, 1670. The buccaneers are at the height of their power. In July, England signs the Treaty of Madrid and promises Spain it will put them down. The safe places where men like Jigsaw and Crumble could disappear are starting to close.

**A note on names.** The book still needs an in-world word for people whose bodies magic has changed. "Cursed," "strange" and "different" all give the wrong tone. Until a word is chosen, this outline says "people changed by magic."

**The codex follows old rules.** It does not care about friendship, love or destiny. The crew can use it, or stop it, only because they have become people who trust each other enough to do work that needs several people acting at once.

- **Jigsaw, 25, the narrator.** His father was a Barbary corsair who gave up the sea and made a deal with Spain so his son could have a safer life. Jigsaw went to a good Spanish colonial school and was its most gifted student, but he never belonged. When he was seventeen his parents caught malaria. The family had spent most of its money on his schooling and could not pay for the treatment that might have saved them. Both died. He walked away from the Spanish navy and became a buccaneer. He hates Spanish officers, wealthy men and men of rank, and often takes it out on Spaniards who never harmed him. Underneath, he wants proof that he mattered and that the world was wrong to reject him. That is why he has to be the best sailor and the best fighter, why money and reputation matter, why Rodrigo bothers him and why losing enrages him. His arc: he learns that he gets to decide what is worth something. His father understood that first.
- **Crumble, 24.** An ordinary English boy who loved a girl named Anne. At sixteen he thought he was not good enough for her yet, so he left for the Caribbean to make money and a name, meaning to come back as somebody. He met Jigsaw on Tortuga. On a raid he saved Amara, and she sent him to her father, Balam, who trained him for about three years. Years after leaving England he went home and found Anne happily married to an ordinary man, with a child. She never needed him to be extraordinary. He then did the same thing with Balam, turning him into an impossible standard he has to live up to. He believes Balam held back part of his teaching because he did not trust him. His arc: loving Balam does not mean becoming Balam.
- **Old Bonesy.** A Maya peasant, dead for centuries and kept walking by magic. He is crude, curious, funny and happy. He has no fear because he is already dead, which makes him hard to threaten. The hat the brothers buy him is the first gift of his life. He does not care much about the codex. He cares about them. If the codex is used, he becomes a pile of old bones.
- **Amara.** Balam's daughter, fully human and not changed by magic. She belongs to a hidden Maya community on a small island in a forest lake, far up a river from the coast, where magic is stronger than anywhere else. The people there are her neighbors and relatives, and she has taken responsibility for protecting them. People listen to her. She knows the land and is decisive under pressure. Crumble saved her on a raid years ago. She could leave and survive, but she chooses to defend the people she loves. Her flaw: she knows what everyone else needs and cannot say what she wants herself. She and Crumble slowly fall in love. Jigsaw is attracted to her too, sees what is growing between them and quietly steps aside.
- **Don Mateo de Cárdenas.** A Spanish naval officer in his early forties. When he was young, magic killed his little sister, and nobody could explain, stop or control what was happening to her. He joined the navy, did very well in the Americas and rose fast, but never stopped trying to find out what killed her. His career took him to Balam, who finally explained it. The answer did not give him peace. It convinced him that magic creates forces no army, church or king can control, so it should not exist. He hates magic and uses it constantly, but only magic he has studied and controls. He wants to wipe magic out of the Caribbean, because a world without it is easier to understand and to control. He prefers to break important enemies rather than kill them, and he still kills when he needs to. He understands coercion, greed and fear. He never understands people who choose loyalty when they are free to walk away, and that is why he misreads Jigsaw and loses to him.
- **Don Rodrigo, suggested name.** Jigsaw's old schoolmate. His family opened doors for him, but he is also capable: a disciplined, educated, politically smart officer and a strong swordsman. Jigsaw was the more gifted student. Rodrigo married the girl Jigsaw loved. Jigsaw looks at him and thinks, you got the life that should have been mine. Rodrigo looks at Jigsaw and thinks, you had more talent than I did, and look what you chose to become. Rodrigo is partly right. The girl did not leave Jigsaw only for Rodrigo's rank. At seventeen Jigsaw was already angry and could not picture a quiet life.
- **The elder, suggested name Ah Pech.** A respected elder of Amara's community who knew Balam and fought beside him when they were young, so he knows Balam's fighting methods. Years ago, afraid the Spanish would destroy the community, he made a secret arrangement with Cárdenas: information in exchange for being left alone. He believes cooperating is the only realistic way to keep his people alive. He acts from fear and compromise, not cruelty.
- **Balam.** Crumble's teacher, a Maya fighter and keeper of old knowledge, dead sixteen months. Years ago he taught a grieving young Spanish officer, Cárdenas, because he believed the young man deserved answers about his sister. When he saw what Cárdenas became, he cut him off, disappeared and never spoke of him again. Cárdenas counts Balam as one of the great teachers of his life. Balam counted Cárdenas as his greatest mistake. Separately, Balam made practical deals with Spain to protect his people and his daughter. Those are two different decisions.
- **Domingo.** A Maya man at Petit-Goâve who knew Balam. He agrees to meet Crumble because of that connection. Someone betrays the meeting, the Spanish capture him, and under torture he tells Cárdenas where the hunt goes next. His part in the story ends there.

### Chapters 1 to 3: drafted

#### Chapter 1, The Temple

**What happens.** The three are two days into a storm, sheltering in a ruined Maya temple. They have just walked out of a Spanish fort near Veracruz with nothing. Jigsaw saw the storm coming an hour early and called off the search for the saint's finger they were hired to steal. Bonesy flirts with a stone carving he calls Beatriz.

Bonesy brings up a book he once heard priests talk about while he sat on their cellar barrels. They broke it into pieces on purpose and hid them in a line. The young priests said gold was buried with every piece. The old ones lowered their voices about what the book does, and Bonesy caught one word: before. Crumble says this is the codex his dead teacher Balam was hunting. It is real, it is broken, and each piece leads to the next. Jigsaw says the first piece may be in a tarpon pool on a half moon shaped island. He found the pool at twenty and never went in.

At first light the plan dies because they have no money to pay for the trip. Then Bonesy pulls out the saint's finger. He stole it from the cellar and let them mourn the job for two days because he thought it was good for them. The finger is not only a holy relic. It really works, which is why it was locked in a vault and why the hire will pay so much for it. He says goodbye to Beatriz without a joke.

**What changes.** They have a goal, a first clue and the money to chase it. Without knowing it, they have also made Cárdenas their enemy: the finger came out of his own collection.

#### Chapter 2, Petit-Goâve

**What happens.** They sail nine days east. On the way Crumble tells them the cellar they robbed belonged to Cárdenas. At the small pirate outpost of Petit-Goâve they sell the finger to a dealer named Vaz, who can tell at once that it is real, and buy supplies for the sloop. They buy Bonesy a hat, a belt and a blade, the first things he has ever owned. Crumble goes up the hill into the forest to meet Domingo, a Maya man who knew Balam and may know where the codex leads after the first piece.

Jigsaw and Bonesy wait in the town's one tavern. A cursed skull is being gambled at cards, and a mermaid is kept behind a curtain for paying customers. Jigsaw notices a man whose hands have never done hard work and realizes he is a spy. The outpost's law forbids fighting in town, so Jigsaw cannot touch him. The spy leaves.

An hour later Jigsaw sees four lanterns coming down from the fort. Soldiers are coming. Cárdenas has bribed the garrison. Jigsaw and Bonesy hurry to the sloop. A full water barrel needs three men to load, and there are only two, so they leave their water on the dock. The pirates and sailors of the town come out of the tavern and off their boats and stand in the street. The soldiers stop at the top of the street and do not push through.

Crumble is still missing. The tide is about to turn, and after that the way out of the harbor becomes too dangerous until morning. Jigsaw works out how long he can safely wait. He waits longer than that. Crumble finally comes along the shore from the west, wet to the thigh, with only Balam's blade in his hand. Jigsaw pulls him aboard and they escape on the last of the tide.

**What changes.** Cárdenas knows about them and is hunting them, for his stolen relic as much as anything. They are at sea with no water.

**To tighten.** A reader felt the quest should get moving sooner after the finger. The sale, the spy and Domingo all do plot work and stay. The fat is in 2.2, which describes their gear at length. Trim 2.2 hard and fold the short 2.1 into a neighbouring scene. Marked here only. The drafted prose is unchanged.

#### Chapter 3, The Cay

**What happens.** They sail through the night. Crumble explains that someone betrayed his meeting with Domingo. The garrison came straight up the path. Crumble escaped through a smoke he set, but the soldiers captured Domingo. Domingo had confirmed that the pool is the first mark, but he was captured before he could tell Crumble where the hunt goes next. Now Cárdenas has Domingo and may get the rest of the route from him.

They left their water behind, so after two days they are badly dehydrated. Bonesy remembers a small island, a cay, with a fresh spring under a stone carving of the goddess Ixchel. They catch a tuna and eat it raw because they cannot afford to stop and cook. Bonesy keeps asking Crumble about the girl he left until Crumble names her, Anne, and says a few words about going home and finding her married. Jigsaw tells how Crumble came to Balam. Years ago, on a raid with their old crew, Jigsaw saw Crumble come out of the smoke carrying a Maya girl over his shoulder. The girl told Crumble her father would make something of him. Every dawn Crumble names the day under his breath, using the Maya day count Balam taught him.

A Spanish ship appears behind them. The cay is surrounded by reef with one narrow opening. Jigsaw knows the big ship cannot follow them through it safely, so he takes the sloop through and reaches the spring. The ship waits outside the reef and sends a rowboat with ten men.

Jigsaw and Crumble ambush the boat from the rocks and kill eight men. They throw the bodies into the water one at a time where the ship's captain can watch through his glass. They cut the backs of the last two men's legs and throw them in alive to drown. They do this to make it so ugly that the captain cannot stand off and watch. It works. He brings his ship in too fast and runs it onto the coral. Bonesy fires the sloop's one gun into the stuck ship's deck. While the crew is looking at the damage, Jigsaw and Crumble climb down from the cliff onto the ship's masthead, slide down the rigging and attack from behind. The captain, a trained gentleman fencer, was taught by Don Rodrigo, who briefed Cárdenas's men on Jigsaw and Crumble. He taunts Jigsaw as the easy one of the pair. Jigsaw kills him with a dirty thrust no school would teach.

**What changes.** They have beaten a ship Cárdenas sent after them. Cárdenas now knows that three men did it. The reader has seen how they win: fast, clever, cocky and brutal. Chapter 8 will show a very different kind of fight.

### Chapter 4, The Half Moon

The crew's first big success in the hunt. Years ago Jigsaw stood at this pool alone and could not make himself go in. Now he comes back with Crumble and Bonesy. He is still afraid, and this time he goes in.

#### 4.1 Open Water: drafted

**What happens.** They strip the wrecked Spanish ship of powder, food, rope, a good spyglass and careful Spanish charts, and refill their water at the spring. Crumble warns that Cárdenas will hear that three men took his ship, so next time he will not send a rowboat. They sail eleven days west and south. Jigsaw keeps rereading the charts without taking them in. Crumble quotes Balam at him: a man who hurries the hunt feeds the jaguar. Crumble keeps his dawn day count, and Bonesy joins in and gets the day wrong. Bonesy asks why Jigsaw never went into the pool. Jigsaw does not answer.

**Why.** Cárdenas now takes them seriously. The pool is the only lead they have left, because Domingo had the rest of the route.

**What changes.** The pool stops being Jigsaw's private memory and becomes the crew's only road.

#### 4.2 The Pool: drafted

**What happens.** They anchor on the sheltered side of the island and walk across to the pool. The giant tarpon are still there, circling. Jigsaw puts his hand in the water, as he did at twenty, and the biggest fish swims past slowly with its eye on him. Crumble notices that the water is pulling, with no wind or wave to move it, and asks where it goes. Jigsaw admits he never found out.

**Why.** Crumble is the first person Jigsaw has ever shown the pool to.

**What changes.** They know the water leads somewhere under the rock.

#### 4.3 The Night Before: drafted

**What happens.** They spend the night anchored off the island before going into the pool. Crumble sleeps against the mast and Bonesy sits forward turning his hat over in his hands. Jigsaw falls asleep at the tiller with the dead captain's voice in his ear and dreams the last bout of term at his Spanish academy. Rodrigo, the better born boy who already has his navy commission, taunts him before the bout and between touches: the girl came to supper at his house, his mother asked where Jigsaw's father came from, and his father has written to the girl's father. Jigsaw feels the rush of winning and wants more of it. He wins three touches to two. The master calls the bout finished and Rodrigo raises his blade to salute. Jigsaw strikes him across the head and enjoys it. By the next morning he is no longer a student at the academy. He wakes at first light gripping the tiller, having said Rodrigo's name in his sleep. Bonesy heard it and asks who Rodrigo is. Jigsaw says nobody. He still tells himself Rodrigo brought it on himself, some mornings, but he knows the bout was over.

**Why.** It introduces Rodrigo and the rage that does not stop when the fight is over, in one memory, before either matters to the plot.

**What changes.** The reader knows Rodrigo's name and what he means to Jigsaw, and Bonesy now has a name to pry at. The parents' deaths are saved for a later quiet moment.

#### 4.4 Into the Pool: drafted

**What happens.** Bonesy walks down into the pool on a line, since he does not need to breathe. He comes back and says the rock on the low side of the pool is not rock. Jigsaw is afraid. He goes anyway. He takes two breaths, swims straight at what looks like solid stone and passes through it. The stone is an illusion. He comes up in air on the other side. Crumble follows him.

**Why.** The way in is simple. The priests hid the entrance behind something that only looks like a wall.

**What changes.** Jigsaw has done the one thing he could not do alone. Nobody comments on it.

#### 4.5 Come On Through: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** A passage cut by the old priests is full of every trap from the old stories: loose floor slabs, a pit of stakes and darts that shoot from holes in the walls. Bonesy strolls through all of it. Darts pass through his ribs. A slab drops on him and he climbs out of the pieces complaining about his hat. He crosses the stake pit by walking on the points. "Come on through." The other two follow exactly where he stepped, feeling foolish.

**Why.** It is a comic take on the trap scene, and Bonesy is useful simply by being what he is.

**What changes.** They reach the chamber safely, and the reader is laughing before the puzzle.

#### 4.6 The Leaf: about 1,800 words

**What happens.** In a dry chamber they find a sealed jar holding one folded leaf of bark book. This is the first piece of the codex. It is a painted map of a coast none of them recognizes. Crumble explains the old Maya rules for maps: east at the top and painted red, footprints for roads, bars and dots for numbers. It still makes no sense until Jigsaw turns it with the red edge up and recognizes the coast he sails. The footprints count canoe days north to a long island: Cozumel. Beside it is a second symbol that Crumble cannot read.

Crumble knows who could read it: Amara, Balam's daughter, the girl he carried out of the smoke years ago. She lives in a hidden Maya community far up a river. Going there also solves a second problem. The community is so secluded that the Spanish will lose their trail for a while. Past the chamber the water keeps pulling down into the dark. Jigsaw looks at it for a long moment, then leaves with his friends.

**Why.** The first puzzle is solved on the page, and it needs both men: Crumble's knowledge and Jigsaw's sea.

**What changes.** They have the first piece and a heading. They also need Amara, both to read the symbol and to give them a place to hide. The deeper water stays a mystery for later books.

### Chapter 5, The Hidden Village: proposed

They go up river to Amara's community, the one place where magic is still strong. A Spanish patrol comes too close, and they fight it beside her. Amara reads the symbol and joins them.

#### 5.1 Up River: about 2,500 words

**What happens.** They sail to the river mouth and take the sloop up a brown river under heavy trees. There is no horizon, the air does not move and every sound is too close. Jigsaw keeps looking for a sky he cannot see and walks with his hand on his hilt. Crumble knows this country from his years with Balam and leads. Every time he looks back, Jigsaw relaxes a little. At the end of the river they leave the sloop hidden and go on foot and by canoe to a small island in a forest lake.

**Why.** Jigsaw only knows the sea. Inland he has to depend on Crumble.

**What changes.** The usual order between them flips. It also sets up Florida, where neither man knows the ground.

#### 5.2 The Village: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** They reach the community. Magic is stronger here than anywhere Jigsaw has been, and many of the people have been changed by it. At first Jigsaw sees only things that frighten him. Over the next days he sees families. Suggested examples:

- An old man whose legs have slowly become mangrove roots that go down into the wet earth under his house. He can no longer leave. His grandchildren sit on his roots, his daughter brings him food, and he complains, tells stories and argues with everyone.
- A fisherman with rough skin like a shark's, changed eyes, slits like gills in his neck and several rows of small teeth. Jigsaw assumes he is dangerous. Then he sees him cleaning fish beside his son and worrying about the boy's marriage.
- A woman with one arm turned into a heavy crab claw. It is awkward, ugly and painful to carry, not a weapon.
- A man whose skin keeps growing coral and barnacle shell. Every few weeks his wife cuts it off him with a knife, and it hurts.
- A girl whose skin is turning to bark, with thin vines growing from her shoulders.
- At least one tragic case. Suggested: a young man whose coral growth has reached inside his chest. He is slowly dying and everyone knows it. Or a man changed so far that he no longer knows his own family, who keep him penned and still feed him every day.

Bonesy fits in at once. He is one more person whose body magic has changed, and the children love him. Crumble sees Amara again for the first time since he carried her out of the smoke. She is now the person the community turns to.

**Why.** The reader needs to know what Cárdenas wants to erase. Some of these people suffer because of magic, which makes his argument stronger. But it is also their life.

**What changes.** Jigsaw stops seeing monsters and starts seeing families. Crumble and Amara meet again.

#### 5.3 The Patrol: about 3,500 words

**What happens.** Scouts report that a Spanish patrol has come further up the river than any patrol has come before. Amara takes charge at once. She knows the land, picks the place for an ambush and gives orders, and people obey her. Jigsaw, Crumble and Bonesy join in.

It goes to blades. Jigsaw is fast and frightening, and at first it is thrilling to watch. Then a Spanish soldier drops his weapon and kneels, and Jigsaw's blade is already moving toward him. In that moment Jigsaw remembers a raid two years ago. He and Crumble attacked a Spanish merchant ship. His violence was useful, even exciting. Then the crew surrendered, the fight was over, and he kept killing. Crumble saw the exact moment fighting turned into rage. Bonesy saw it too. Nobody said a word to him about it. Back in the present, Jigsaw stops. Barely. Crumble sees him stop.

On the patrol officer they find papers. One is an order from Cárdenas to search the river mouths on this coast for a sloop with two men and a walking skeleton aboard. Another is a letter reporting the Treaty of Madrid: England has agreed to put down the buccaneers. The chain that led the patrol here is simple. Under torture, Domingo told Cárdenas that Balam had a daughter living up one of these rivers, and that only she could read the old signs. Cárdenas guessed they would go to her and ordered patrols into every river. A fishing canoe saw the sloop go into this one. Cárdenas still does not know exactly where the village is.

**Why.** One event does several jobs. It shows Amara as a leader before she is ever a translator. It gives action. It tests whether Jigsaw still behaves the way he did on the merchant ship. It shows that Spanish pressure has reached even this hidden place. It also reveals the Treaty, which means the places where men like Jigsaw and Crumble could disappear are closing.

**What changes.** Jigsaw has shown he can stop, but only just. Amara's community is no longer safe. When the patrol does not come back, Cárdenas will know roughly where to look.

#### 5.4 The Singing Shrine: about 2,500 words

**What happens.** Amara reads the symbol from the map. It names a small shrine on the south point of Cozumel that whistles when the north wind blows, and says the door opens only when the shrine sings. So the second piece is in a sea cave under the shrine, and it can only be reached during a norther, the hard north wind of the winter months. Crumble goes quiet. Balam taught him about the shrine but never this. He takes it as proof that Balam did not fully trust him.

A respected elder of the community, who knew Balam and fought beside him as a young man, offers to guide them to Cozumel. Crumble trusts him at once because he knew Balam. Nobody knows that years ago the elder made a secret arrangement with Cárdenas to keep the Spanish away from the community.

**Why.** The second puzzle is solved. Crumble's belief that he was never good enough for Balam gets worse.

**What changes.** They know where and when the second piece can be reached. A traitor is now in the crew.

#### 5.5 Amara Joins: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** Amara decides to come. Balam told her that the codex in Cárdenas's hands would be the end of people like her neighbors, though he never told her how. The patrol proves Cárdenas is already reaching for them. She could leave and survive, since magic has not changed her. She chooses to defend her people. On the way down river she takes the tiller from Crumble without asking, and he lets her, which Jigsaw has never seen him do. Bonesy asks her something filthy, she answers him straight, and he adores her. Then he asks what she wants for herself. She can say what her community needs, what her father wanted and what Crumble needs. She has no answer for herself.

The elder comes too. At the river mouth he slips away for an hour and sends a fishing canoe out with a message for Cárdenas: the shrine, the north wind and the next norther.

**Why.** He believes giving Cárdenas what he wants is the only way to keep the community alive after the missing patrol. He acts from fear, not cruelty.

**What changes.** The crew is now four, plus a guide. Amara's flaw is clear. Cárdenas knows exactly where they are going and when.

### Chapter 6, The Singing Shrine: proposed

They get the second piece, and Cárdenas takes it from them. The capture at the end of this chapter is the midpoint of the book.

#### 6.1 The Cave Under the Shrine: about 2,500 words

**What happens.** They reach the south point of Cozumel as a norther comes in. The shrine on the point starts to whistle. Under it, the sea cave opens only while the wind holds, and the surge inside is strong enough to kill a man. Cárdenas's men already tried to blast their way in with powder, and six fresh graves above the beach show what the sea did to them. Jigsaw reads the surge and calls the moments to move. Amara reads the old words cut beside the door. They get inside and find the second piece: a leaf painted with the great star, Venus, and a count of days.

Coming out, Jigsaw is sure of this water. He takes them toward open sea by a channel he trusts. Then the current in the channel turns against them, with no change in wind or tide to explain it. From his ship, Cárdenas is using a magical object he has studied and controls to turn the water. By the time Jigsaw understands, the sloop has been pushed back into the cove and a Spanish ship has closed the way out.

**Why.** Cárdenas let them do the dangerous part, because they could do what his men could not. Jigsaw's pride in his own seamanship walks them into the trap.

**What changes.** They have the second piece and no way out.

#### 6.2 The Mirror: about 2,300 words

**What happens.** They are forced ashore. Cárdenas waits on a camp stool by the shrine. He is polite. He holds up a black scrying mirror, and each brother sees his best memory inside it. Crumble sees a day of training with Balam. Jigsaw sees his father taking him out in a boat for the first time and showing him the sea. Then Cárdenas walks into each memory and kills the man at its center.

**Why.** Cárdenas goes straight for what each man loves most and shows he can reach it. He shakes them before a blade is drawn.

**What changes.** They go into the fight already off balance.

#### 6.3 Taken: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** It goes to blades, and both brothers cut down several soldiers. Crumble finds himself facing the elder. He expects his training from Balam to give him the advantage. It does not, because the elder trained and fought beside Balam and knows every move Crumble knows, and some Crumble was never taught. The elder defeats Crumble and captures him. Several soldiers overpower Amara together. Bonesy laughs at Cárdenas's magic, and Cárdenas locks him rigid with a jade piece from his collection.

Jigsaw is still fighting. Then he sees the officer in command of the soldiers: Don Rodrigo, his old schoolmate. Rodrigo sees at once that Jigsaw's weakness is Crumble. He puts his blade to Crumble's throat and tells Jigsaw to drop his weapons. He mocks him for caring so much about another man, with an insult that fits the period and treats Jigsaw as a woman or a man's wife. Rodrigo: "You had more talent than I did. Better marks. Better with a blade. Everyone knew it. And I became an officer while you became this." Then he looks at Crumble and makes a joke about Jigsaw's manhood, asking which of the two plays the wife. Suggested: "Drop it, or I open your husband's throat." Jigsaw hates it. He drops his blades anyway.

**Why.** For the first time Jigsaw has to choose between his pride and Crumble's life, and he chooses Crumble. He does not yet understand what that choice says about him.

**What changes.** All four are prisoners. Cárdenas has both pieces. This is the midpoint of the book.

### Chapter 7, The Hold: proposed

Cárdenas shows them his collection, confides in Bonesy alone, and drugs them. In the vision the brothers live through each other's pasts. Bonesy gives himself up for the others.

#### 7.1 The Cabin: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** Chained, they are brought to Cárdenas's cabin. It is full of magical objects he has collected over twenty years, each on its own shelf with a label in his handwriting: where it came from, what it does, what it costs and how to control it. Idols and saints, an Inca knotted cord, the black mirror, jars of seed, a jade that locks a man's limbs, a mask that makes a man forget his own children. He shows them around like a host. For him, understanding a thing and controlling it are the same, which is why everything is labeled. One shelf is empty. Jigsaw reads its label: it describes the saint's finger they stole, where it came from and what it does. Bonesy says he misses her.

Bonesy treats the collection like a room full of interesting junk. He picks things up, makes rude remarks about the idols and asks what the mask does at parties. Cárdenas cannot stop watching him. He cannot explain Bonesy, and Bonesy does not care why he exists. He is already dead, so threats do not work on him. Worst of all, Bonesy is happy. Cárdenas has spent his whole life trying to master the force that destroyed his family, and here is a walking corpse who simply enjoys being alive.

**Why.** The cabin shows what Cárdenas is in one room. Bonesy is the one thing in it he cannot label.

**What changes.** Cárdenas decides he wants Bonesy kept and studied.

#### 7.2 What Bonesy Heard: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** The others are taken down to the hold. Cárdenas keeps Bonesy in the cabin alone. To him Bonesy is a specimen, not a listener, and a specimen bound for a chest will never repeat anything. Bonesy is also the one being who knows from the inside what magic does to a body. So Cárdenas talks to him more openly than he has talked to anyone in years.

He tells Bonesy his history. When he was a boy, magic killed his younger sister, and nobody could explain it or stop it. He joined the navy, rose fast, and never stopped looking for an answer. His career brought him to a Maya settlement tied to what killed her, and that led him to Balam. Balam did not trust a Spanish officer until Cárdenas stopped soldiers from burning the settlement and executing its people. Then Balam taught him and explained his sister's death. The answer did not bring peace. It convinced him that magic is a force nobody can control and should not exist. His collection grew through conquest, until Balam cut him off.

Then he tells Bonesy what the codex does. It restores everything magic has changed to what it was before. That was the word Bonesy overheard years ago. Bonesy was an ordinary Maya man who died centuries ago, so restored he would be old bones. Many of Amara's people would die or be unmade. Cárdenas calls the deaths a tragedy and accepts them. He asks Bonesy what he was before and whether being what he is hurts. Bonesy remembers mostly barrels, and says it doesn't.

Bonesy is thrown into the hold with the others and tells them what he heard, in his own words: short, crude, out of order, with long pauses. Suggested lines: "He had a sister." ... "Magic took her." ... "Now he wants all of it gone." ... "Me too."

**Why.** A private confession to the one being Cárdenas thinks does not count is more believable than a speech to prisoners. It also comes from his fascination with Bonesy. Bonesy's retelling keeps it short, keeps the book's tone and leaves gaps for the vision and the fort to fill.

**What changes.** The crew finally knows what they have been chasing, and what it would do to Bonesy and to Amara's people. Bonesy has seen a side of Cárdenas nobody else has.

#### 7.3 Jigsaw's Life: about 4,000 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas pours a cup of ololiuhqui, a seed drink the Church has banned. "Every man I have broken thought he was the exception. Drink." They refuse. Soldiers press swords to their throats until they drink. Bonesy cannot be drugged, but he appears in the vision anyway, as the man he was: short, bow legged and missing teeth. "Hello, fellas."

The brothers are present beside each other inside each other's lives. They can watch but cannot change anything. They talk aloud in their sleep, and Cárdenas sits beside them and writes down every word. First, Jigsaw's life, in order:

1. His father as a successful Barbary corsair, with money, danger and status.
2. His father deciding to give that life up.
3. His father making a deal with Spain so his family can have a safer future.
4. Jigsaw's childhood, sailing the islands with his father.
5. Jigsaw entering the Spanish colonial school.
6. His rivalry with Rodrigo.
7. The school duel. Jigsaw wins, the instructor calls the fight and Rodrigo stops. Jigsaw keeps attacking after he has already won.
8. Jigsaw with the girl who later marries Rodrigo.
9. His parents falling sick with malaria and dying.
10. Jigsaw growing angrier and more alone.
11. His pirate years.
12. The years he and Crumble spent apart, showing Crumble how violent Jigsaw became without him.

Then Jigsaw speaks with his father while Crumble watches. Jigsaw asks why he gave up the life he had. His father says he chose his family. He got to raise his son, and he does not think of it as surrender. Jigsaw has spent years measuring his father by what he gave up. His father measured his life by what he got. It is not a speech. It is a tired man talking to his grown son.

**Why.** For the first time, Crumble sees the parts of Jigsaw's life that Jigsaw would never explain.

**What changes.** Jigsaw has heard his father's answer, but he does not yet act on it. Cárdenas now knows his wounds.

#### 7.4 Crumble's Life: about 4,000 words

**What happens.** Then Crumble's life, in order:

1. Young Crumble in England.
2. Crumble and Anne.
3. His fear that an ordinary life means he has failed.
4. Leaving Anne at sixteen, because he thinks he has to become someone important before he deserves her.
5. Arriving in the Caribbean.
6. Meeting Jigsaw.
7. Their early pirate years.
8. Saving Amara on a raid.
9. Amara sending him to her father.
10. Balam beginning Crumble's training.
11. A short look at Balam and young Cárdenas, years earlier.
12. Balam realizing what Cárdenas has become and cutting him off.
13. Balam being demanding with Crumble.
14. Crumble building Balam into an impossible ideal.
15. Crumble going back to England.
16. Anne, happily married to an ordinary man, with a child.

Then Crumble speaks with Balam while Jigsaw watches. Crumble asks why Balam taught Cárdenas. Balam says he saw a grieving young man desperate for answers and believed knowledge might help him. "I regret what he did with what I taught him. I could not know what he would become." Crumble asks why Balam made deals with Spain. Balam says that was a separate choice. He had a daughter and people he was responsible for keeping alive. Crumble asks why Balam was so hard on him, and why Amara knew things he was never taught. Balam says Amara grew up with that knowledge, while Crumble had three years. He was hard on Crumble because the world would be harder. He never expected Crumble to become another Balam. Crumble made that duty up himself.

**Why.** For the first time, Jigsaw sees why Crumble became Crumble. Crumble learns that the debt he has been paying was never asked of him.

**What changes.** Crumble is left with a real man to grieve, not a standard to meet. Cárdenas, listening, learns that Crumble still keeps Balam's Maya day count every dawn.

#### 7.5 The Chest: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** They wake in irons. Cárdenas wants Bonesy kept for study. Bonesy offers himself if the others are kept alive, and climbs into a gilded chest made for a saint's bones. "I've been in one. I'll manage." He tosses his hat to Crumble. "Mind that." Cárdenas takes it as surrender. It is love, and Cárdenas does not count love when he plans. He keeps Bonesy apart from the others as a hostage, leaves the chest under guard at his camp on Cozumel and sails for the fort at Campeche with the rest.

**Why.** Bonesy has been the first thing thrown over the side of every boat he has been on. This time he chooses to go, for his friends.

**What changes.** The crew is split. Cárdenas has misread Bonesy, and that mistake matters later.

### Chapter 8, The Fort: proposed

The vision gave Jigsaw understanding. The fort tests whether it changes what he does. Cárdenas tries to win Jigsaw with comfort, then tries to break the other two, then is forced to make a deal. The crew genuinely gives up.

The chapter follows one line in Cárdenas. He studies Jigsaw, believes he understands him, and tries to control him through comfort. He offers Jigsaw freedom alone, and Jigsaw refuses. Cárdenas is furious, punishes him and turns on Crumble and Amara. That fails to get him everything he needs, so he has to bargain with all three. They give him what he needs, he tests them with their weapons, and they leave the weapons behind. He believes he has finally broken them. Then, in chapter 9, they choose to come back. Cárdenas understands coercion, greed, fear and people who have no choice. He never understands people who choose loyalty when they are completely free to walk away. That blind spot is why he loses.

#### 8.1 The Cell: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** The first weeks in the fort at Campeche are misery. Jigsaw, Crumble and Amara are held together in one cell. Jigsaw stops talking and does push ups until his arms give out, because it is the only thing in the cell he can control. Crumble meditates the way Balam taught him and recites prayers, and every dawn he names the day under his breath. A guard reports the day count to Cárdenas. Amara paces because sitting still drives her mad. Nobody tells them where Bonesy is.

**Why.** Time in the cell costs them something before the real pressure starts. Each of them holds on in his or her own way.

**What changes.** They are worn down, and Cárdenas is watching to see which of them gives first.

#### 8.2 Privileges: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas takes a special interest in Jigsaw. He sees himself in him. Both are intelligent and angry. Both hate feeling powerless and try to control every dangerous situation themselves. Both have built their lives around never having to submit. Cárdenas believes Jigsaw is a younger, less disciplined version of himself who chose the wrong side, and he sets out to prove it.

He does it slowly. First Jigsaw gets better food. Then a book. Then time outside the cell and a chance to wash. Then dinner at Cárdenas's table, where Cárdenas talks to him as an intelligent equal, not a prisoner. Rodrigo is at one of these dinners, a reminder of the life Jigsaw could have had. Cárdenas points out that Jigsaw calls himself free, yet his anger, Spain, Rodrigo and his friends have made most of his choices for him. Each night Jigsaw comes back to the cell clean and fed, and Crumble and Amara watch him come back.

**Why.** Cárdenas wants Jigsaw to feel how easy life becomes when he stops resisting. Over these weeks he decides that what Jigsaw wants most is independence.

**What changes.** A gap opens between Jigsaw and the other two. Part of him likes the dinners.

#### 8.3 Stepping Aside: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** Jigsaw is attracted to Amara too. One night he comes back from Cárdenas's table with bread hidden in his shirt, meaning to give it to her. He finds her asleep against Crumble's shoulder, with Crumble's hand holding hers. He gives the bread to Crumble and lies down. Nobody says anything. Crumble never knows what Jigsaw gave up.

The next day Cárdenas moves Jigsaw out of the shared cell into a room of his own in the officers' quarters.

**Why.** Jigsaw once lost a girl, partly through his own anger. This time he steps back by choice and asks nothing for it. Cárdenas moves him to separate him from the other two and make the comfort feel like his real life.

**What changes.** Crumble and Amara are together, though neither has said so. Jigsaw is now living apart from them.

#### 8.4 Freedom Alone: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas makes Jigsaw the offer he has been building toward. Jigsaw can leave the next morning with a boat, money and papers that will protect him from the English crackdown on buccaneers. Crumble and Amara stay. Cárdenas is plain about what that means. He will get what he needs from them, and if he has to break them to get it, he will. Jigsaw can sail away and never see any of it.

Jigsaw refuses.

Cárdenas becomes genuinely angry. It is not that he dislikes hearing no. He spent weeks studying Jigsaw, believed he had found his weakness and named his price, and the refusal proves there is still something in Jigsaw he cannot control. He presses him. Jigsaw has spent years saying freedom means owing nobody anything, and now he is choosing a cell for two other people. Cárdenas tells him Crumble and Amara will break whether he stays or not. Jigsaw still refuses.

The special treatment ends at once. No more dinners, books or better food. Cárdenas stops treating him as a younger version of himself. Guards throw Jigsaw back into the cell with Crumble and Amara. For the first time in weeks they see him come back dirty and bruised instead of clean and fed. He does not tell them he was offered freedom and turned it down. Crumble works it out slowly, from small things: the guards' anger, the missing book, the fact that Jigsaw is here at all. Crumble has seen Jigsaw's whole life in the vision, so he knows what that choice cost. He does not make a speech about it either.

Cárdenas tells Jigsaw that if he insists on staying with them, he can watch Cárdenas get what he needs from them.

**Why.** Jigsaw is offered the exact freedom he has always claimed to want, at the price of the people he loves, and he chooses the people. This time nobody has a blade at Crumble's throat. He has had weeks to think.

**What changes.** Jigsaw is back with his friends and in a worse place than before. The imprisonment turns harsher.

#### 8.5 What He Cannot Take: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas sets out to prove that Jigsaw's refusal achieved nothing. He separates Crumble and Amara and questions each of them alone. He uses threats, lies about what the other has already told him, isolation and some physical force. He is normally very good at breaking people. This time he gets pieces, never the whole.

Working with what he gets, he finally sees the size of his problem. The Venus page needs three kinds of knowledge, and he has none of them:

- Crumble's unbroken Maya day count, kept every dawn since Balam died, to tell which morning the page means.
- Amara's part of Balam's tradition, the old way Balam's people read and counted the days written on a leaf like this, which Crumble was never taught.
- Jigsaw's navigation, to turn a star, a number of days, currents and coastlines into a course a ship can sail.

He has ships, soldiers, charts, magical objects and most of the codex. He has spent his career destroying exactly the people and traditions who kept this kind of knowledge. Now he needs three prisoners, and none of them will give it to him by force.

**Why.** For a man obsessed with control, needing anyone is infuriating. It is also an early sign of how he will lose: he cannot do everything himself, and he will not admit it until he has no choice.

**What changes.** Cárdenas concludes that breaking them is slower and riskier than buying them. He decides to negotiate.

#### 8.6 The Deal: about 3,000 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas brings Jigsaw, Crumble and Amara together in one room. The tone is different from the dinners. He is not trying to recruit Jigsaw or convince him that they are alike. This is business. If they give him everything he needs to reach the place where the codex can be used, all three go free together.

Jigsaw expects a trick. There is none. Cárdenas has simply decided that freedom together is the one thing they want, and paying it is cheaper than breaking them. They accept. They have no secret plan, they do not give him false information, and they do not mean to follow him. They believe they have lost.

The second leaf is a Venus page: stand at the shrine on the morning the great star first rises again before dawn, then sail toward it for a set number of days. Cárdenas can translate the symbols but cannot tell which morning they mean. Crumble gives him the day from his count. Amara explains how Balam's people read the count of days on the leaf. Jigsaw turns the star, the days, the currents and the coastline into a course. It points to a deep temple in the mangrove estuaries of south Florida.

Cárdenas keeps his word. Before they leave, he has their confiscated weapons laid out on a table and tells them they may take them. Among them are the swords Jigsaw's father once mended for him, and the blade Balam made for Crumble. Cárdenas watches Jigsaw closely. If Jigsaw reaches for his swords, the fight is not over. Jigsaw looks at his father's swords for a long moment. Then he leaves them. Crumble leaves Balam's blade. They are not giving up weapons on principle. They believe the fight is over and they lost. They mean to get Bonesy back and disappear.

Cárdenas watches them walk out empty handed and takes it as proof that he has finally broken them. After weeks of frustration with Jigsaw, he lets himself enjoy it.

**Why.** The weapons are Cárdenas's last test, and they pass it in the way that convinces him. Jigsaw leaving the swords is not a rejection of his father. After the vision he understands that what he has from his father is not kept in the objects his father left. Nobody says this.

**What changes.** They have given Cárdenas Florida. This is his strongest moment and their lowest. Jigsaw no longer has his father's swords, and they do not come back to him before the end of the book.

#### 8.7 Bonesy: about 2,500 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas does not tell them where Bonesy is, but they guess: his camp on Cozumel. They sail back with no swords, only a boat hook, a knife, sticks and their hands. This fight is the opposite of chapter 3. There is no clever ambush and no swagger. They take the guards one at a time. Someone gets hurt badly, Jigsaw loses a tooth, and they drag each other back up. They win by inches and break open the chest. Bonesy complains about the dark.

**Why.** Chapter 3 showed how they used to win. This shows what winning means now: smaller, uglier and done for one friend. Getting Bonesy back is the one thing they refuse to give up.

**What changes.** The crew is together again, beaten and unarmed.

#### 8.8 The Beach: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** That night on a beach they agree to quit. There is no plan to go after Cárdenas. Bonesy saw everything they saw in the vision, and he uses it.

> "You learned it, then." ... "Sit still. Keep your head down. Like your fathers." ... "Good men." ... "Dead men." ... "That was their choice." ... "What's yours." ... "He's coming for me first. Then her people. Then Tortuga." ... "Nobody's hiding me."

Then Amara says, for the first time, something she wants only for herself. It is small and almost childish. Suggested: to sail far enough north that the stars change. Later, at sea, Bonesy admits he was working on them a little. If Cárdenas wins, Bonesy turns to bones.

**Why.** Bonesy is not reminding them of a plan. He is asking beaten people to choose the fight again. Both men have treated their fathers' compromises as instructions. Their fathers chose based on the lives they had. Jigsaw and Crumble have to decide what they believe is worth doing. Bonesy's confession keeps it honest and funny.

**What changes.** They decide to go back, free to walk away and choosing not to. This is the one thing Cárdenas never planned for. Amara has named a want of her own.

### Chapter 9, Florida: proposed

The whole chapter is the climax, about 20,000 words. It tests whether they have changed, not whether they fight better. They have always fought well together. The difference is in their heads. Jigsaw no longer needs to be the best man in the room. Crumble no longer acts on what he imagines Balam would do. Amara makes a choice because she wants it. Bonesy stays Bonesy. Cárdenas loses because he cannot hand anything essential to another person.

#### 9.1 Preparing Again: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** They sail to Tortuga with no weapons and no money. Jigsaw's father's swords and Balam's blade are still on Cárdenas's table in Campeche, where they chose to leave them. Friends there, including people changed by magic whom the three once helped, give them what they need, because the codex would kill them too. They choose different gear this time, because they are not the men who lost. Suggested: Jigsaw takes a curved corsair blade like his father's. Crumble takes a plain English cutlass instead of a Maya weapon. Amara chooses her own gear for the first time in her life. Bonesy keeps the hat.

**Why.** What they carry shows who they have become. Tortuga also shows how many lives are at stake beyond Amara's village.

**What changes.** They leave as a crew of four, each one there by choice.

#### 9.2 Reaching Florida: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** They sail north toward the course Jigsaw worked out for Cárdenas. Cárdenas has a head start and bigger ships, but the sloop is faster and they push it hard. Jigsaw reads the water and Crumble keeps the count so they arrive on the right days.

**Why.** They are now racing toward a place they handed to their enemy.

**What changes.** They reach the Florida coast with little time to spare.

#### 9.3 The Estuary: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** The temple lies deep in the mangrove estuaries of south Florida, Calusa country, which the Spanish never conquered. Jigsaw's whole sense of himself rests on understanding water, and this water makes no sense to him. There is no clean line between sea and land. Mangrove roots form walls. Fresh and salt water mix. Channels disappear with the tide. Mud looks solid and is not. Water looks deep and is not. He can see the sky but never a horizon.

**Why.** Neither brother is the expert here. They learn the place together, as equals.

**What changes.** Jigsaw has to depend on the others without resenting it.

#### 9.4 Cárdenas Is Already Here: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** They find signs of Cárdenas: his big ships anchored outside the estuary because they cannot get in, and his men moving through the channels in boats with local pilots. He is ahead of them and close to the temple.

**Why.** The race is real, and they are behind.

**What changes.** They have to get past his boat parties to reach the temple before the counted morning.

#### 9.5 Smaller Fights: about 2,000 words

**What happens.** A series of short, sharp fights against Cárdenas's boat parties in the narrow channels. They win by knowing small things: Jigsaw judging which channel will still have water in an hour, Amara reading old markers on the shell mounds, Bonesy walking underwater to cut a boat's line.

**Why.** It builds pace and shows each of them useful in this strange place.

**What changes.** Cárdenas learns they are coming.

#### 9.6 Separated: about 1,000 words

**What happens.** An ambush at a falling tide splits them. Jigsaw is cut off in one channel, Crumble in another. Amara and Bonesy push on toward the temple.

**Why.** Each brother has to face his own rival alone.

**What changes.** The crew is in three places at once.

#### 9.7 Rodrigo: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** Rodrigo finds Jigsaw. They fight, and Rodrigo is good. Jigsaw beats him and has him on the ground. Rodrigo throws the girl and the school at him, waiting for Jigsaw to finish it. Jigsaw has the chance to kill him. He leaves him alive and goes, because his friends are somewhere ahead and that matters more.

**Why.** Jigsaw no longer needs revenge, Rodrigo's respect or proof that he was always better. Rodrigo no longer matters to his sense of his own worth.

**What changes.** Jigsaw has shown, in action, that he has chosen what is worth something. Rodrigo is left alive and irrelevant.

#### 9.8 The Elder: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** Crumble meets the elder again. At the shrine he lost because he fought like Balam and the elder knew Balam's methods. This time he fights his own way, mixing Balam's training with the dirty buccaneer fighting he learned beside Jigsaw. He wins and spares the elder.

**Why.** Crumble stops trying to be Balam and wins as himself.

**What changes.** Open: whether the elder turns and helps them, or simply walks away.

#### 9.9 The Temple: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** Amara and Bonesy reach the temple first, and the brothers arrive soon after. It is a shell mound on a mangrove island with a stair cut down the inside of a deep sinkhole. Halfway down is a ledge at tide level. At the bottom, under black water, is a flat stone. Amara explains how it works. At low water the stone can be reached. On the counted morning the spring tide floods the sinkhole through hidden channels, and for a few minutes the water rises to the ledge. The leaves must lie on the stone and the rite must be spoken from the ledge as the water reaches it.

**Why.** The final rules are explained before they are used.

**What changes.** They know exactly what Cárdenas has to do, and when.

#### 9.10 Cárdenas Takes Control: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas has competent people with him, but he cannot trust any of them with something essential. He goes down the stair himself at low water and lays the leaves on the stone. Then he takes the ledge to speak the rite himself, using what Balam taught him years ago. Someone who knows the full rite, Amara or the elder if he has turned, warns him that his version is incomplete. He will not hand the rite to anyone. Once the tide covers the stone, no living man can reach the leaves. He has never counted Bonesy as anything but a specimen.

**Why.** Every choice he makes comes from the same place: he will not let anyone else hold anything that matters.

**What changes.** The rite begins, flawed but working.

#### 9.11 The Codex Begins to Work: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** As the water rises, the codex starts to restore everything near it, and the effect spreads outward. The labeled pieces of Cárdenas's collection he brought with him crumble back into plain stone, wood and bone. Bonesy starts coming apart. His fingers loosen and his jaw slips. Everyone can see what restoration really means. Because Cárdenas's rite is incomplete, it is working slowly. That buys a few minutes.

**Why.** The danger stops being an idea and becomes visible.

**What changes.** They have until the tide reaches the ledge to stop it.

#### 9.12 Breaking It: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** They split the work without discussing it. Jigsaw holds the stair against Cárdenas's men and calls how long until the water peaks. He does not try to be the one who wins it. Crumble keeps the count so they know the moment. Amara chooses to break the codex rather than use it or save it, even though it is her father's life's work and her people's old knowledge. It is her own choice. Bonesy walks down the stair into the black water, where no living man can go, coming apart as he goes, and scatters the leaves into the deep before the water reaches the ledge.

**Why.** The codex follows rules, not feelings. They can stop it because they trust each other to do separate jobs at the same moment. Crumble decides to destroy what Balam spent his life hunting.

**What changes.** The rite fails. The restoration stops. Bonesy stays Bonesy, though he is badly shaken.

#### 9.13 Cárdenas Loses Control: about 1,000 words

**What happens.** Cárdenas is left on the ledge with an incomplete rite, a ruined collection and the leaves gone. Rodrigo has stopped following him. He survives, and he has lost what he cares about more than his life: control.

**Why.** He could have won if he had trusted anyone else with anything.

**What changes.** For the first time since his sister died, he cannot predict what happens next.

#### 9.14 Aftermath: about 1,500 words

**What happens.** They leave the estuary. Jigsaw is quiet, his rage spent but not cured. Crumble grieves Balam as a man for the first time, not as a standard. Bonesy is cheerful and privately shaken by how close he came to being bones. Amara holds one thing that is only hers: a course north, far enough that the stars change.

**Why.** The book ends on what they chose, not on a victory speech.

**What changes.** Book one ends here. What comes after, including Morgan and Panama, waits until this ending is settled.

### How the codex works: proposed

These rules are older than anyone in the book. The crew does not fit them because of destiny. They simply have the knowledge and skills the rules happen to require.

- **What it does.** It restores everything magic has changed to what it was before. That sounds harmless until you think it through. Bonesy was once an ordinary Maya man, but that man died centuries ago, so restored he becomes old bones. Many people in Amara's community have magic woven into their bodies and lives, and restoring them may kill them or unmake them. Cárdenas understands this and accepts the deaths as the price of removing something that should never have existed.
- **What it needs.** Five things together: the written leaves, a living day count, the spoken rite, the right place and the right time. Missing any one, nothing happens.
- **Why it was broken.** The old priests saw what it could do and split it up, hiding the leaves in a chain and leaving the rite and the count with living keepers. Bonesy overheard them in their cellars, which is the legend in chapter 1.
- **The leaves are directions.** The map, the singing shrine leaf and the Venus page are not treasure. They are the road to the one place where the codex can be used.
- **The place is in Florida.** A deep sinkhole in the mangrove estuary. Balam's people were its keepers, not its makers.
- **Using it takes several actions at once.** The leaves must lie on the stone at the bottom, and the rite must be spoken from the ledge as the spring tide reaches it on the counted morning. One person cannot easily do all of it, though Cárdenas tries.
- **A flawed rite works slowly.** If the rite is spoken incompletely, the codex still starts, but slowly. That is what gives the crew time in chapter 9.
- **It can be broken again.** Scattered, it sleeps. Breaking it is the only choice that does not decide for everyone what gets restored.

### How the Florida climax was built

The three earlier battle versions were not picked from mechanically. Chapter 9 above combines the parts that best test the updated arcs.

- **From version A:** the sinkhole, the ledge, the stone below the water, Bonesy going where no living man can, and Cárdenas speaking the rite himself and getting it wrong.
- **From version B:** the moment the codex starts working and everyone sees what restoration means, and Rodrigo left behind.
- **From version C:** the maze of channels, Cárdenas's big ships stuck outside, and his men coming in by boat with local pilots.
- **Dropped:** the four stations, and anything that worked only because the crew was symbolically perfect for the ritual.

In the end Cárdenas survives and loses control. His collection is ruined, Rodrigo is gone, the codex is scattered and Bonesy is still walking. Book one opens with a book broken on purpose and ends with the crew choosing to break it again.

Open questions: whether one leaf survives into book two, and whether the elder turns.
