The sale went easy. The buyer’s agent kept a room above a cooperage on the harbor front. A round Portuguese named Vaz who smelled of cloves. He took the finger in a square of black cloth and turned it over a candle. For a breath he forgot to smile. Then he counted out our pay. We went down the cooperage stair heavier in the coat than we’d been in a year.

We’d need some of that money to fit out for the island.

We kept our own weapons.

Crumble carries the blade Balam had made for him when his teaching was done. Shorter than a cutlass and straight, with a square tip and dyed leather on the grip. There are little square marks on the guard that Crumble can read and I can’t. He wears worked cloth and plate over his coat and a sash dyed red with a bug and a root. He wears them into Spanish rooms, too.

I cut the hides for my coat and stitched them down over canvas. I put them at the facings where salt eats wool and at the shoulders and forearms where steel generally finds me. Oiled dark, they shed water without making me sweat through my shirt. I killed every animal that went into the coat. From across a room all you’d see was an old coat.

And I do not carry a cutlass. I carry two Spanish blades, lighter than shipboard iron and quicker in a crowd. They are Toledo in shape, but the steel is older than that in its making: crucible steel, wootz by another name, high-carbon and watered in the Arab fashion, with dark lines running through it when the light catches right.

I learned the Spanish sword before I even knew what Tortuga was, in rooms with swept floors and boys whose fathers had paid for them to stand straight. They called it form. Form is useful until someone is trying to kill you. I learned the rest in back rooms with dirt on the floor and coin underfoot, where steel went where it could reach and no one called it unfair until he was opened.

Neither of us needed a new sword.

We went for stores. Powder, dry, off a Dutchman who kept it dry and charged for the keeping, and a fresh paper of flints to spark it. Cartridge wadding and a ball-mold and bar lead to cast our own. A coil of good hemp and a bolt of canvas for the sailmaker’s work that never ends. Pitch and oakum for her seams. Salt pork in the cask and turtle we’d take live and keep on its back in the bilge for fresh meat, and hard biscuit, and a netted sack of limes and another of oranges against the rot that loosens a man’s teeth and then takes the rest of him. A breaker of water sweetened with a little rum so it would keep. Crumble went off on his own account and came back with the things no Dutchman stocks, got from the Mayans who keep to the back lanes of any port on this coast. Ground cacao pressed into hard tablets, bitter as bilge and worth more than its weight to a man on a long sail. A twist of dried chiles. Pom, the tree-blood incense the old people burn for their dead. He didn’t tell me why he bought it. I didn’t ask.

And at a slop-seller’s stall with the lamps coming gold along the front, we bought Bonesy a hat. We thought nothing of it. A good one, black felt, broad, the brim stiff, a band of plain dark ribbon, the kind of thing any of the three of us might have wanted off the table, and Crumble paid for it the same as he’d paid for the limes.

Bonesy turned the hat once in his hands and put it on. A moment later he put two fingers to the brim and settled it a little lower.

I had been about to say something idle and did not. In all the time he had been with us, Bonesy had worn nothing, a cord at his neck and that the whole of it, and I had taken it for one of his ways, like the not sleeping, like the talking. It was not a way. A hermit crab will go bare and pinched its whole life before it climbs into a shell that is not its own, and the bare ones do not last. He had been the bare kind. He had stopped looking for a shell so long ago he had forgotten it was a thing a creature could have, and not one of us, in a year, had thought to hand him one.

He did not say anything about it, and neither did I. Some things you carry better unsaid.

We got him a broad belt and hung a short sword on it. He swore he had no use for the blade and kept it anyway. He drew the line at a coat and boots.

Crumble was already pulling his collar up, though it was warm. “I’m away up to find Domingo,” he said. “He’ll know if the old man left word with anyone but me. He keeps the ears of half the coast and lends them cheap to people he likes.” He looked at me. “He knew Balam. I can ask him about the book without having to tell him why I’m asking. He goes by Domingo down here because a Maya name in a Spanish port is a thing men collect you for. To me he’s not Domingo.” He said the other name, soft, with the finish I do not have. “Don’t follow me up. He won’t open with strangers by.” He grinned. “And the two of you are a great deal of strange.”

“We’ll find a tavern and lower rum,” I said. “You’ll know us by the wreckage.”

He went up the hill into the dark of the town, and I watched him go until I couldn’t, the way I always do.
