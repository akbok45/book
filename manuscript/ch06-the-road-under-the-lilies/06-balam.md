Crumble did not answer him straight off.

He stood there with his shirt off in the grey, and his arms at his sides, and looked at Balam the way I have seen men look at land after a long passage. As if he did not trust it to stay.

“Yes,” he said.

“Of course you are.” Balam did not sound surprised. He sounded tired. “What have you found?”

“A leaf. Out of a cave on the half moon, behind a pool.” Crumble’s voice was steady. “A road painted on it, going north up the coast. Seven days. It stops by the long island. There’s a sign at the end of it.”

“Show me.”

Crumble did not have it. It was back in Amara’s house, on its oiled cloth. He looked at his empty hands as if it might be in them.

Then he crouched and drew it in the grey with his finger. The grey took the line the way wet sand does. A face, side on, with its mouth open and curls coming out of it.

Balam looked down at it a long time.

“I looked for that face for twenty years,” he said. “I never found it.” He crouched beside Crumble and put his finger on the curls. “That is not breath. That is song.”

“A place that sings.”

“A stone that sings. On the long island, on the south point, where the old women went in their canoes to ask the goddess for children. My grandfather heard it once, when he was a boy.” He looked up at Crumble. “It sings when the north wind comes. Only then. Go in a norther.”

“That’s all?”

“That is all I have.” Balam stood up and wiped his finger on his thigh. “The rest of it you will have to find. Amara will know more than I do. She always did.”

Crumble stood up too.

“Who was he?” he said.

Balam did not pretend not to know who he meant. He looked away, toward the place in the grey where the trees had been, where the man in the cloak had walked in among them.

“A student,” he said. “My first.”

“And me?”

Balam looked back at him.

“My last,” he said.

Crumble’s jaw worked. He looked down at his own hands, and then up again.

“There was something in your hands,” he said. “Before. When the bell rang. There was something that should have been in them, and it wasn’t there.”

“Yes.”

“You never taught me that.”

Balam did not answer. He looked at the place in the grey where the trees had been, for a long time. Then he looked back at Crumble, and he did not say anything at all, and that was the answer.

Crumble nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he has been handed something heavy and is finding where to hold it.

Then he said the two words.

He said them quietly, in Maya, the way he says the day at dawn. A number, and a day.

Balam’s face changed. He shut his eyes.

“She sent you with that,” he said. “The first time. You came up the river in a canoe you could not paddle, with those two words, and you said them so badly I thought you were asking for water.”

“You let me in.”

“I let you in.” He opened his eyes. “Is she well?”

“She keeps the whole island,” Crumble said. “All of them. Nobody tells anybody what to do, and they all go to her.”

Balam did not say anything for a while.

“That sounds like her mother,” he said.

⸻

He turned to me.

“You,” he said.

I stood up straighter. I could not have said why.

“He asked you to watch his back.”

I looked at Crumble. He had asked me that on the step, with the lilies in a basket at our feet. Balam could not have heard it. He had been dead a year and four months.

“I said I would,” I said.

“Say it again.”

“I’ll watch his back.”

Balam looked at me a while longer, at my face and my hands, the way he had before. Then he nodded once, as if a bargain had been struck and he had got the better of it.

⸻

Far off, so far I thought I had made it up, I heard the drum.

It was one stroke, and then nothing, and then another. Slower than it had been on the lake. Fainter.

Bonesy was sitting in Crumble’s shirt. When I looked at him, the skin on the back of his one good hand was coming away. It went off him in flakes, grey, like ash off a burned log when the wind gets at it. The cheek he had got back was going the same way.

He saw me look.

“Going off,” he said. “Like a fish left in the sun.”

The grey round us was getting lighter. It was not the light of a sun coming up. It was a pale light with no place it came from, like the light under water in the morning when you look up from the bottom of a bay. And there was a smell in it, faint, sweet and heavy, that I knew.

The lilies.

Balam smelt it too. He lifted his head.

“They are opening,” he said.

He looked at Crumble. He put his hand up once more, and this time he did not lay it on his face. He put it on his shoulder and gripped it, hard, the way Crumble grips. The way Crumble has gripped my hand across a leaf with his knuckles going white.

He let go first.

Then he turned round and walked away into the grey.

He walked the way he had walked across the open ground to us, not hurrying. The grey took him a little at a time. His feet first, then his legs.

Crumble took a step after him and stopped.

When Balam was nearly gone, he stopped too. He did not turn all the way round. He turned his head, and lifted his hand, the one that had held the net needle, and held it up.

Then the grey had all of him.

Crumble stood looking at the place where he had been.

Bonesy got up off the shirt. It took him two tries. He came and stood beside Crumble, half skin and half bone, with the flakes coming off him, and put his hand on Crumble’s arm.

“Come on, Crum,” he said. “This way.”
