I opened my eyes and looked down. There was red earth under me. I was lying in a furrow between two rows of maize.

I put a hand to my wrist. Nothing was holding it.

I remembered the torches on the shore. The fisherman called after me to remember his canoe.

Amara leaned across from her canoe and handed me a gourd. I gripped it in both hands and took a swallow. It smelled sour. I could taste the honey and the bitterness of the bark. There was a smell of lilies on the rim. Crumble had emptied his and was watching me. I tipped mine up and drank the rest.

The antler struck the shell. Bonesy took my wrist. His fingers were cold.

I sat up. The maize was higher than my head. There were beans climbing the stalks and drops of water on the leaves. Somewhere in the field a dove called. I listened for the drum.

Crumble was sitting up in the next furrow. He had dew in his hair and red earth down one side of his face. He was looking past me, up the row.

I turned. A man stood with his back to us. He was bare to the waist with a cloth tied round his hips. Black hair hung down the back of his neck.

“Fellas,” he said.

I knew him at once. I still looked past the man to see if Bonesy was standing behind him.

“Bonesy?” Crumble said. He was on his feet before the man turned.

He was short and a little bowed at the knees. He had brown skin and a nose that bent to one side. Two of his front teeth were gone. His eyes were brown. I kept looking at them. I’d seen daylight through those holes a hundred times.

“God’s teeth,” Crumble said.

Bonesy put a hand to his mouth. He ran a finger over the gap. Then he spread his fingers and looked down at the skin between them.

He drew a breath. I could see his ribs push the skin out and let it fall again. In a year of sleeping beside him I’d never heard him breathe.

I put a hand on his shoulder.

“You feel warm,” I said. I left my hand where it was.

He looked at it. “Don’t get ideas.”

Crumble laughed. So did I. I took my hand off him.

Bonesy knelt between the rows. He pushed his hands into the earth and shut his eyes. I could see the dirt work in under his nails. We sat there with him till he was ready to get up.

He opened his eyes and took his hands out of the ground. He wiped them on his thighs. He looked down at himself, at the cloth round his loins.

He pulled the front of it out and looked under it.

“Huh,” he said.

He let it go.

“Smaller than I remember.”

I couldn’t look at Crumble after that. Every time I did we started laughing again.

His hat was lying in the furrow behind him. It was the same hat, the black felt, with dew on the brim. He picked it up and shook the dew off it and put it on.

It sat lower on him now. There was a head under it.

We began walking out through the maize. Bonesy led, and Crumble and I followed.

The maize brushed my shoulders on both sides and left them wet.

The row came out onto a path. It was beaten earth, red like the rest, running along the edge of the field.

Two children came running out of the maize ahead of us.

They were a boy and a girl, eight or nine years old, and they did not belong there. They had English faces, burnt red across the nose. The boy was barefoot and had a stick held up over his head like a cutlass. He was shouting that he was captain and she was to strike her colours. The girl ran after him with her black hair flying out behind her. She was laughing, and then she was singing at him.

As the girl passed us, Crumble began singing with her, low enough that I almost missed it.

The children ran across the path and into the maize on the other side. The stalks shook where they had gone in, and then stopped.

Crumble had stopped in the path. He stood looking at the place where the maize had closed behind them.

He kept singing after she was gone.

Then he went on faster, ahead of us.

Bonesy kept his eyes on the path and did not change his pace. I kept mine with him.

I turned and looked back across the field we had come through. Beyond the maize stood the academy.

My old fencing master stood at the top of the steps with his cane under one arm, exactly as he had every morning when I was a boy.

He looked down at me and shook his head.

I blinked. There was only maize behind us. When I turned forward again, Crumble was well ahead of us on the path. He was still looking into the maize where the children had gone.

The path rose onto a low hill. Bonesy caught up with Crumble and put an arm round his shoulders as we walked.

The maize came apart around us in flecks of grey. The flecks drifted to either side of the path and gathered into hedges. Red earth darkened under our feet until it was mud. Then rain began hitting my shoulders.

Bonesy still had his arm round Crumble. Crumble was looking down the lane.

“I know this lane,” he said.

The hedges thinned as we walked. Green fields spread out on either side, and a village came into view below us. The nearest house had a low stone wall round its yard. Its door was open. A woman was singing inside.
