There was dew on my face.

I opened my eyes. It was not yet morning. Over me there were long ragged leaves, black against a sky gone the grey of a gull’s back, with the last stars still in it. The leaves dripped. A drop came down off the end of one and hit me in the eye.

I lay still. I did not know where I was.

The air was cool, cooler than the lake had ever been. Mist lay knee high along the ground between the rows, and I was lying under it. Somewhere a dove was calling, the same three notes over and over. Far off a dog barked once and stopped.

There was no drum.

The drum was the last I had of the night. Before it, the whole island on the shore with its torches, and the grey fisherman calling after me to remember his canoe. Amara leaning across from her canoe with the gourds. The drink, thick and sour, with the bark bitter under the sweet and the smell of the lilies under that. I had drunk all of mine because Crumble had drunk all of his and was looking at me. Then the first stroke of the antler on the shell, and Bonesy’s hand coming over and closing on my wrist, the bones cold.

Then the going down. It was the pool again. The dark and the weight, and the light going brown, then green, then nothing. I had waited the whole way for the false wall and the step and the air. There had been no wall.

I put my hand to my wrist. Nothing was holding it.

I sat up.

The mist came up to my chest. Over the top of it the light was coming grey and then white along one edge of the sky. I was lying in a furrow. The earth under me was red and soft and wet. On both sides the maize stood in rows higher than a man, with the cobs fat in their husks and beans climbing the stalks. Squash vines ran along the ground between the rows under the mist.

Crumble was sitting up in the next furrow. He had dew in his hair and red earth down one side of his face. He was looking past me, up the row.

I turned round.

A man was standing in the row with his back to us.

He was short. He came no higher than my chin. His legs were bowed, the way a man’s legs go who has carried loads since he could walk. He was brown all over and bare to the waist, with a cloth round his loins and his black hair tied up on the back of his head. He stood with his arms hanging and did not move.

“Fellas,” he said.

The voice was high and rasped, the same as it had always been.

He turned round.

His face was broad and flat across the cheekbones, and lined. There was a band across his forehead, a hand’s width above his brows, where the skin was paler and worn smooth. When he opened his mouth to say something else I saw that he had lost two teeth at the front, top and bottom.

He did not say anything else. He put both hands up to his face.

He felt it the way a blind man feels a stranger. The forehead first, and the band across it. Then the cheeks, and the nose, which had been broken once and set badly. Then his mouth. He put his fingers into his mouth and felt along the teeth that were there and the gaps where they were not.

He took his hands away and looked at them.

They were a farmer’s hands. Short in the finger and broad in the palm, cracked across the knuckles, with the nails worn down to the quick. He turned them over. He looked at the backs and then at the palms again, a long time, the way a man looks at a chart of water he has sailed and never seen drawn.

I looked at the maize.

Crumble did not. He sat in his furrow and watched him.

Bonesy got down on his knees between the rows. He sat back on his heels and pushed both hands into the earth, to the wrist, and left them there. Then he closed his eyes. The sun had come up over the maize and it was on his face.

The dove called its three notes. Water dripped off the leaves.

He sat like that a long while. Neither of us said anything. I picked a bit of red earth off my sleeve and rolled it between my fingers and did not look at him.

Then he opened his eyes and took his hands out of the ground. He wiped them on his thighs. He looked down at himself, at the cloth round his loins.

He pulled the front of it out and looked under it.

“Huh,” he said.

He let it go.

“Smaller than I remember.”

Crumble laughed. It came out of him like a cough, and he put his hand over his mouth, and it came out round the hand. Then I was laughing too. Bonesy sat on his heels in the furrow and looked at the two of us, and he grinned with his broken teeth.

His hat was lying in the furrow behind him. It was the same hat, the black felt, with dew on the brim. He picked it up and shook the dew off it and put it on.

It sat lower on him now. There was a head under it.

He got up and went along the row. We went after him.

The maize brushed my shoulders on both sides and left them wet. A cob hung out into the row at the height of my face, fat and yellow at the end where the husk had split. I reached up to break it off.

My hand closed on nothing.

The cob was there. I could see the dew on it. My fingers went through it the way they go through the smoke over a fire, and came out the other side dry.

I stood there with my hand up.

Bonesy had stopped and was looking back at me.

“Can’t,” he said.

“You put your hands in the dirt.”

“Mine.” He looked down the row at the maize. “It’s mine. You’re only visiting.”

Crumble put his hand out to a leaf as he passed. It went through. He took the hand back and put it on his sash.

The row came out onto a path. It was beaten earth, red like the rest, running along the edge of the field. On the far side of the path three jars stood in the grass.

They were big jars, as high as my waist, round in the belly and narrow at the neck. Each neck was stopped and sealed over with wax. They were the same red clay and the same shape as the jar we had carried out of the cave under the half moon. Only these were new. The wax on them was yellow and still soft at the edges.

Crumble saw them too. He looked at them, and at me.

Bonesy walked past them. He did not look at them. As he passed his hand went up to the pale band on his forehead and rubbed it, the way a man rubs a place a strap has worn. Then he dropped the hand and went on.

Neither of us said anything.

⸻

There was a house at the end of the path.

It stood where the field gave out, under a tree, with a thatched roof gone black on the underside and smoke coming up through the thatch. There was a fence of sticks round a little yard. A dog lay in the dust of the yard with its chin on its paws.

A woman was kneeling in the doorway at a grinding stone.

She had her back half to us. She was rolling the stone back and forth over the maize with both hands, leaning her weight into it, and she was singing. It was a low song with no tune to it to speak of, the kind a woman sings to keep time at work.

I understood every word.

*The rain comes over the hill. The rain goes over the hill. The maize stands. My man is late. The maize stands.*

It was not Spanish and it was not English. I knew that much. I did not know what it was. I only knew what she was saying, as plain as if she had said it in my ear.

I looked at Crumble. He had heard it too. His lips were moving with it.

Bonesy had stopped in the middle of the path.

He stood with his back to the house. He did not turn his head toward it. He stood so still that the dog in the yard lifted its head and looked at the empty path where we were, and put its head down again.

The woman sang it again from the beginning. *The rain comes over the hill.*

Bonesy turned round and walked the other way.

He walked fast, the bowed legs going quick in the red dust. He went back past the jars and along the edge of the field. He did not look round to see if we were coming.

We went after him.

I looked back once. The woman was still at her stone in the doorway, rolling it back and forth. She had not looked up. She could not see us. There was nobody on the path to see.

Crumble did not look back. He walked beside me with his hand on his sash and his eyes on Bonesy’s back. He did not ask, and I did not ask, and Bonesy did not say.

⸻

The field ran on a long way. Then it stopped.

It did not end the way a field ends, in scrub or forest or a fence. The last row of maize stood there, green and wet, and past it the ground was something else.

It was a lane. It ran between two banks with hedges on top of them, close and dark and dripping. The sky over it was low and grey. Rain was falling on it, a thin cold rain that made no sound, and the ruts in the lane were full of brown water. The air that came off it was cold. It smelt of wet stone and dung and coal smoke, the way the air smells in a town in a country where it rains more days than it does not.

Bonesy stopped at the last row of maize. He stood with the sun on his back and the cold coming at his face, and he looked at the lane.

Then he looked round at Crumble.

“His,” he said to me.

Crumble had stopped beside me. He was looking down the lane, past the hedges, to where it bent and went out of sight. The rain was coming down on the edge of his boots. His hand had come off his sash. The fingers of it were moving a little, the way they move when he is counting the day.

“I know this lane,” he said.
