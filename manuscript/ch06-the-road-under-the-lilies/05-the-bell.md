I came up in a river.

It was brown and warm and slow, and it came to my chest. Trees leaned out over it from both banks and met overhead, and the light came down through them green. Somewhere upstream a woman was laughing.

Crumble came up beside me. Bonesy was already standing in the shallows with the water running off his hat.

I looked at him and then I looked away. There was next to nothing left of his face. The skin was over it still, but it was the skin of an onion, and the bone showed white through all of it. His hands were bone with a shine on them.

“Where,” I said.

“His,” Bonesy said, and tipped the hat at Crumble.

Crumble did not hear him. He was looking up the bank.

⸻

There was a village at the top of it.

It was bigger than Amara’s island. There were thirty houses or more, set back from the river under the trees, with smoke coming up through the thatch. Gardens behind them. Canoes pulled up along the bank below. A dog. Two women at the grinding stones outside a doorway, talking over their work and not looking at it.

On the bank, a little way along from us, a man was sitting on an upturned canoe mending a net.

He had the net across his knees and a bone needle in his fingers. He worked fast, without looking, the way a man works who has mended a thousand nets. He was perhaps forty. He was broad in the shoulders and going grey at the temples. There was nothing about him to look at twice.

Crumble had stopped breathing.

“His hands,” he said.

I looked at the hands. The needle went in and out.

“That’s how he held the stick,” Crumble said. “When he hit my ankle. Every morning.” He took a step up the bank and stopped. “He’s young.”

I had never seen Balam. I had seen Crumble wear his blade and say his name and stand in a lake at dawn for him. I looked at the man on the canoe for a long time and could not make him into any of that. He was a fisherman mending a net.

A woman came down the bank to him with a child on her hip.

The child was perhaps two. She had a fist in her mouth and her other hand in the woman’s hair. The woman said something to Balam and he laughed, and put the net down, and held his arms up. The child went to him.

She looked over his shoulder, straight down the bank at the three of us. She had a long look for a child that size. She did not smile.

Crumble made a sound.

I knew that look. I had seen it over the top of a canoe on a lake, and over a burning rail, and across a leaf by lamplight. It was Amara’s.

⸻

Somebody called out in the village. A man had come out of the trees on the far side of the houses, walking.

He was alone and on foot. He wore a plain brown cloak with the hood up, though the morning was warm. People turned to look at him, and when they saw who it was they called out to him. A boy ran to him and took his hand. An old woman came out of a doorway with her hands on her hips and said something to him, scolding, and pinched his arm through the cloak to show how thin it was. Everyone near her laughed.

The man in the cloak laughed with them.

I could hear him laugh. I could not see his face. The hood was forward, and he kept it turned away from us, and when he turned his head it was always away. I saw the back of the hood. I saw his hands.

They were clean. Long and white, with the nails pared. They had never hauled a line in their life. I disliked him for them before I had heard him say a word, and I knew why I did, and it did not stop me.

Balam had stood up on the bank with the child in his arms. He was looking at the man in the cloak.

He did not look glad.

Then I saw the boats.

They were three launches, long and low, coming down the far bank of the river under the trees where the light did not reach. They were pulling slow and quiet with the oars muffled. There was a glint in the bow of each one, and then another. Morions. Pike heads. The slow match of matchlocks, red in the green dark.

I knew how to come up on a village from the water. It is my trade. I had done it myself, coming up under a bank where the watch looks the wrong way, while the people in the village look at a man walking in out of the trees.

I shouted.

I shouted it in English and in Spanish, and I put my arm out and pointed at the boats. A woman walked past me with a water jar on her head. She did not turn.

“They can’t hear you,” Crumble said. He did not take his eyes off Balam.

Balam had seen them too.

He gave the child to the woman. He said one word to her, low, and she went white and went backward up the bank with the child held against her. Then he walked out into the open ground between the houses, toward the man in the cloak.

He stopped a few paces from him.

“You brought them here,” he said.

I understood him. I did not know his tongue and I understood every word, the way I had understood the song in the maize.

The man in the cloak did not answer.

He put his hand in under the cloak, and brought it out again.

There was a bell in it. A little black bell, no bigger than a man’s fist, with a black clapper. It looked like the bell a priest rings at the altar, except that it was black all over, and it did not shine.

He rang it once.

⸻

It was one note. It was low, lower than a bell that size had any business making. It did not ring out and fall away the way a bell’s note goes. It went out and stayed.

The smoke from the cook fires stopped leaning. It stood straight up into the trees, every column of it, as if the air had set hard round it.

Beside me, Bonesy went down on his knees.

He did not make a sound. He put his bone hands out on the ground in front of him, as if the ground had tipped. The last of the skin went off his face the way frost goes off a stone when the sun gets onto it. Then the bones of his arms came apart at the elbows, and he went down onto his face, and the rest of him came apart after.

It was a heap of bones in the path with a hat on top.

“Bonesy,” I said.

The heap did not say anything. The note went on.

⸻

The soldiers came up out of the launches.

There were forty of them, perhaps more. Morions and breastplates, pikes and matchlocks, and two officers with swords. They came up the bank at a run and into the open ground, and the village came apart in front of them.

I watched the strange go out of it, one person at a time.

A woman ran down to the river and stood in the shallows and breathed out hard over the water, at the canoes, the way you breathe on a fire to start it. The canoes should have gone. I saw in her face that they should have gone. Nothing moved. She breathed again. A soldier came down the bank behind her.

A big man with his back gone hard and ridged like a turtle’s planted himself in a doorway with his arms out. The first pike went into him as if he had been any man.

A boy no older than the one who slept against my knee on Amara’s island stood in the path with his hands up in front of him. He was doing something. He was trying to. Nothing came. He looked at his hands, surprised.

They were not lambs. I want that set down. The village fought with what it had: digging sticks, stones, fish spears, an adze, a pot of boiling water off a fire. I saw a soldier go down with a fish spear through the gap under his arm where the breastplate does not reach, and another go down with his face broken in by a stone. I saw an old woman with a digging stick put a soldier on his back in the dirt.

But there were forty of them, and they had pikes and powder, and the strange had gone out of everything.

Balam had not moved from the open ground. He put his two hands together in front of his mouth, cupped, and spoke into them. I did not hear the words. His mouth moved fast and sure, the way a man says something he has said a thousand times.

He opened his hands. There was nothing in them.

He looked down at them.

Crumble’s face had gone the colour of ash. I do not think he had known, until then, that there was anything that should have been in Balam’s hands.

Then Balam had a blade. I did not see where it came from. It was short and broad and squared at the end, and he went into the soldiers with it, and I knew the way he went because I had watched Crumble go that way on a dozen decks. Only Balam did it better. Every step Crumble takes, Balam took first, and cleaner. He killed three of them before they could bring their pikes round.

Beside me Crumble watched his own forms done better than he had ever done them.

⸻

The woman with the child had not got far. She was standing at the top of the bank in front of a house, with the child behind her legs and a fish spear in her hands.

A soldier came at her with a pike.

She did not run. She put the fish spear into the meat of his arm and he roared and dropped the pike, and another came up behind him.

I will not set down the rest of it. She was a long time going down. The child stood behind her legs and watched.

Balam came up the bank.

He did not get there before it was done. He went past the soldier who had done it and the soldier stepped back from him. He went down on his knees by the woman and put his hand on her face. Then he picked up the child.

He stood up with the child against his chest, and the blade still in his other hand, and the pikes came round to him. Six of them, from every side.

The man in the cloak had walked up the bank behind them. He stood a little way off, with his back to us.

He lifted one clean hand.

The pikes stopped. They stayed where they were, a hand’s width from Balam, and did not go in.

Balam looked at the man in the cloak over the heads of the soldiers. I could see his face. I could not see the other one.

“I taught you everything I knew,” Balam said.

The man in the cloak let his hand fall.

“That was your first mistake,” he said.

He turned and walked away through the burning houses. He walked back into the trees the way he had come out of them, alone, on foot, with the hood up. The soldiers lowered their pikes and went after him.

Nobody ever saw his face. Not the village, and not us.

The note stopped.

⸻

It did not fade. It stopped, the way a rope stops when it is cut.

The smoke leaned over again in the wind.

At my feet the heap in the path moved. The skull rolled a little way off the pile and fetched up against my boot with the hat still on it.

“Fellas,” it said.

It was his voice, gone small as a cricket’s.

I went down on my knees. I could not think what to do with my hands.

“Pick me up,” the skull said. “Mind the hat.”

Crumble came and knelt on the other side of the pile. He pulled his shirt off over his head and spread it on the ground. We put the bones in it, all of them, the long ones and the little ones from the hands and the feet. The skull went in last, on top, with the hat over it. Crumble tied the corners of the shirt. He lifted it in both arms against his chest, the way you lift a child.

“Easy,” the skull said through the cloth. “Easy. I’m in a lot of pieces.”

Crumble stood up with him.

The village was burning. All of it. The thatch went up in sheets and the smoke went into the trees. Nobody in it was moving, except one man.

Balam was standing in the open ground in the middle of it, with the child in his arms. Her face was in his neck. He had dropped the blade. He was looking at nothing.

Then he lifted his head.

He looked across the open ground, through the smoke, at the place where we were standing. At Crumble, with his shirt off and his arms full of bones.

He looked straight at him. Not through him, the way the woman with the water jar had looked through me. At him.

Crumble did not move.

Balam’s face changed. It was a small change. It was the face of a man who has been waiting at a door a long time and hears a step on the path.

He knew him.
