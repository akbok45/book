The old woman with the pipe opened the hives on the first morning.

Amara had gone to her door the night before. I saw it from our step. Amara stood in the doorway a long while and talked low, and the old woman sat inside with her pipe and listened. Then she nodded once. Amara touched her arm and went on to the next house. I stayed on the step till she was halfway to the landing. A lamp would come to a door and stay there while she talked. At one house a woman came out with her lamp and went ahead of Amara to wake her neighbor.

By first light the old woman was at the first log. She had a smoking pot of bark in one hand and her pipe in her teeth. The log hung under the thatch on two cords, and she was picking the clay out of one end of it with her thumbnail. The women Amara had spoken to were there with gourds. One held the log steady while the old woman worked.

The bees came out round her fingers. They were small and black, smaller than a fly. They walked over her knuckles and onto her wrist.

I stopped a good way off.

She saw me stop. She took the pipe out of her mouth and said something, and the women at the grinding stones laughed.

Amara came up beside me and put her hand on my shoulder. I felt the warmth of it through my shirt.

“She says come closer. They don’t bite Spaniards.”

“What do they bite?”

Amara asked her. The old woman answered round the pipe.

“Nothing,” Amara said.

I looked down at her hand. I’d forgotten to move.

I came closer.

The bees walked on her arm and did nothing. They got in her hair. One of them walked across the back of my hand and I held still till it had gone.

She put her whole hand into the log.

She brought it out full of little round pots of wax, dark yellow, each the size of a musket ball and stuck to the next. There was no comb in it. The pots hung off her fingers like grapes, and she broke them open over a gourd one at a time with her thumb. The honey that came out was thin and dark and smelt of flowers I did not know.

She did not look up at me again. She reached into the log for more.

⸻

It went on like that the whole day, and all of it went through Amara.

She went from house to house. She did not carry anything. She would stop at a door and say a few words, and a man would put down his net, and by the time she had gone round the next house he was coming up the path with a jar.

I asked Bonesy what she was saying.

He listened at the next door.

“She asked if he had a big jar,” he said. “He said his wife had. She asked if his wife would lend it.”

“That’s all?”

“He asked what it was for.” Bonesy listened a while longer. “She said for her father’s student. And his brothers.”

I looked at the door. The man had already gone in for the jar.

It was the biggest on the island, as high as my hip, with a crack mended in black gum round its belly. Two men carried it down to the house by the water and set it in the shade. The old woman brought the gourds of honey. Women came with water from the lake and poured it in. Somebody stirred it with a paddle.

On a ship somebody is always telling somebody. A mate shouts, a bosun’s cane comes down, a man moves. Here there was Amara at a door, asking, and the whole island answering her.

She came down at noon and looked into the jar. She did not say anything to us. A girl went off and came back with more water.

⸻

At supper the grey fisherman asked if I could still feel where he’d hit me.

“When I laugh,” I said.

He was grinning when the old man with the roots spoke. Two men across the fire turned to look at me.

Bonesy leaned in and put it into English, low.

“He says the road is for the island’s dead. He says it’s no road for a Spaniard.”

The old man spat into the fire.

Nobody answered him. I felt it in my belly again, the same tightness I’d had in the canoe on the way to the island. I put my bowl down. My hand went to my hilt. I took it off again and laid it on my knee.

Amara set her bowl down beside mine. She answered the old man from where she sat. He spoke over her once. She waited for him to finish and went on.

Bonesy bent his head to mine. “She says you’re both going. She’ll keep the drum for you both.”

The old man shifted his roots in the dust under his stool.

Across the fire the grey fisherman stood up. He said a few words to the old man. Then he looked at me.

“My canoe says the Spaniard’s first ashore,” he said in Spanish.

I smiled at him. He showed me his teeth.

Nobody spoke until the man with the jaguar’s shadow reached behind him and took up his bow. He laid it across his knees and said a word I knew.

“Crumble.”

“He’ll put the bow against the canoe,” Bonesy said.

The fisherman looked at the bow. He took a step round the fire and held out his hand. The other man got up to take it. His shadow went up the wall behind him with its tail out straight.

That brought the men in from the edge of the fire. One held up two fingers for Crumble. A man beside him held up three for me. Bonesy had them say what they were putting up and stopped the second man till the first had finished. Smoked fish at first. Then a length of fishing line and a knife with the handle half worn away. The knife went down on a stone where we could all see it.

I looked at Crumble. “Two fish says I’m out before the canoe touches sand.”

“You’ll be wet to the waist when I pass you.”

“Then put two on it.”

He put his bowl down. “Two.”

Bonesy bent over the dirt and drew a line. There were so many calling to him by then that he had to ask them which side they meant twice.

“Both of us have to come back for it to count,” I said.

Bonesy gave them that in Maya. The fisherman nodded and so did the man with the bow.

Crumble looked across the fire at me. “Aye. Both of us.”

The old man grunted. Then the smallest of his grandchildren got up from beside him and came round the fire and sat down against my leg. She had my coin on its cord round her neck. She sat there turning it in her fingers.

The old man looked at the coin, and at me. He did not say anything else.

Amara took up her bowl again. She did not look at us once. Across the fire Crumble was looking at her.

⸻

By the next morning the bark was steeping in the jar.

The children cut the lilies.

I saw them from the shore that afternoon. They were out in the shallows at the south end of the island, where the lilies grow thick over the water. There were eight or nine of them, up to their waists, with knives and a basket floating between them on a cord.

Bonesy was out there with them.

He was in to his ribs. The water went in at his collarbone and came out between the bones of his back. He held the basket while they cut. The lilies were white with yellow in the middle, as wide as my two hands, and the children laid them in the basket with their stems.

The girl with the bark on her skin cut the most. She went from lily to lily with her knife and never cut one that was not all the way open.

I asked Bonesy about it after.

“Only the open ones,” he said. “She says the shut ones are sleeping.”

Crumble helped me carry the basket up to the house by the water. We settled it in the shade outside the door.

“What are you going to ask him?” I said.

“About the sign.”

“I know about the sign.”

He bent to pick a leaf off one of the stems. He got it off in two pieces.

“I don’t know what I’ll say to him,” he said. “I knew what to say when he was alive.”

“Hell of a reunion,” I said. “We could at least bring him a drink.”

Crumble looked down at his hands. His mouth moved a little.

I sat on the step. “I wonder if I could see my father down there.”

“Would you want to?”

“Aye.” I rubbed a bit of dirt off my boot. “I don’t know if he’d be proud of what I’ve become.”

Crumble sat beside me. He dropped the two pieces of leaf between his feet.

“I’d wanted to go back and show Balam I could manage on my own,” he said. “Here I am needing his help again.”

“I’ll ask him,” he said. “Give me a minute with him first.”

“Aye.”

“Watch my back, will you? I doubt all the dead are as friendly as Bonesy.”

I looked at him. “I’ll be there.”

Bonesy came up the path and sat on the step below us. A bit of lily stem was stuck between two of his ribs. I pulled it out for him.

“You two coming to eat?” he said.

“In a minute,” I said.

He stayed on the step with us.

We left the lilies by the door. That night Amara put them into the jar.

⸻

The claw woman brought the drum on the third day.

It was a turtle’s shell, the whole back of a big river turtle, scrubbed white inside. She carried it under her claw against her hip, the way she carried her water jar. She put it down in front of Amara in the shade of the house by the water and sat down across from it.

Amara picked up a deer’s antler from beside her and struck the shell near the edge.

It made a hollow sound, flat and short, like a hand slapped on a hull.

She struck it again near the middle. That was lower, and it went on a while longer.

The claw woman shook her head.

She leaned over and tapped the shell with the tip of her claw. Here, and here, and here. Then she sat back. Amara struck it where the claw had been. The sound came out longer and lower. It went on under the floor and into my feet.

The claw woman nodded.

Amara struck the shell slow, again and again, at the same place. After a while I found I was breathing in time with it. I made myself stop.

“How long do you play it?” I said.

“Till you come back.”

“And if we don’t?”

“Till the lilies open.” She struck the shell again. “Then I stop.”

I looked at the jar behind her in the shade. It had a cloth over it and a stone on the cloth. It had begun to smell, sour and sweet and bitter all together, and the flies had found it.

⸻

That evening Amara set two gourds beside the jar. Bonesy came to stand beside her with his hat in his hand.

“Which canoe are we taking?” he said.

“The middle one.” She looked up at him. “The drink won’t take you, Bonesy.”

“Oh, I’ll be there.” He was smiling when he said it.

“Between us, then,” I said. “You can tell them who gets ashore first.”

He went down to the water. I went with him. The three canoes were pulled up side by side. He got into the middle one and lay on his back with his hands folded on his ribs and his hat over his face.

“Like this,” he said from under the hat.

The children came and stood round the canoe and looked in at him. One of them put a lily on his chest. He did not move.

Crumble came down the path and stood beside me. He looked at Bonesy in the canoe with the lily on him.

“Comfortable?” Crumble said.

“Very.”

Crumble looked down at him a while. Then he stepped into the canoe and lay down on the bottom boards beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and folded his hands on his chest.

The children shrieked. One of them ran for another lily and put it on him.

“Like this?” Crumble said.

“Hands lower. You look like a bishop.”

Crumble moved his hands lower.

Up at the house by the water Amara was sitting in the doorway with the antler in her lap. She was watching the two of them in the canoe. She did not laugh with the children. She watched a long time.

⸻

I could not sleep that night.

The other two were in their hammocks. Crumble lay on his back and did not move, but his breathing was wrong for sleep. Bonesy lay in his with his hat on his chest. He does not sleep. He lies still so we can.

I got up and sat on the step of the house with my back to the post.

The lake was black. The fire at the house by the water had burned down to red, and somebody was sitting by it. I could not see who. Across the water the forest made its noise.

I took the purse out of my coat.

I tipped the coins out into my palm and counted them by the feel. The finger money, what was left after the sloop’s stores and the powder. I counted them twice. I put them back in the purse and took them out again and counted them a third time, and it came out the same.

I held them a while.

Behind me in the dark a hammock creaked.

“How many,” Bonesy said.

I told him.

“Same as this morning?”

“Same.”

He did not say anything else. After a while the hammock creaked again as he settled.

I put the coins back in the purse and pulled the string tight and put it in my coat, on the inside, over the ribs. I sat on the step till the fire by the water had gone out.
