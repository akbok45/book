The old woman with the pipe opened the hives on the first morning.

Amara had gone to her door the night before. I saw it from our step. Amara stood in the doorway a long while and talked low, and the old woman sat inside with her pipe and listened. Then she nodded once. Amara touched her arm and went on to the next house.

By first light the old woman was at the first log. She had a smoking pot of bark in one hand and her pipe in her teeth. The log hung under the thatch on two cords, and she was picking the clay out of one end of it with her thumbnail.

The bees came out round her fingers. They were small and black, smaller than a fly. They walked over her knuckles and onto her wrist.

I stopped a good way off.

She saw me stop. She took the pipe out of her mouth and said something, and the women at the grinding stones laughed.

Bonesy came up the path behind me with a child on each shoulder.

“She says come closer,” he said. “She says they don’t bite Spaniards.”

“What do they bite?”

He asked her. She answered round the pipe.

“Nothing,” Bonesy said. “She says they’ve got no sting. She says you’re standing there like a man waiting to be shot.”

I came closer.

The bees walked on her arm and did nothing. They got in her hair. One of them walked across the back of my hand and I held still till it had gone.

She put her whole hand into the log.

She brought it out full of little round pots of wax, dark yellow, each the size of a musket ball and stuck to the next. There was no comb in it. The pots hung off her fingers like grapes, and she broke them open over a gourd one at a time with her thumb. The honey that came out was thin and dark and smelt of flowers I did not know.

She did not look up at me again. She reached into the log for more.

⸻

It went on like that the whole day, and all of it went through Amara.

She went from house to house. She did not carry anything. She would stop at a door and say a few words, and a man would put down his net, and by the time she had gone round the next house he was coming up the path with a jar.

I asked Bonesy what she was saying.

He listened at the next door.

“She asked if he had a big jar,” he said. “He said his wife had. She asked if his wife would lend it.”

“That’s all?”

“He asked what it was for.” Bonesy listened a while longer. “She said for her father’s student. And his brothers.”

I looked at the door. The man had already gone in for the jar.

It was the biggest on the island, as high as my hip, with a crack mended in black gum round its belly. Two men carried it down to the house by the water and set it in the shade. The old woman brought the gourds of honey. Women came with water from the lake and poured it in. Somebody stirred it with a paddle.

On a ship somebody is always telling somebody. A mate shouts, a bosun’s cane comes down, a man moves. Here there was Amara at a door, asking, and the whole island answering her.

She came down at noon and looked into the jar. She did not say anything to us. A girl went off and came back with more water.

⸻

That evening the old man with the roots would not have it.

He said it at the fire after we had eaten. He said it loud, so the houses at the far end could hear. He did not look at me while he said it. He did not need to.

Bonesy leaned in and put it into English, low.

“He says the road is for the island’s dead. He says it’s no road for a Spaniard.”

The old man spat into the fire.

Nobody answered him. I put my bowl down. My hand went to my hilt and I took it off again and laid it on my knee.

Amara stood up.

She did not raise her voice to him. She talked a long time, and the fire talked under her. Bonesy gave it to me in pieces.

“She says her father trusted that one.” He tipped his skull at Crumble. “She says that one trusts him.” He tipped it at me. “She says that is enough for her.” He listened again. “She says if they don’t come back up the road, it’s on her head. Not his.”

The old man looked at her a long time. His roots shifted in the dust under his stool.

Across the fire the grey fisherman said something and tapped his own neck, under the gills. The men round him laughed.

“He says the Spaniard put a stick under his gills,” Bonesy said. “He says no Spaniard ever got that close and lived.”

The coral man’s wife said something after him. They laughed at that too.

“She says he mended her roof,” Bonesy said. “Too tight.”

The old man grunted. Then the smallest of his grandchildren got up from beside him and came round the fire and sat down against my leg. She had my coin on its cord round her neck. She sat there turning it in her fingers.

The old man looked at the coin, and at me. He did not say anything else.

Amara sat down again. She did not look at us once. Across the fire Crumble was looking at her, and he did not look away when she sat.

⸻

The bark had to come from the forest on the shore.

The man with the jaguar’s shadow went for it on the second morning. He came down to our house at first light and stood at the door with an axe over his shoulder. He did not say anything. He looked at Crumble, and then at me.

“He wants hands,” Crumble said.

We went across in his canoe. He paddled and we sat. He did not talk on the water, and he did not talk when we pulled the canoe up under the trees on the far side. He went into the forest and we went after him.

Under the trees there was no sun. The light came down green and broken, in pieces the size of a hand. He walked through it without a sound.

His shadow walked ahead of him.

It should have been behind him or under him, the way the sun put it. It was not. It went on in front through the broken light, low and long, with the tail swinging. Where the light fell through the leaves it went dark across the patches. Where there was no light at all I lost it, and then it was there again further on, waiting.

I kept my hand off my hilt. It was hard work.

Crumble walked between us. He was watching the shadow too. He walked the way he walks on a deck at night, with his weight back on his heels, ready.

The man stopped at a tree with smooth grey bark. He looked up at it a long time. Then he put his hand flat on the trunk and said something to it, low. He cut.

He took the bark off in long strips, from the height of his chest down to his knee. He did not ring the tree. He took one side and left the other. When he had a bundle of strips he tied them with a vine and handed them to me.

They were wet on the inside and smelt bitter, like a green stick broken in the rain.

“Balché,” Crumble said. He was looking at the bundle in my arms. “The tree and the drink have the same name.”

“You’ve had it.”

“No.” He took a strip from the bundle and smelt it. “Balam told me about it. On the coast the Spanish priests whip a man for drinking it. They call it the devil’s drink.” He put the strip back. “Nobody up here asks them.”

The man with the axe had gone on to the next tree. His shadow was lying at the foot of it, with its head on its paws.

“Did Balam ever make it?” I said.

Crumble did not answer that. He went to hold the strips while the man cut.

We came back across the lake at noon with the canoe low in the water. The man paddled. His shadow lay along the bottom of the canoe between our feet. I sat with my boots out of the way of it the whole crossing.

On the far shore he took the bundle from me without a word and carried it up to the jar himself.

⸻

The children cut the lilies.

I saw them from the shore that afternoon. They were out in the shallows at the south end of the island, where the lilies grow thick over the water. There were eight or nine of them, up to their waists, with knives and a basket floating between them on a cord.

Bonesy was out there with them.

He was in to his ribs. The water went in at his collarbone and came out between the bones of his back. He held the basket while they cut. The lilies were white with yellow in the middle, as wide as my two hands, and the children laid them in the basket with their stems.

The girl with the bark on her skin cut the most. She went from lily to lily with her knife and never cut one that was not all the way open.

I asked Bonesy about it after.

“Only the open ones,” he said. “She says the shut ones are sleeping.”

By the middle of the afternoon the basket stood on the shore in the sun. I walked past it on my way to the canoes.

Every lily in it had closed.

They had been wide open when the children cut them. Now they were shut up tight on their stems, white and hard as a fist. I stood over the basket a while. Then I went and found Crumble and brought him back to look.

He looked at it. He did not say anything.

“They do that on the water,” I said. “Every day. I’ve seen it.”

“I know.”

“So it’s nothing.”

“It’s the lily,” he said.

That night they went into the jar.

⸻

The claw woman brought the drum on the third day.

It was a turtle’s shell, the whole back of a big river turtle, scrubbed white inside. She carried it under her claw against her hip, the way she carried her water jar. She put it down in front of Amara in the shade of the house by the water and sat down across from it.

Amara picked up a deer’s antler from beside her and struck the shell near the edge.

It made a hollow sound, flat and short, like a hand slapped on a hull.

She struck it again near the middle. That was lower, and it went on a while longer.

The claw woman shook her head.

She leaned over and tapped the shell with the tip of her claw. Here, and here, and here. Then she sat back. Amara struck it where the claw had been. The sound came out longer and lower. It went on under the floor and into my feet.

The claw woman nodded.

Amara struck the shell slow, again and again, at the same place. After a while I found I was breathing in time with it. I made myself stop.

“How long do you play it?” I said.

“Till you come back.”

“And if we don’t?”

“Till the lilies open.” She struck the shell again. “Then I stop.”

I looked at the jar behind her in the shade. It had a cloth over it and a stone on the cloth. It had begun to smell, sour and sweet and bitter all together, and the flies had found it.

⸻

Bonesy tasted it that evening.

Amara was at the jar with a gourd, lifting the cloth to look. Bonesy came and stood at her shoulder and looked in too. He had his hat in his hand.

“That’s it, then,” he said.

“That’s it.”

“Can I?”

She looked at him. Then she dipped the gourd and held it out.

He took it. He tipped it into his jaw.

It went in at his teeth and came straight out under his skull, down through his neck bones and over his ribs, and ran off the bottom of him into the dust. A little of it caught in the hollow of his collarbone and stayed there. He looked down at it.

The children by the fire laughed. One of the small ones ran over to look at the puddle.

“Huh,” Bonesy said.

“It won’t take you,” Amara said.

“No.”

“You can’t come.”

“Who says.”

“It’s the drink that takes you down. You can’t drink.”

“I been down.” He gave her back the gourd. “Long time ago. Don’t need a drink for it.”

She did not say anything.

“They lie in the canoe,” he said. “Yes?”

“Yes.”

“Both of them.”

“Yes.”

“Room for one more.” He put his hat on. “Small fella. Doesn’t take up much.”

He pointed the hat at me.

“Somebody’s got to hold this one’s hand. He talks in his sleep.” He pointed it up the path toward our house. “And the other one gets lost on a straight river.”

“I don’t talk in my sleep,” I said.

“You do.”

Amara looked at him a long while, the way she had looked at him in her doorway when he asked how far down it was. Then she looked at me.

I shrugged. I had stopped arguing with Bonesy about where he could go a year back.

He went down to the water. The canoes were pulled up on the sand, three of them, side by side. He got into the middle one and lay down on his back with his hands folded on his ribs and his hat over his face.

“Like this,” he said from under the hat.

The children came and stood round the canoe and looked in at him. One of them put a lily on his chest. He did not move.

Crumble had come down the path. He stood beside me and looked at Bonesy in the canoe with the lily on him.

“Comfortable?” Crumble said.

“Very.”

“You look laid out.”

“I am laid out.” The hat did not move. “Been laid out longer than you been alive. Somebody’s got to show you how.”

Crumble looked down at him a while. Then he stepped into the canoe and lay down on the bottom boards beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and folded his hands on his chest.

The children shrieked. One of them ran for another lily and put it on him.

“Like this?” Crumble said.

“Hands lower. You look like a bishop.”

Crumble moved his hands lower.

Up at the house by the water Amara was sitting in the doorway with the antler in her lap. She was watching the two of them in the canoe. She did not laugh with the children. She watched a long time.

⸻

I could not sleep that night.

The other two were in their hammocks. Crumble lay on his back and did not move, but his breathing was wrong for sleep. Bonesy lay in his with his hat on his chest. He does not sleep. He lies still so we can.

I got up and sat on the step of the house with my back to the post.

The lake was black. The fire at the house by the water had burned down to red, and somebody was sitting by it. I could not see who. Across the water the forest made its noise.

I took the purse out of my coat.

I tipped the coins out into my palm and counted them by the feel. The finger money, what was left after the sloop’s stores and the powder. I counted them twice. I put them back in the purse and took them out again and counted them a third time, and it came out the same.

I did not know why I was doing it. There was nothing on that island to spend it on. There was nothing down the road either, if Amara was telling it right.

I held them a while.

Behind me in the dark a hammock creaked.

“How many,” Bonesy said.

I told him.

“Same as this morning?”

“Same.”

He did not say anything else. After a while the hammock creaked again as he settled.

I put the coins back in the purse and pulled the string tight and put it in my coat, on the inside, over the ribs. I sat on the step till the fire by the water had gone out.
