We went down into the village in the rain.

It was one street, with a church at the top of it and houses of grey stone down both sides. Smoke came out of the chimneys and lay along the roofs. Nobody was out. A cart stood in the street with no horse in the shafts, and the rain ran off the end of it.

The house with the low wall was the last one before the fields. The door stood open. The singing came out of it.

It was a girl’s voice. It was an English song, and I knew it was English, and that was all I could have told you about it after.

*Come in, come in, the kettle’s on. The road is long and the light is gone.*

Crumble stopped at the wall.

He put his hands on top of it. They did not go through. He stood with the rain running off his hair and down his face and looked at the open door.

Bonesy stood at his shoulder. He did not take his arm away.

I stood on the other side of him. Nobody said anything.

Then there were feet in the lane behind us, running.

A boy came down out of the rain. He was thin, all wrists and neck, in a coat that had been made for a bigger man. He had a canvas bag over one shoulder that bumped his back as he ran. His hair was plastered flat. He could not have been more than sixteen.

He came past us so close I could have touched him. He did not see us.

I knew him by his feet. He set his left foot down turned out, the way it turned out every morning on the shore of the lake till Ah Pech pushed it straight.

I looked at Crumble. Crumble was looking at the boy.

“That you?” Bonesy said.

Crumble did not answer.

Bonesy looked the boy up and down.

“Skinny,” he said.

The boy stopped at the gate. He stood there with his chest going, and the rain coming down on him, and he did not knock. He put his hand on the gate and took it off again.

The singing stopped.

She came to the door. She had flour on her hands to the wrists and she was wiping them on her apron. It was the face from the grinding stone. Freckles across the nose, and the black hair pinned up and coming down, and the mouth that turned up at one corner.

It turned up when she saw him. Then she saw the bag.

It came down again.

“You’re soaked,” she said.

“There’s a ship.”

She stopped wiping her hands.

“At the river mouth,” he said. “Bound for the Indies. The mate says they’re short. He says he’ll have me.”

“When?”

“The tide. Tonight.”

She came down the path to the gate. She did not open it. She stood on her side of it and he stood on his, with the rain between them.

Crumble’s hands had gone white on the top of the wall.

“Tonight,” she said.

“I’ll come back.”

“When?”

“When I’ve got something to come back with.” He hitched the bag up on his shoulder. “A share. A name. Something. You don’t want a man with nothing.”

“You’ve got you.”

He looked at her. For a moment I thought he would put the bag down.

“That’s not enough,” he said.

She did not argue with him. She looked at him a long time. Then she put her hand out over the gate.

He took it.

They stood there holding hands over the gate in the rain. Her hand was white with flour and his was red with cold. Neither of them said anything. The rain came down on the two of them at the gate and on the three of us at the wall.

Then the boy opened his mouth. I saw the words come up in him. I know what a man looks like when he is about to ask for something he wants more than his own breath.

He shut it again.

He let go of her hand.

He let go first. I have known Crumble since we were boys on our first ship. I have sat across a table from him and across a leaf from him with our knuckles going white. He has never once let go first.

Beside me Crumble’s hands came off the wall. He closed the right one on itself.

The boy stepped back from the gate.

“I’ll come back,” he said again.

He turned round and ran. He ran back up past us and up the street, past the cart and the church. The bag bumped on his back. He did not look round once. Then the rain took him.

She stood at the gate with her hand still out over it.

After a while she put it down. She looked at the flour on it. She wiped it slowly on her apron, both sides, the way a woman wipes a hand that has finished with something.

Then she went in.

The door stayed open. We stood at the wall and waited for her to sing again.

She did not.

We stood there a long time.

Crumble was looking at the open door. The rain ran down his face, and I could not have told you what else did. I did not look long enough to know.

“Quick about everything,” he said.

He said it to the door. It was what he had said to Bonesy in the boat off the cay, turning his leaf. He had said it then as if it were a small thing.

I moved over till my shoulder was against his. I did not say anything. There was nothing in me to say that would have been any use to him. A girl did not wait for me either, and I have never told him her name, and he has never asked. I stood there with my shoulder against his and let that be the whole of it.

Bonesy took his arm from round Crumble’s shoulders. He looked up at him from under the hat.

“Pretty,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Laughs easy.”

“Yes.”

Bonesy waited.

“She’d have said yes,” Crumble said. “If I’d asked her.”

Bonesy did not say anything to that. He put his hand flat on Crumble’s back between the shoulders and left it there. Then he took it away and started up the street after the boy.

After a while Crumble went after him. I went after Crumble.

The street went past the church and out of the village and down. It went down a long way, between hedges, and the hedges got lower and the rain got thinner.

Then there was no rain.

There was sun. It came down on the backs of my hands so hot I felt it through the skin. The hedges were gone. The lane was white sand between scrub and sea grape, and the air that came up it was thick and wet and smelt of tar and fish and rum.

At the bottom of the lane was a harbour.

It was full of masts. Sloops and brigantines and a big Dutch flute with her yards cockbilled, and boats going between them, and men shouting on every deck. A fort stood on the hill above it all with its colours hanging slack. The front was crowded with men and barrels and goats and a woman selling something out of a basket.

I knew it before I had looked at it twice. It was Tortuga, the year I came to it.

Down on the quay a sloop lay alongside with her plank out, and men were carrying casks up it. A boy was sitting on a bollard at the foot of the plank with a bag between his feet. He was thin, all wrists and neck, in a coat made for a bigger man. His hair was dry now.

I knew the sloop. I had stood on that plank with my sword belt over my shoulder and asked the mate for a place.

I looked along the quay. There was nobody coming yet.
