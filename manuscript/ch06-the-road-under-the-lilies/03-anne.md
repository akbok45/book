We went down into the village in the rain.

It was one street, with a church at the top of it and houses of grey stone down both sides. Smoke came out of the chimneys and lay along the roofs. Nobody was out. A cart stood in the street with no horse in the shafts, and the rain ran off the end of it.

The house with the low wall was the last one before the fields. The door stood open. The singing came out of it.

It was the same song. I had heard it an hour before from a doorway at the end of a maize field.

*The rain comes over the hill. The rain goes over the hill. The maize stands. My man is late. The maize stands.*

Crumble stopped at the wall.

He put his hands on top of it. He stood with the rain running off his hair and down his face and looked at the open door.

Bonesy stood at his shoulder. He did not take his arm away.

I stood on the other side of him. Nobody said anything.

Then there were feet in the lane behind us, running.

A boy came down out of the rain. He was thin, all wrists and neck, in a coat that had been made for a bigger man. He had a canvas bag over one shoulder that bumped his back as he ran. His hair was plastered flat. He could not have been more than sixteen.

He came past us so close I could have touched him. He did not see us.

I knew him by his feet. He set his left foot down turned out, the way it turned out every morning on the shore of the lake till Ah Pech pushed it straight.

I looked at Crumble. Crumble was looking at the boy.

“That you?” Bonesy said.

Crumble did not answer.

Bonesy looked the boy up and down.

“Skinny,” he said.

The boy stopped at the gate. He stood there with his chest going, and the rain coming down on him, and he did not knock. He put his hand on the gate and took it off again.

The singing stopped.

She came to the door. She had flour on her hands to the wrists and she was wiping them on her apron. It was the face from the grinding stone. Freckles across the nose, and the black hair pinned up and coming down, and the mouth that turned up at one corner.

It turned up when she saw him. Then she saw the bag.

She stopped wiping her hands.

She came down the path slowly and stood on her side of the gate. She did not open it. He stood on his side with the rain running off the end of his nose.

“Where is it going?” she said.

“The Indies. She sails on the tide.” He shifted the bag. “The mate says they’re short. He’ll have me.”

She looked at the bag a while.

“You told me you’d stopped thinking about it.”

“I said I’d stopped talking about it.”

Her mouth turned up at the corner. It did not get far.

“What’s out there?” she said. “That isn’t here.”

“Money.” He said it to the gate. “Enough of it. You come home with a share and a ship’s name behind you, and men take their hats off in the street. Nobody takes his hat off to me.”

“I don’t want men taking their hats off.”

“I want it for you.”

She did not answer that. She put her hand on the top bar of the gate, near his. She did not touch him.

“I look at you,” she said. “Now. The way you are.”

He looked down at her hand on the bar. For a moment I thought he would put the bag down.

He did not.

“I’ll come back,” he said. “When I’m worth coming back.”

She took her hand off the gate.

She looked at him for a long time, with the rain coming down between them. Her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them. She had flour on her hands.

“Then go,” she said.

She said it gently. She said it the way you let go of a line you can no longer hold.

The boy opened his mouth. I saw the words come up in him. I know what a man looks like when he is about to ask for something he wants more than his own breath.

He shut it again.

He stepped back from the gate.

“I’ll come back,” he said again.

He turned round and ran. He ran back up past us and up the street, past the cart and the church. The bag bumped on his back. He did not look round once. Then the rain took him.

She stood at the gate.

Crumble went past me.

He did not go round by the gate. He went over the wall the way a boy goes over a wall, and across the little yard, and put his arms round her from behind.

She did not know he was there.

She stood in his arms and looked up the empty street where the boy had gone. The rain came down on both of them. He had his face down against her hair. His shoulders were going.

She put one floury hand up to her face and held it there. Then she took it down and turned and went in, out of his arms, as if there had been nothing in her way.

He stood in the yard with his arms still up round nobody.

After a while he put them down.

The door stayed open. We stood and waited for her to sing again.

She did not.

We stood there a long time.

Crumble came back over the wall. He stood beside me and looked at the open door. The rain ran down his face, and I could not have told you what else did. I did not look long enough to know.

“Quick about everything,” he said.

He said it to the door. It was what he had said to Bonesy in the boat off the cay, turning his leaf. He had said it then as if it cost him nothing.

I moved over till my shoulder was against his. I did not say anything. A girl did not wait for me either, and I have never told him her name, and he has never asked. I stood there with my shoulder against his and let that be the whole of it.

Bonesy put his hand flat on Crumble’s back between the shoulders and left it there. Then he took it away and started up the street after the boy.

After a while Crumble went after him. I went after Crumble.

The street went past the church and out of the village and down. It went down a long way, between hedges, and the hedges got lower and the rain got thinner.

Then there was no rain.

There was sun. It came down on the backs of my hands so hot I felt it through the skin. The hedges were gone. The lane was white sand between scrub and sea grape, and the air that came up it was thick and wet and smelt of tar and fish and rum.

At the bottom of the lane was a harbour.

It was full of masts. Sloops and brigantines and a big Dutch flute with her yards cockbilled, and boats going between them, and men shouting on every deck. A fort stood on the hill above it all with its colours hanging slack. The front was crowded with men and barrels and goats and a woman selling something out of a basket.

I knew it before I had looked at it twice. It was Tortuga, the year I came to it.

There was a crowd on the quay at the foot of a sloop’s plank. A barrel stood on its end in the middle of them with a paper on its head, held down at the corners by four stones. A big man with one ear stood behind it and read the paper out loud.

I knew the voice. He had been our quartermaster three years.

He read the articles. No prey, no pay. A share each, and a share and a half to the captain. So much for a right arm, so much for a left, so much for an eye. Every man a vote. The captain chosen by the crew and put down by the crew. Every man to keep his piece clean and fit for service. No man to strike another aboard.

The men round the barrel nodded along. Most of them had heard it before on other decks.

Then they went up one at a time and signed.

The first boy up was the one from the lane. He had lost the coat. He was in a shirt gone grey and a pair of slops with the knees out, barefoot, with nothing in his hands and nothing at his hip. No sash, no plate, no blade. He was sixteen and he had nothing on him that a man would steal.

He took the pen. He bent over the barrel and wrote his name slowly, with his tongue between his teeth, the way a boy writes who has not done it much. Then he stood back and looked at it.

The next one up was me.

I was seventeen. My head was shaved to the skin and the cut on it from the razor had not healed. I had a white shirt that had been good once and a pair of breeches that still had the shape of a tailor in them. There was no hide on me anywhere. My hands were soft. I did not know yet what the sea does to hands.

I took the pen from him. I signed fast, in the hand they beat into us at the academy. It came out too neat for that paper.

The quartermaster looked at my name, and then at me, and grunted.

I gave the pen back to the boy, because he was standing there. He took it and did not know what to do with it, and held it.

Then the boy I was put his hand out.

The boy Crumble was looked at it a moment. He shifted the pen to his other hand and took it.

They gripped. I remembered the grip. He was thin, but there was more in his hand than there looked to be. I squeezed and he squeezed back. I squeezed harder. So did he.

The quartermaster said something and the men round the barrel laughed. The two boys did not look round. They stood there at the foot of the plank with their knuckles going white, each one waiting for the other to let go first.

Beside me Crumble made a sound. It was half a laugh.

Neither of the boys let go.
