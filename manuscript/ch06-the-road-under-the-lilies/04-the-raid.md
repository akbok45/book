The boys went up the plank and over the brigantine’s rail.

Crumble did not go after them. He stayed at the foot of the plank, looking down at the water between the quay and the ship.

It was turning red.

It spread through the water the way the ink had spread on the barrel head. In a few breaths the whole harbour had gone red, from the quay to the point, and the air coming off it smelt of smoke.

Bonesy came and stood beside Crumble at the edge of the quay.

“This way,” he said, and stepped off into it.

Crumble jumped in after him. So did I.

The water was hot. I went under, and it roared in my ears like a fire. When I came up I had my hands on the rail of a ship. I pulled myself over it.

It was night, and the ship was burning.

⸻

There was no moon. A north wind was driving the smoke across the deck, and the foresail overhead was on fire, dropping pieces of itself onto the planking.

She was a Spaniard, big and slow and low in the water. Our brigantine lay alongside her with the grapnels over. Our crew were coming down off the brigantine’s rail onto the Spaniard’s deck, eighty of them near enough, with pistols going off and men shouting.

I knew this night. It was off the Campeche coast, in our second year.

Crumble stood beside me by the mainmast. Bonesy came and stood on his other side.

I looked at Bonesy. His face had gone thin as the skin of an onion, and I could see his teeth through his cheek.

He saw me look.

“Had my looks for a morning,” he said. “More than most get.”

⸻

I knew most of our men by their backs. The one-eared quartermaster with a boarding axe. A big Dane we lost the next year off Cartagena. A boy from Bristol with a pike, who never shaved in his life and never would.

Then I saw the young man I had been.

He was eighteen. His head was shaved to the skin again over the old cut, and there was a white scar on his forearm he had not had at Tortuga. He had the old rapier out, and there was blood on it to the hilt.

A Spanish sailor came out of the smoke in front of him. He threw his knife down on the deck. He went down on his knees with his hands up.

“Me rindo,” he said. “Me rindo.”

I surrender.

The young man I had been looked down at him. His lips were drawn back off his teeth.

He put the rapier through him.

He pulled it out and looked round the deck for the next one.

For years I had told myself that man had a knife in his hand. I watched it lie on the planking a full yard from him, where he had thrown it.

The boy was standing a little way off with his cutlass hanging at his side. He had seen it. He looked at the young man with the rapier, and the young man did not look back.

⸻

Then the boy turned his head toward the open hatch in the waist.

I heard it too, under the shouting. Voices, coming up out of the hold with the smoke. Women. A child crying. Somebody beating on wood from underneath.

The boy went to the hatch and looked down. Smoke came up out of it thick and yellow.

He went down the ladder into it.

We went to the edge of the hatch and looked down after him. I could see nothing but smoke and the red of the fire through the seams of the deck below.

“There’s people,” the boy shouted up. “There’s people chained down here!”

Then another voice came up out of the hold. A girl’s. She was speaking Spanish, quick and hard, the way a bosun talks in a blow.

“The key. The mate has the key. On his belt. The fat one, with the red sash.”

The boy came back up the ladder with his eyes streaming. He looked round the deck.

The mate was lying by the wheel with a pike through him. He was fat, and he had a red sash. The boy ran to him and cut the key off his belt with the cutlass and ran back and went down the ladder again.

I heard the lock go.

Then they came up.

The children came first. A small boy, coughing. Two girls holding hands. A baby handed up by somebody below to the boy at the top of the ladder, who did not know how to hold it. Then two old men, slow. Then the women, one after another, with their faces black from the smoke.

The girl came up last.

She was perhaps seventeen. She had both hands on the back of the last woman, an old one who could not climb, and she pushed her up the ladder ahead of her rung by rung. Then she came up out of the smoke herself and stood on the deck.

She did not look at the fire or the fighting. She looked at her people. Her lips moved as she counted them. There were twenty. Then she said something in Maya, and they went where she pointed. They lined the rail out of the way of the fighting, the small ones in the middle and the women round the outside.

She did all of that before she looked at the boy.

He stood there with the key still in his hand.

“Thank you,” she said, in English. Her English was slow and good.

Crumble had come to stand beside me again. He did not take his eyes off her.

⸻

By the time the Spanish ship stopped fighting, the fire had the whole of her forward. The Spaniards who were left were on their knees by the mainmast. Our crew were going round them taking knives and rings.

A man with a black beard went and stood over the captives along the rail. He looked them over the way a man looks over a catch.

“There’s forty pieces a head in these at Port Royal,” he said. “More for the young ones.”

Some of the men laughed. Some nodded.

“No,” the boy said.

Everyone turned round.

He went red to the ears. He was seventeen and he was the youngest man on that deck, and every one of them was looking at him. But he did not take it back.

“We put them ashore,” he said.

The black beard laughed in his face. A good few laughed with him.

The quartermaster held up his hand. “It’s the crew’s say,” he said. “Every man a vote. Who’s for putting them ashore?”

The boy put his hand up. Three or four others put theirs up with him. That was all.

“And who’s for Port Royal?”

The hands went up all over the deck.

The girl by the rail watched them go up. She did not say anything. She put her arm round the small boy who was coughing and held him against her.

Then the young man I had been walked out into the middle of the deck.

He was wiping the rapier on his sleeve. He did not hurry. Nobody on that deck wanted to cross him, after what they had watched him do that night. The black beard stopped laughing.

“The boy went down into a burning hold for them,” the young man said. “Not one of you went. I was up here, and I did not go either.”

Nobody answered him.

“Twenty heads at forty pieces,” he said. “Split eighty ways, that comes to ten pieces a man.” He put the rapier up. “Any man who wants his ten can come to me for it. I’ll pay it out of my own share.”

Nobody came.

The quartermaster looked round the deck. “Who’s for putting them ashore?”

The young man put his hand up. Then the hands went up, slow at first, and then all over.

The boy looked at him across the deck. The young man shrugged.

“You owe me ten pieces,” he said.

Then he went and stood at the rail beside the boy. They did not say anything else to each other. They stood there and watched the Spaniard burn.

Beside me Crumble said, “You never told me why you did that.”

“You never asked.”

He looked at me. Then he looked back at the two of them at the rail.

Behind them the girl was watching the young man with the rapier. She had watched him kill the man who said he surrendered. She had watched him put his hand up. She looked at him a long time, and her face did not move.

Years after, on a lake shore with three spears on me, she would tell me I still drew first. I had thought she meant that morning on the shore.

⸻

They put the captives ashore at first light.

The sea had gone grey and flat. The Spanish ship was a long smudge of smoke behind us, going grey and soft at the edges the way everything on that road went once we had passed it. The longboat was full of women and children. The boy was at the bow oar, and the girl sat on the thwart in front of him with her back to him.

We were in the boat too. Nobody had to make room for us.

Bonesy sat in the stern sheets with his hands in his armpits. Crumble sat on the thwart beside the girl. He was close enough to touch her. He did not.

Halfway to the beach she turned round on the thwart and looked at the boy.

“My father would have liked you,” she said.

The boy kept rowing. “Who’s your father?”

“His name is Balam.”

The name meant nothing to him. I watched it mean nothing to him. Beside her, the man he would be put his head down.

“He is up a river,” she said. “A long way up. He teaches. He does not teach many.” She looked at the boy. “A man who goes down into a fire for people he does not know is meant for more than this.”

She tipped her head back at the smoke on the sea, and at the crew in the boat with rings on every finger.

“If you have it in you,” she said, “go up the river and find him.”

The boy looked at her over the oar. He did not say he would.

Beside her Crumble’s lips were moving with the words. He knew every one of them.

The boat grounded. The women went over the side into the shallows and up the beach. The girl went last, after the last of them. At the top of the beach, where the sand met the trees, she stopped and looked back once at the boat.

Then she went in under the trees.

⸻

Crumble got out of the boat and stood in the shallows to his knees, looking at the place where she had gone in.

The water round his legs went red again.

It was not ink this time. It moved. It flickered. It was firelight, coming up through the sea from somewhere underneath, the way the light of a burning town comes up off its harbour at night.

Bonesy waded past him out into it, to his waist, to his chest. He looked back at us from under the hat.

“This way,” he said.

He went under. Crumble went in after him. Then I did.
