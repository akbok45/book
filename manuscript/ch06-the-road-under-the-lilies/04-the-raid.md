The boys went up the plank and over the brigantine’s rail and were gone.

Crumble did not go after them. He stood at the foot of the plank and looked down.

Between the edge of the quay and the brigantine’s side there was a strip of harbour water as wide as a man is long. It was green and clear. I could see the stones of the quay going down into it, and weed on the stones, and a crab on the weed.

Something was coming up into it.

It came up off the bottom in threads, red, the way the ink had gone out from our names on the barrel head. The threads spread and joined. The crab went into the red and I lost it. The whole strip between the quay and the hull went red, and then the red went out under the brigantine and into the harbour, and the harbour went red from the quay to the point.

The smell of the harbour changed. The fish and the tar went out of it. It smelt of smoke.

I looked up.

The quay was gone. The brigantine was gone. We were standing on a deck in the dark, and the deck was on fire.

⸻

It was night. There was no moon. A north wind was blowing the smoke across the deck in sheets, and the foresail overhead was burning, dropping pieces of itself onto the planking.

The ship under us was a Spaniard. She was big and slow and low in the water. Our brigantine lay alongside her with the grapnels over, her rail higher than this one, and our crew were coming down off it onto the Spaniard’s deck. Eighty of them, near enough. Pistols going off. Somebody screaming in Spanish for his mother.

I knew this night. It was off the Campeche coast, in our second year.

Crumble was beside me with the smoke going past his face. Bonesy was on his other side.

I looked at Bonesy and wished I had not.

His face was going. The skin was still on it, but it had gone thin over the cheekbones and the jaw, and in the red light off the burning sail I could see the shape of his teeth through his cheek. The two gaps at the front showed through it dark.

He saw me look. He tipped the hat lower.

“Don’t,” he said.

I did not.

⸻

I knew most of our men by their backs. The one-eared quartermaster with a boarding axe. A big Dane we lost the next year off Cartagena. A boy from Bristol with a pike, who never shaved in his life and never would.

Then a young man came down off the brigantine’s rail beside us.

It was me. Two years older than the one at the barrel head. Eighteen. My head was shaved to the skin again over the old cut, and there was a white scar on my forearm I did not have at Tortuga. I had the old rapier out before my feet touched the deck.

A Spanish sailor stood up out of the smoke in front of him with his hands empty. He was opening his mouth.

He did not wait to hear what was in it. He put the rapier through him under the ribs and pulled it out and went on past him before the man had finished falling.

I watched myself do it.

I had forgotten that one. I had not forgotten him. I had forgotten that I did not let him speak.

Crumble did not say anything. Bonesy did not say anything. The sailor lay on the deck with his mouth still open round what he had meant to say.

⸻

The boy came down off the rail behind him.

He had filled out in two years. His shoulders had come, and the coat was gone, and he was in a shirt with the sleeves torn off. There was a cutlass in his hand. He held it the way the quartermaster had taught us, wrong, with the thumb on the back of the grip.

He came down onto the deck and stopped. He had his head on one side.

I heard it too. Under the noise of the deck and the fire there was another noise, coming up out of the open hatch in the waist. Women’s voices. Somebody beating on wood from underneath.

He went to the hatch and looked down. Smoke was coming up out of it thick and yellow.

He went down.

Crumble went after him. He did not wait for me or for Bonesy. He went down the ladder into the smoke after himself.

I went after Crumble. Bonesy came after me.

The hold was black and full of smoke, and hot as a bread oven with the door shut. The fire was in the deck over our heads. I could see it through the seams, red lines in the black. Pitch was dropping out of the seams in burning drops and hissing on the planks.

There were people chained along the ship’s side. Women, mostly, and some children, and two old men. They were Maya. They had iron round their ankles and a chain through the iron, and the chain was made fast to a stanchion with a lock on it. They were pulling at it and screaming.

The boy was at the stanchion with the cutlass, hacking at the chain. Sparks came off it and the chain held.

Crumble stood behind him and watched.

The girl nearest the stanchion was not screaming. She was perhaps seventeen. She had her back to the ship’s side and her feet drawn up. Every time the boy leaned in to get at the chain she kicked at him. She caught him in the hip. Then the knee.

“Hold still,” the boy said. “Hold still. I’m trying to help.”

She kicked him again.

The boy threw down the cutlass and went back up the ladder.

For a breath I thought he had gone for good. The women along the side thought so too. The screaming went up.

Then he came down again with the quartermaster’s boarding axe. He set his feet. He brought it down on the chain where it went round the stanchion, once, twice. On the third stroke a link split and went across the hold like a pistol ball.

The chain ran out through the irons. The women were up and pulling their feet free and going for the ladder.

The girl did not go for the ladder. She sat with her back to the planking and looked at the boy with the axe.

The deck over our heads groaned. A beam came down at the far end of the hold in a shower of fire.

The boy dropped the axe and went to her. She bit him. She got her teeth into the meat of his hand below the thumb and held on, and he did not pull it away. He got his other arm round her waist and lifted her over his shoulder. Her teeth were still in his hand. He went up the ladder with her like that.

Crumble was looking at his own hand. He was looking at the place below the thumb. There was a white scar there in two crescents. I had seen it a thousand times. I had never once asked about it.

⸻

We came up onto the deck behind them.

The fighting was done. The Spaniards who were left were on their knees by the mainmast with their hands on their heads. Our crew were going round them taking knives and rings. The foremast was burning from the top down like a candle.

The boy set the girl on her feet by the rail. She let go of his hand. There was blood on her mouth that was not hers.

She looked round the deck at the burning mast and the men on their knees and the dead.

Then she looked at the young man I had been.

He was standing over a Spaniard by the mainmast with the rapier up. The Spaniard was on his knees with his hands on his head. He was not doing anything. The young man had the point at his throat and was waiting for him to.

The girl looked at that a long time. Her face did not move.

The quartermaster said something. The young man took the rapier away and wiped it on the Spaniard’s shirt and walked off.

The girl watched him go.

Years after, on a lake shore with three spears on me, she would tell me I still drew first. I had thought she meant that morning on the shore.

⸻

They put the captives ashore at first light.

The sea had gone grey and flat. The Spanish ship was a long smudge of smoke behind us, going grey and soft at the edges the way everything on that road went once we had passed it. The longboat was full of women and children. The boy was at the bow oar, and the girl sat on the thwart in front of him with her back to him.

We were in the boat too. Nobody had to make room for us.

Bonesy sat in the stern sheets. In the grey light I could see the line of his jaw through the skin of it, and the hinge where the jaw went up into the skull. He had both hands in his armpits.

Crumble sat on the thwart beside the girl. He was close enough to touch her. He did not.

Halfway to the beach she turned round on the thwart and looked at the boy.

His hand was bound up in a strip of somebody’s shirt. The blood had come through it.

“My father would have liked you,” she said. Her English was slow and good.

The boy kept rowing. “Who’s your father?”

“His name is Balam.”

The name meant nothing to him. I watched it mean nothing to him. Beside her, the man he would be put his head down.

“He is up a river,” she said. “A long way up. He teaches. He does not teach many.” She looked at the boy’s bound hand. “A man who goes down into a fire for a girl he does not know is meant for more than this.”

She tipped her head back at the smoke on the sea, and at the crew in the boat with rings on every finger.

“If you have it in you,” she said, “go up the river and find him.”

The boy looked at her over the oar. He did not say he would.

Beside her Crumble’s lips were moving with the words. He knew every one of them.

The boat grounded. The women went over the side into the shallows and up the beach. The girl went with them. At the top of the beach, where the sand met the trees, she stopped and looked back once at the boat.

Then she went in under the trees.

⸻

Crumble got out of the boat and stood in the shallows to his knees. He was looking at the place where she had gone in.

The water round his legs went red.

It came up the same way it had come up at Tortuga, in threads off the bottom. Only it was not ink this time, and it was not blood. It moved. It flickered. It was light, firelight, coming up through the sea from somewhere underneath us, the way the light of a burning town comes up off the water of its harbour at night.

Crumble looked down at it.

Then the sand went out from under my feet, and the sea went with it.
