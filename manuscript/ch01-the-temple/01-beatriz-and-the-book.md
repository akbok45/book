Two days into the storm, Bonesy was getting nowhere with Beatriz.

He stood in front of her with what he had in place of a hand resting on her stone shoulder. He had drawn a heart in the dust between them on the first morning. He had drawn an arrow through it on the second. The arrow had wandered because his foot kept getting in the way, and now the arrow had two points and no shaft, which he had told us was a metaphor and we had not asked him for. Beatriz did not return his looks. Beatriz had not returned anyone’s looks in nine hundred years, but the fact of this had never been brought up to Bonesy in any way he would respect.

“You’ve been quiet today,” he said to her.

The wind hauled into the headland above the temple’s mouth, and a branch came down off the upper stones and skidded down the slope in pieces. The lamp Crumble had wedged between two broken slabs guttered. The shadows of the chamber moved.

“I noticed,” Bonesy said.

I had been on the windward end of what remained of a stone bench for an hour, working a knot out of a length of cord with my teeth, listening to the storm and to him. We were in the back room of an old Maya temple that had given up at the front a long time ago. The front had come down at the doorway in stones and the stones had grown over in vines, and the door was a mouth now. The chamber behind the mouth was lower than a man’s reach and four long paces deep, and at this hour of an afternoon, with the weather what it was, it was dark enough that Crumble had lit the lamp. The lamp gave us back a little of the day. It lit the carvings in the band along the walls well enough that a man could read them, if a man knew how, and Crumble did. It lit the three of us, and the three of us in that light looked like three men of dignity, which we were not.

Crumble was against the south wall with a leather pouch open in his lap. He had taken a tobacco leaf out of the pouch some time before and was working a corner of it slow in the back of his cheek, the way Balam had taught him, the way the people of this country had done before the Spanish ever came and dried the leaf and rolled it into sticks. He had a chart laid across his thighs to pass the time. He had been reading it for two days. He read charts the way a priest reads a scripture, in the small steady way of a man who is not in a hurry and does not feel he is being watched. He had closed his eyes some time back without leaving the chart there, which was his way of saying he did not want to be talked to. I knew the look. I had known it fifteen years.

I should say, on Bonesy.

Bonesy was a skeleton.

He had been a man before he had been a skeleton. By his own account he had been a Maya man, from a town he could no longer find on a map and no longer wanted to. He had not been anyone of importance, in his time. He had worked a patch of corn ground. He had not been a priest. He had not been the son of a priest. The priests of his town had not let him near anything they did not want everyone to see, and that had stayed with him ever since. He could not say how long ago that had been, exactly. He had been a skeleton a long time. He had not aged in any way I could see, which was the part of him that took some getting used to, and the part of him that, once you were used to it, made him hard to think about clearly. He stood a little under my height. He had nothing on him except a leather cord around his neck. He had been with Crumble and me about a year, attached the way a stray attaches to a fire, and we had stopped trying to put him anywhere else.

He talked to Beatriz the way a man talks to a wife he has been married to too long and not long enough.

“I made a list,” he said. “Things I appreciate about you. Things I find difficult.”

Crumble, without opening his eyes, said, “She’s a corn god.”

“She is what I say she is.”

“He.”

“She is a goddess.”

“He’s a god of corn. He has the husk on his brow.”

“That’s hair.”

“It’s a husk.”

“She has lovely hair.”

“He is bald under a husk.”

“Beatriz,” Bonesy said, “tell him.”

Beatriz did not.

The wind eased a notch and the lamp steadied. The lords of the underworld watched from the band along the upper wall, where they had been watching for nine hundred years. There were thirteen of them, by Crumble’s count. They had their hands folded in front of them. They had not blinked, in the time of any man in this chamber but one, and that one not by much.

Two and a half days back we had walked out of a Spanish fort at the edge of Veracruz with nothing for our trouble. The hire wanted a finger. Not any finger. The finger of a small Castilian saint who had died half a century back at a mission in Yucatán, kept now in a reliquary of gilt silver in the lower vaults, which we had taken to be one vault and had found, on getting in, to be several. There was time to crack one. Not several. I had read the sky off the high wall on the way in. The sky was wrong by an hour, in the direction that does not let you bargain with it. Crumble wanted to go down anyway. I said no. He said yes, and then he said yes once more, more quietly, which was him telling me he had heard me. The quartermaster on the south wall had given his face a longer look than I wanted to think about, and his corporal a longer one. We had not run on the way out. We had walked. We had put the sloop three hours west with the first edge of the squall already on our shoulders and tucked her into a pass we had kept in our pocket for years, between two outflung arms of stone where the surge runs up the outer arm and breaks on it and never makes the inner. The cove behind the arms was the reason the cove existed, and the reason we knew of it. She had been holding for two days. She was holding now. She was also coming out from under cover, by the hour, as the storm let up. The two were one fact looked at from two sides.

Bonesy turned his skull a quarter to look at me. He turned it the way a man does not turn his head. There is a way the head sits on a man, with the muscles of the neck holding it, and it does not move that way on its own. Bonesy’s slid round on the spine the way a stone slides on a table when the table tips. He had a kind of decency about it. He did it slowly. He gave you time to take it in.

“Jigsaw.”

“Yes.”

“Tell him she’s a rain goddess.”

“She’s a rain goddess.”

“There,” he said to Crumble. “Quorum.”

“You bribed him.”

“He volunteered.”

“He always volunteers.”

“He is loyal in his way.”

“He is bored in his way.”

I let them go. I was listening. The squall had been blowing two days out of the east-southeast and was tired now. A gale wears itself out the way a man does. In pulls and pauses. Getting more honest at the end about how it cannot keep this up forever. I could hear the headland breaking the worst of it. I could hear the cove sounding fine in the easing.

The wind drew in a long breath. Held it. Let it out a touch softer than the breath before.

There.

“It’s hauling round,” I said.

Crumble opened his eyes.

“How long.”

“Few hours. Teeth come out, then it’ll quiet by first light.”

He nodded. He closed his eyes. He took another small piece off the leaf.

“A shame,” Bonesy said. “We were making progress.”

“You were making progress,” Crumble said.

“It is a partnership.”

“It is a wall.”

“Hard women are like that.”

“She is a wall.”

“They take time.”

Crumble’s mouth almost moved. He held it. He took another piece of the leaf.

Bonesy bent down. The bones of his knees made the sound of a man cracking walnuts. He picked up a small piece of broken stone from the floor, considered it, set it on the heart in the dust as if he were laying a wreath, and stood up again. The same sound on the way up.

“So,” he said. He was looking at the wall. He was talking to the wall, which is what he did when he was talking to us. “The old priests used to talk about a book.”

I did not stop working the cord. Crumble’s jaw did.

“They used to sit in their cellars,” Bonesy said. “Not the cellars they cleaned for the festivals. The ones in the back. They didn’t want me down there. They never said so. They just looked at me a certain way and shifted their bones around. I went in anyway. I’d sit on a barrel. I’d ask questions. They’d pretend they didn’t hear me. They’d talk to each other about a book that wasn’t a book anymore because they had taken it apart on purpose. They’d argue about whose grandfather had touched a corner of it. Whose grandfather’s grandfather had. They could never agree. They’d argue all night. They could never get rid of me. It was the only thing they could never get rid of.”

He picked up another small piece of stone. He set it next to the first one.

“It was an interesting book,” he said. “By the sound of it.”

The wind eased another notch.

Crumble had not opened his eyes. He had stopped chewing. That was all I needed to know.

“Balam used to talk about it,” he said.

Bonesy did not turn round.

“Did he.”

“Yes.”

“Did he have one of the pieces.”

“He was working on one. Before he went.”

“Which one.”

Crumble was a long time answering. He chewed the leaf and looked at the lamp.

“He said it was off a half-moon island,” he said. “He said the cave was hid by a thing the old priests couldn’t put a name to. He said it works the way certain weather does. A man on the shore wouldn’t see the cave. He’d see a pool.”

“He’d see a pool,” Bonesy said.

“Yes.”

I let the cord lie still in my hands.

“Tarpon,” I said.

Crumble’s eyes opened.

“What.”

“There’s tarpon in a pool on the windward side of a half-moon island west of the Bay of Honduras. Six and seven feet of them. They slide past each other on a current the pool has got no business having. Water no deeper than my waist. They don’t turn off when a man comes up on them. I crouched on the stone nine years back and put my hand in the water. They went past my hand. They went past it slow.”

The chamber went quiet the way a chamber does when the wind that has been working at it for two days finally lets up.

“Jigsaw.”

“I know, Crumble.”

“Jigsaw.”

“I’ve been there.”

He closed his eyes again. He did not look surprised. He looked like a man who had been holding his breath without knowing it and had only just been able to let it out.

I had stood at the edge of that pool nine years back. I had been twenty. The water behind the tarpon had not stopped moving the way a small shallow pool’s water stops, when the wind quits and there is no reason for it to keep on. The water had been pulling somewhere I could not see. I had stood and watched it the better part of an hour. I had got into the dinghy and gone home. I had not spoken of it to Crumble. I had not spoken of it to anyone. I had been telling myself for nine years that someday I would go back.

Bonesy made a noise. We looked at him.

“What.”

“Nothing. Carry on.”

“Bonesy.”

“I am very moved. Don’t mind me. Beatriz is moved. We are both moved. Carry on.”

“Bonesy.”

“What.”

“Sit down.”

He sat down in front of Beatriz. He took her, gently, off her base, and set her in his lap, and stared at the wall.

Outside, the storm came down off its peak and the wind hauled another quarter. The air in the chamber had changed. There is a way air changes in a small room when three men in it have been quiet for two days and one of them has finally said the thing the other two were waiting for. The lamp does not light it any differently. It sits a little heavier on the shoulders, is all.

“We can’t leave it,” Crumble said. He was saying it to himself.

“No,” I said. “We can’t.”

The lamp guttered. The lords of the underworld in their row watched, as they had been, and would.

Bonesy stepped on the heart in the dust by accident, and stepped off it carefully, the way a man does when he doesn’t want to admit he’s done damage.

“Beatriz,” he said. “I am about to have an adventure. Try not to take it personally.”

Beatriz did not.
