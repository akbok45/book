She took the bait. We had made it ugly enough that the captain could not stay out in deep water and watch. His whole boat crew was in the water below the rock now, drifting in a red bloom, the small fish already rising to them and the bigger fish under those. He brought her in.

She came in fast, too fast for water like that. She found the deep channel and ran it a while, then lost it. Her keel went up onto the coral and stuck, and she hung there with her bow into the cove and her stern swinging slow on the swell, caught in the reef the way a fish hangs in a weir.

Bonesy had already gone back to the sloop. He got to the one gun and laid it while she was still settling, and when she came to rest he had her whole deck open below him. The first ball went in at the bow and walked the length of her, the rail going to splinters, men going down.

That was our door.

Every face aboard turned forward to the smoke and the wreck of the bow, and that was when Crumble and I came off the cliff.

The masthead stood level with the rock where we crouched, near enough to touch. Crumble went first. He caught the topmast shroud where it ran up to the cap and went down it hand under hand, quiet, his feet barely touching the rope. I came after. We came down the rigging into the smoke with the whole crew’s backs to us, four pistols apiece across our chests, and dropped onto the deck behind them.

No one had looked up. The bow was smoke and splinters, and every eye was on it.

A barracuda does its waiting before the rush. After that there is only the flash of it, and the water opening red behind.

We had waited long enough.

I set the first pistol to the nearest back and fired. The shot went through him and found the sailor in front of him, and both dropped into the smoke. Crumble fired twice beside me. Two more went down before the deck understood the trouble had come from behind.

Then they turned.

Badly. Some toward me, some toward Crumble, some back toward the bow as if the first trouble were still the only one. Smoke had them by the eyes. Fire had them by the backs. The reef had the ship by the belly. We were in the one place no one had guarded.

I drew both blades.

The first sailor came at me wide, cutlass high. I stepped inside the swing and put one blade under his arm, the other across the back of his knee as I passed. He folded wrong, one half of him still trying to stand and the other half finished with the matter.

The next had a pistol halfway out. I cut across his hand before he cleared it. The pistol dropped with three fingers still curled around it. He looked down, slow with shock, and I put the other blade under his jaw. His teeth shut on the steel. I felt it in the grip.

Crumble was on the other side of the smoke with Balam’s blade low in his hand. He does not fight pretty. He fights like a snare taking shut. A sailor swung an axe at him. Crumble stepped inside it, caught the wrist on the way down, and opened him from hip to ribs. The axe hit the boards after its owner did.

After that the deck went slick.

Blood does not stay where it falls on a ship. It runs with the tilt, finds seams, gets under your feet. The smoke rolled back from the bow in hot sheets, and the killing went half blind. Shapes came at us and we cut them down. One slipped before he reached me and rose on one knee, which was high enough. Another backed into the broken rail and put his hand into a nest of hot splinters. He looked at the hand. I opened him.

Someone called for the captain. Someone else called for God.

Neither came quick enough.

We pushed them toward the middle of the deck. Crumble from one side, me from the other. Not clean work. Deck work never is. Too close, too loud, too much footing to lose. A sailor would come out of smoke with his mouth open and be dead before he had shaped a word. Another would swing at where I had been and find Crumble instead. The ship bucked under us on the reef, each swell lifting her and setting her down harder, so the wounded slid with the blood and the dead shifted as if they had been called back to their stations.

A sailor came out of the smoke with a boarding pike. I caught the shaft between both blades, turned it, and drove my shoulder into him. He went back against the rail. I put one blade through his belly and pinned him there long enough to take the pike from his hands.

The smoke thinned.

The deck opened in front of me, and the captain was there.

He stood at the break of the quarterdeck with his coat off and his sleeves rolled back, a long Spanish blade low at his side. He had not rushed with the rest. He had watched them die and used the time well.

I hated him for that first.

He had the look of the boys from the clean rooms. Not soft now. The sea had taken some of that off him. But he had been soft once. Fed once. Named once. The kind of face that had never had to ask the world for room.

Then he lifted the blade, and I knew the school in his wrist.

He came in without hurry.

The first touch told me he was good. The second told me I could die there.

He did not spend himself. That was the worst of it. Anger gives you handles. Fear gives you gaps. Pride makes a fool reach too far. He gave me none of that. His point stayed in the middle of me, patient as a hook in deep water, and his feet took the deck a little at a time.

So I gave ground.

Past the mast. Past a body rolling under my heel. Past the smoke blowing hot from the bow. I had both blades moving and still he made me work harder than I liked. Every time I tried to turn him, he was there. Every time I tried to crowd him, his point was waiting in the road.

Crumble had his own work. I saw him only in pieces. The red sash through smoke. Balam’s blade rising and falling. A man going down with both hands at his throat. Then the captain took the sight from me again.

He pressed me back toward the broken rail.

The cove was below. Fire to my left. Smoke to my right. His blade in front, already measuring the place under my breastbone.

Then his mouth changed.

Not a smile. He knew better than to smile. Only the smallest easing at the corner, as if the deck had righted under him, as if the dirty sailor in hides had remembered where he belonged.

That was when I knew what I hated in him.

A proper master would have been proud of him. That was his trouble. Proper masters teach a man to beat another proper man. Same floors. Same rules. Same clean lies.

I let my left blade catch his.

Not hard. Not desperate. Just enough to look like a mistake. He felt the bind and took it, because it was the right thing to take. His strength came forward. His eye went with it. For one breath, all of him belonged to my left hand.

My right went under.

No flourish. No lesson. Just the short ugly thrust from rooms where nobody was trying to become a gentleman.

The blade went up beneath his jaw and stopped deep.

The captain froze.

His sword fell out of his hand and struck the deck once.

He looked surprised. Not hurt yet. Not afraid. Only surprised, as if the world had broken a promise it had been keeping with him all his life.

I held him there a moment.

I wanted him to know it had not been reef, or smoke, or luck.

Me.

Then I drew the blade out and let him down.

The fight went out of the ship with him. There were still bodies moving. Still breath catching wet where it should not. Still Crumble standing in the smoke with Balam’s blade dark to the grip. But the ship knew. You can feel it when a crew is finished. The noise changes. The living start listening for permission to stay that way.

Out past the rail, the cove had gone red. The fish were rising through it.
