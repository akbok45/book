Which was when I looked astern, the way I had since Tortuga without rightly stopping, and at the hard rim of the sea where there had been nothing all morning, there was a sail.

I watched it to be sure. A tired eye builds ships out of haze. But it held, too much canvas for the easy wind, a hull driven and not sailed, standing off no honest business of its own.

We had known he sent it. That much we carried out of Tortuga for certain, that the man does not wait on weather the way we do, that a hull built to cut right through a storm the way a sword tears through flesh.

“There he is,” Bonesy said, almost fond, the way he greets what the rest of us dread.

And dead ahead, low and green and ringed all round with bright shoal water, the cay came on, and the one mean gate in it, and behind the gate the only water for sixty miles that would not kill us.

The gate did not look like a gate. It looked like a wide bright road in. That is the trick of clear water, and it has drowned more men than rough water ever has. Rough water frightens a man honest. Clear water lies to him sweet. I read the reef off the colour of it, the dark green that is depth and the brown green that is a coral head a foot down. There was one way into that cove, the way there is one gap in a fence a fox can take, and we barely made it.

The cay sat low and green ahead. A hook of palms, and behind them other trees climbing higher, all of them competing for the sun, a jungle island shouldering up out of the sea. A beach under the palms the colour of bone. No track on it, no fire-black, no mark of a boat ever drawn up.

As we rounded into the cove we dropped the anchor. The water was warm as blood, and we went over the side and swam in.

Bonesy went up the sand ahead of us, skipping, a choirboy let out of the cathedral early.

“Mind the wet rocks,” he sang out.

Behind the rocks the spring came out of a low grey face of stone, and over it the Mayans had cut a woman, worn near to nothing, only the line of the nose and a closed and patient eye. Bonesy stopped and caressed her face.

“I spent many years with this one,” he said.

“She’s a rock, Bonesy.”

“It’s almost a good thing she can’t talk.”

We got down to the stone and put our faces to the water and drank like a Tortuga cur drinks from the gutter after it rains. No shame in it.

When I sat back I looked out past the reef. She was there. She had come up while we drank, topsails to the mast, riding easy. Not coming on. Not firing. Just waiting, the way a barracuda waits, hung still in the water with everything in it pointed at you, patient because it knows it is faster than the thing it means to have.

“Two days dry and you forget there’s a King’s ship come to take you off in irons,” Bonesy said, watching us watch her.

Crumble looked at me. I looked at him, and both of us were in the same place at once. Twenty men against the two of us, three years back, in a cove much like this one. Only two remained to tell the tale.

The smile got away from me before I could stop it. Crumble’s mouth did the same.

“What,” Bonesy said. “What. I miss things, being dead. What is it.”

“High ground,” I said, and got up.

The high ground was a shelf of broken rock above the spring, screened by scrub, with a clean line out past the reef to the deep blue where she lay. We went up on our bellies the last of it and lay in the scrub and watched.

She was busy now. The easy riding was gone out of her, the sails hauled up and hanging slack against her masts as the men worked the falls, and the long shape of a boat swung out on her davits, slow against the bright water.

“Rowboat,” Crumble said.

“Aye.”

A crew at the oars, armed to the teeth. The reef that wrecked a hull was an open invitation to a boat drawing two feet.

That was the trouble, and it sat us both back on our heels. Three years gone, the pirate had brought his whole ship in after us, fat and sure as a pig walking itself to the butcher knife, run her into the cove on his own arrogance, and we had only to be there and let the ground do the rest. It had been easy. This was a different man.

Bonesy watched our faces and smiled, the way he smiled the last time me and Crumble ran aground.

“You weren’t expecting him to drop a rowboat in, were you,” he said.

Neither of us answered.

But a day from now there would be two more ships among us. After a day, a whole fleet. The hunt had gone out the moment the finger left the box, and every hour we sat here it drew its net a little tighter.

Crumble looked at me. I looked at him. The only way that ship came onto the rocks was if the man at her helm stopped thinking. You have to take it from him. You have to make him feel he has no choice. You have to take the choice from him the way you take it from a slave, leave him nothing in his hands but the one road you’ve left open, and watch him walk it thinking it was his own feet that chose.
