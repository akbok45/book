The boat came in on the slack water with the oars dipping easy, ten men in her, the morning sun bright on the steel they’d brought to take three tired pirates off a rock. They came the way a dog comes for a thrown bone, sure of the having, eyes on nothing but the prize. They did not look up.

The cove was ringed in palms that lean out over the water, and a man in the crook of one is a shadow among shadows to an eye fixed on the beach, and I had been in mine since before they cleared the slot. I watched the boat come under me. The bald spot on the bow oar, the soft clean neck of the one in the stern sheets who wore his coat better than the rest. I let them slide in under the tree. I let the bow grate on the sand.

Then I came down on them out of the green.

I took the steersman first. I came down with the whole weight of the drop behind the upper sword and drove it in at the join of his neck and shoulder, down through the meat of him, and felt it grate and stop on bone deep in his chest. He folded over it. The blade was stuck and I put my boot on his shoulder and tore it free and the wound opened and gave up its dark wash down his front, and he went into the bilge thrashing, heels drumming the boards.

There is no art to it, in a boat. A boat is a pen. Men in a boat cannot run, cannot get round you, cannot bring their numbers to bear. They can only stand up into the thing killing them, one and one and one, and a man standing up in a rocking boat is a man handing you his belly. The next one came up with his cutlass half drawn and I took the hand off at the wrist before the steel cleared the scabbard, clean, the hand dropping with the hilt still in its grip, and he stood a moment staring at the stump pumping bright into the sun before I put the point under his jaw and up. He bit down on the blade as it went through. I felt his teeth shut on the steel.

The man behind him slipped in his fellow’s blood and went down to one knee in the bilge, and I brought the pommel of the lower sword across his temple and the bone gave with a wet crack and his eye started from the socket, and he pitched forward over the thwart and lay with his legs still in the dead steersman’s lap, twitching. Another came at me low with a bayonet and laid the back of my forearm open, and I let him have the arm and took his throat with the short blade, sawed it, felt the gristle of the windpipe part under the edge, and turned him loose to drown in the sound he was making.

Crumble came in over the bow with Balam’s blade. He took a man through the kidney from behind and ripped sideways, the way you gut a fish, and the man came apart at the middle and sat down in what came out of him, holding it, trying to gather it back. Another swung an oar at Crumble’s head and Crumble ducked it and opened his thigh to the bone and left him to fall, and he fell screaming, both hands jammed in the wound and the blood coming through his fingers in ropes.

“BEAUTIFUL,” Bonesy was howling from the rocks, up on his feet, clapping his hard hands together with a sound like stones knocked. “Oh, the LEFT one, Crumble, mind the left, there’s a — yes! YES! Down he goes! Did you see it!”

It was a butchering. I will set it down as that, because I have heard men call these things fights after, and they lie to make themselves easier to live with. We closed the boat from both ends toward the middle and killed them in it with the sun on the water, and one we did not finish clean, a young one, gut-cut and folded over the thwart still moving, and I took him by the collar and the belt and pitched him over the side into the lagoon to bleed out where he wouldn’t be underfoot, and he went down in a slow red bloom with the little fish already rising to him. The cove that had not heard a human sound in a hundred years heard all of it.

And we kept two.

That was the cold part, the part we’d settled on the rock with a look and no words. The soft-necked officer, because an officer is worth more breathing. And one big slow oarsman who’d dropped his blade and thrown his hands up, which on another morning might have bought him something. We bound them.

Bonesy came down off the rocks and crouched in front of the two of them, his skull a hand’s width from their faces, and looked them over the way you’d look over fish on a market slab.

He crouches close, looks them over. “Hello, fellas.”

The rest we hauled up. Crumble took the shoulders and I took the feet and we carried the dead up off the sand to the high shelf one by one, eight of them, and laid them out along the rock where the line was clean to the sea, where a man a half mile off with a glass to his eye would have no trouble at all counting what was left of the boat he’d sent.

“Then let’s give him something to watch,” said Crumble.

I went to the oarsman first. He was crying. I opened his throat and sent him off the rock and he went in with barely a sound.

The officer did not cry. He watched me come the way a fish holds still when a heron’s shadow crosses the water above it, still because still is all that is left to it. I cut the back of both his knees and stood him up and put my boot to him.

He went in screaming. The coat filled with water and dragged him and he came up anyway, both hands beating at the surface, the ruined legs trailing dark behind him. He got his chin up once. Went under. Came up again with less in it. The coat took him on the third.

I heard him stop.

All eight bodies went into the water off the rock, none laid out on it. Fixed.

I was already cleaning the blade.
