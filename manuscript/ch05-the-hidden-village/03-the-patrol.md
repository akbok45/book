The boys came in at first light.

There were two of them in a canoe meant for one, and they were paddling as if the lake were on fire behind them. I heard them before I saw them. They were shouting the same word over and over, high and cracked, and the dogs took it up all along the shore.

I was out of my hammock with my swords in my hand before I was awake.

Bonesy was already standing in the doorway, looking out.

“What are they saying?”

“Spaniards,” he said. “On the river.”

⸻

By the time I reached the great tree half the island was there.

The boys stood in the middle, wet to the waist and still out of breath. They talked over each other. The older one held his arms wide to show something long. Then he held up both hands and opened and shut his fingers.

Bonesy stood beside me and gave it to me as they went.

“A big canoe. Oars both sides. Men with long guns. Fourteen, maybe. Coming up slow.”

“Where?”

Bonesy listened.

“Past where the river splits. Past the dead tree.” He listened again. “Nobody comes past the dead tree.”

The people round the fire had gone quiet. Some of the women had their children by the arm. The grey fisherman stood with his spear. The man with the jaguar’s shadow stood behind him with a bow.

Nobody got up to speak for the rest. There was nobody to get up. People spoke where they stood, one and then another, and the others listened.

Ah Pech spoke first.

He did not raise his voice. He spread his scarred hands flat, palms down, and pressed them slowly toward the ground.

“He says let them pass,” Bonesy said. “Hide the canoes. Put out the fires. They’ve never found the path to the lake. They won’t find it now.”

Some of the old people nodded.

Amara shook her head.

“She says the sloop’s in the creek,” Bonesy said. “They’ll find it. Then they’ll know somebody’s up here, and they’ll look till they find the path.”

Ah Pech answered her. She answered him back. Neither of them raised their voice.

“He says the sloop’s not theirs to worry about,” Bonesy said. “She says the sloop brought them, so it is.”

Crumble had said nothing. He was looking at the ground between his feet.

“I need to see the boat,” I said.

Everybody looked at me. Most of them had not understood the words.

“I’m not planning a fight off what two boys saw from a canoe,” I said to Crumble. “I need to see the boat. How many, how they sit, what they carry.”

Crumble looked at me a moment. Then he nodded and turned and spoke to the grey fisherman. The fisherman looked at me with those black eyes, the whole of them black, and said something short.

“He’ll take us,” Crumble said. “He knows the river.”

“How long till they reach the bend below the dead tree?”

Crumble asked. The fisherman looked at the sun.

“Rowing against the current, in a boat that size,” Crumble said, “not before the middle of the afternoon.”

Amara was already moving.

She did not say anything to the crowd. She went to the nearest house and spoke to the woman in the doorway, and the woman went in and came out with a bundle and two small children. Amara went on to the next house, and the next. Where she went, women picked up their children and the old people were helped to their feet, and they went off along the paths toward the gardens on the high ground at the far end of the island.

The old man with the roots did not go. He could not. His daughter brought a stool out and sat down beside him and put her hand on his.

⸻

We went down the path at a run, the fisherman first.

He ran the way he paddled, easy and long, and he did not look back to see if we kept up. Crumble ran behind him without a sound. I made enough noise for the three of us.

The path met the river at the bend.

I saw at once why the boys had come home so fast. The river came round a great grey stone, half out of the water and white with bird dirt. Below the stone the near bank stood straight up out of the water, higher than a man, red earth bound with roots. Trees leaned out over it. The deep water ran fast and brown right under that bank, and every stick and leaf that came round the stone was carried in under it.

I stopped and looked at it a long time.

The fisherman did not stop. He went on along the bank, downstream, through the trees. We followed him for half an hour, until the river opened into a long straight reach. There he lay down in the reeds at the edge, and we lay down beside him.

We heard them before we saw them.

A creak and a splash. A creak and a splash. And a man’s voice calling the stroke in Spanish, bored and hoarse, the way a man calls it who has been calling it since dawn.

Then they came out from behind the trees at the bottom of the reach.

She was a ship’s launch, long and broad and black with tar, and she sat deep with the weight of men. Six oars a side. Between the rowers sat soldiers with their muskets upright between their knees. Most of them wore steel caps. A man stood in the bow with a pole, sounding, and calling the depth back over his shoulder.

“Count,” I said under my breath.

Crumble counted with me. Twelve rowers. Fourteen soldiers, with the man in the bow. A steersman at the big oar in the stern. And beside the steersman a man in a good blue coat, sitting with a sword across his lap and a hat with a feather in it.

“Twenty-eight,” Crumble said.

“Twelve of them are rowers.”

“Rowers have knives.”

“Rowers have oars in their hands.”

On the bow post there was a swivel gun, a little brass piece on an iron fork, with a leather cap over the touchhole. A soldier sat beside it. He had a clay pot between his feet with a lid on it, and a thin thread of smoke came up out of the lid.

“He keeps the match in the pot,” I said. “For the swivel.”

“And the muskets?”

I looked along the boat.

None of the other soldiers had a match lit. You do not keep fourteen matches burning on a long row up a river. You keep one going in a pot, and when you need the rest you light them from it, one after another.

That takes time.

As we watched, one of the soldiers in the waist stood up and pointed at the bank. A caiman lay on the mud there with its jaws open. Two of the soldiers lit their matches from the pot and blew on them, and put their pieces up, and fired.

The first one flashed in the pan and did not go off. The man swore and primed again. The second one fired on the third try. The ball went into the mud a yard from the caiman, and the caiman slid into the water, and the soldiers laughed.

The officer stood up in the stern and shouted at them for wasting powder.

“Damp,” Crumble said.

“They’ve been on this river a week.”

I rolled onto my back in the reeds and looked up.

The sky over the river was white and hot. But over the hills to the west, where the river came from, the clouds were building. They were piling up on each other, grey at the bottom and white on top. I have watched clouds pile up like that over the land from the sea, every afternoon of the wet season, and break before evening.

“Does it rain here every afternoon?” I said.

Crumble asked the fisherman. The fisherman looked at the clouds and said one word.

“Every afternoon,” Crumble said. “About the middle of it.”

I lay there and looked at the clouds.

“Crumble.”

“I know,” he said.

⸻

We went back up the bank to the bend and lay down at the top of it in the leaves, where we could see the stone and the water under us.

The fisherman took a stick and drew the river in the mud. The stone. The channel. The bank. He drew it better than I could have.

“Here,” I said, and put my finger on the stone. “They have to come round it close. The rest is shoal.”

The fisherman nodded when Crumble put it to him.

“When they come round it the current wants to put them under this bank. The steersman holds her out with the big oar.” I drew the oar. “If the oar stops, they come in under us. Broadside. Too close to row out and too close to use the guns.”

“And the oar stops how?” Crumble said.

“Bonesy.”

Crumble looked at the river.

“He’s on the bottom at the head of the bend,” I said. “When the stern comes past, he takes the blade of that oar and holds it.”

“That oar’s a big oar.”

“He held a jar against the pull of the pool.”

Crumble said nothing to that.

“We go in the rain,” I said. “When it’s coming down hard. They can’t light a match in that. They can’t keep the powder dry in the pans. Every musket in the boat is a club.”

“The swivel.”

“The swivel has a cap on it and a match in a pot. That one can still fire.” I looked at him. “That’s yours. You go onto the bow first and you take the gunner before he gets the lid off.”

Crumble looked at the bow in the mud.

“And you?”

“I go into the middle. Where the soldiers are.”

“How many bows have they got?”

He asked the fisherman. The fisherman held up eight fingers.

“Put them on the far bank,” I said. “In the trees, down past the stone. When the boat swings in, they shoot first. Every head in that boat turns to look at the far bank. That’s when we drop.”

“And the spears go on top here.”

“The spears come down after us.”

The fisherman had been listening to Crumble’s Maya. He said something and drew a line in the mud with his stick, going downstream away from the lake.

Crumble nodded.

“He says if it goes wrong, nobody runs toward the lake path,” he said. “We run downriver into the trees and make them follow us there.”

“Good.”

I looked at the mud a while longer. The stone. The bank. The oar.

I could find nothing wrong with it. I looked again anyway.

The fisherman stood up. He looked at the sky, and at the drawing, and at me. Then he went off up the path toward the lake at a run.

⸻

They came in the middle of the morning.

There were twenty of them, perhaps, with spears and bows. They came down the path in a line without a sound. The man with the jaguar’s shadow led the bowmen across the river above the stone, swimming with their bows held over their heads, and they went into the trees on the far side and were gone. The spearmen lay down along the top of the bank beside us.

Ah Pech came with them. He had a war club in his hand, flat and heavy and edged with black stone. He lay down a little way from me and did not look at me.

Amara came last. She had a bow and a bag of linen over her shoulder. She lay down behind the spearmen, back from the edge, and laid the arrows out in front of her on the leaves.

Bonesy came down the path with his hat in his hand.

He looked at the stone, and the water, and the drawing in the mud. Crumble told him.

He listened all the way through.

“Just hold it,” he said.

“Just hold it.”

“How long?”

“Till the boat’s under the bank.”

He looked at the river.

“Bit cold,” he said.

He put his hat on and walked down to the water at the head of the bend. He looked back at us once. Then he waded in, and the brown water came up over his ribs and his shoulders and his hat, and he was gone.

We lay in the leaves and waited.

The forest went on with its noise as if nothing had happened. Birds. Insects. A monkey roaring a long way off.

The clouds came on over the hills. The light went yellow, then grey. The air under the trees went still and heavy, and the birds stopped.

Somewhere down the river, faint, I heard a creak and a splash.

A drop of rain hit the leaf in front of my face.

Then another.
