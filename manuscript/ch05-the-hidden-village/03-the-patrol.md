The boys came in at first light.

There were two of them in a canoe meant for one, and they were paddling as if the lake were on fire behind them. I heard them before I saw them. They were shouting the same word over and over, high and cracked, and the dogs took it up all along the shore.

I was out of my hammock with my swords in my hand before I was awake.

Bonesy was already standing in the doorway, looking out.

“What are they saying?”

“Spaniards,” he said. “On the river.”

⸻

By the time I reached the great tree half the island was there.

The boys stood in the middle, wet to the waist and still out of breath. They talked over each other. The older one held his hands apart to show something long, then held up both hands and opened and shut his fingers.

Bonesy stood beside me and said it into my ear as they went.

“A big canoe. Oars on both sides. Men with long guns. Fourteen, maybe. Coming up slow.”

“Where?”

Bonesy listened.

“Past the place where the river splits. Past the dead tree.” He listened again. “Nobody comes past the dead tree.”

The people round the fire had gone quiet. Some of the women had their children by the arm. The grey fisherman had his spear, and the man with the jaguar’s shadow stood behind him with a bow.

Nobody stood up to speak for them. There was nobody to stand up. People talked where they stood, one and then another, and the rest listened.

Ah Pech spoke first.

He did not raise his voice. He spoke slowly and spread his scarred hands flat, palms down, and pressed them toward the ground.

“He says let them pass,” Bonesy said. “Hide the canoes. Put out the fires. They’ve never found the path to the lake. They won’t find it now.”

Some of the old people nodded.

Amara shook her head.

She did not wait to be asked. She spoke short and fast, and she pointed down toward the river.

“She says the sloop is in the creek,” Bonesy said. “They’ll find it. Then they’ll know somebody’s up here. Then they’ll look for the path.”

Ah Pech said something else. Amara answered him. Her voice did not rise either, but it got harder.

“He says the sloop isn’t theirs to worry about,” Bonesy said. “She says the sloop led them here, so it is.”

Crumble had not said anything. He was looking at the ground.

I looked at the boys.

“Ask them what kind of boat.”

Bonesy asked them in English. The older boy looked at him, then at me. Amara said it again in Maya.

The boy answered.

“A long one,” Amara said to me. “Heavy. Six oars a side. A man at the back with a big oar to steer.”

A periagua, then, or a ship’s launch. Fourteen men in a boat like that sit low and turn slow. Up a river they are tied to the channel like an ox to a cart.

“Is there a place where the channel runs close to the bank?” I said. “Close enough to drop into the boat?”

Amara looked at me for a moment.

“The bend below the dead tree,” she said. “The river goes round a big stone there. The deep water runs right under the high bank. Everything goes under that bank. Canoes, logs. Anything.”

“How high is the bank?”

She held her hand flat above her head.

“And the trees?”

“Right to the edge.”

I looked at Crumble. He looked back.

Neither of us had to say it.

“If they come round that stone,” I said, “and the steering oar goes, the current puts them under the bank. They’ll be under us before they know where they are.”

“How does the steering oar go?” Crumble said.

I looked at Bonesy.

Bonesy looked at the river.

“Oh,” he said.

Nobody gave an order after that. I watched for it and it did not come. The grey fisherman went for more spears. The man with the jaguar’s shadow went off with three others carrying bows. Two of the women ran to put out the fires along the shore. Ah Pech stood a while longer by the tree with his hands at his sides.

Then he went into his house and came out with a war club, flat and heavy and edged with black stone, and walked down to the canoes with the rest.

⸻

We crossed the lake and went down the path at a run.

Amara went first. She ran barefoot and did not make a sound, and the rest of us crashed after her like cattle. The path came down off the high ground and met the river where it bent round a great grey stone, half out of the water and white with bird dirt.

She was right about the bank. It stood straight up out of the river higher than a man, red earth bound with roots. Trees leaned out over the edge. Below it the water ran deep and brown and fast, and every leaf and stick that came round the stone was carried in under the bank and along it.

We lay down along the top in the leaves. The bowmen went farther along, where they could shoot down into the water. The spearmen lay at the edge. Crumble lay down beside me with Balam’s blade across his forearms.

Amara stayed back from the edge with a bow of her own. She had a bag of linen over her shoulder.

Bonesy walked down to the water at the head of the bend.

He looked back at us once and touched his hat. Then he waded in and went under, and the brown water closed over the hat, and he was gone.

We waited.

The forest went on with its noise as if nothing had happened. Birds. Insects. Something moving in the branches over the far bank. A long way off a monkey roared and stopped.

I lay with my cheek on the leaves and listened to the river.

I heard the oars first. They came round the long reach below the stone, slow and heavy. A creak and a splash, a creak and a splash. Then a man’s voice calling the stroke in Spanish.

Then I smelled the match.

You do not forget it, once you have smelled it on a deck before a fight. Slow match smoulders in a musket lock with a sour grey stink like a wet fire, and fourteen of them make a cloud of it. It came up the river ahead of the boat.

I put my hand on Crumble’s arm. He nodded.

The boat came round the stone.

She was a ship’s launch, long and broad and black with tar, deep in the water with the weight of men. Six oars a side, as the boy had said. The soldiers sat between the rowers with their muskets upright between their knees and the match glowing in the cocks. Most of them wore steel caps. A man in a good blue coat sat in the stern beside the steersman, with a sword across his lap and a hat with a feather in it.

He was looking up at the trees. He did not look at the bank right above him.

The current took the bow, and the steersman leaned on his oar to hold her out in the stream.

The oar stopped moving.

He leaned on it again. It would not come. He stood up and threw his whole weight on it, and I saw his face go from anger to puzzlement as the blade would not budge in the water.

The bow swung in toward the bank.

Somebody shouted. The rowers on the near side pulled to bring her out, and the far side caught crabs and fouled their oars, and the launch came round broadside in the current and drove in under the bank directly beneath us.

The arrows went in.

They came down out of the trees with no more noise than birds. A rower went over backward off his thwart. A soldier stood up with an arrow through his shoulder and sat down again. The officer was on his feet and shouting. He pointed up at the trees, at the far bank, at nothing.

“Fire!” he shouted. “Fire!”

Six or seven of them got their pieces up and fired into the trees. The noise under that bank was enormous. Smoke rolled across the water, white and thick, and for a moment I could not see the boat at all.

I went over the edge into it.

⸻

I landed on a thwart and a man under it, and we both went down among the oars.

I was up first. I had both swords out. There was smoke everywhere and men shouting in it and the boat rocking under my feet like a horse that has not been broken.

A soldier came at me through the smoke with his musket clubbed, swinging it at my head like a flail. I went under it and cut him across the belly with the left sword and across the face with the right as he folded. He fell against the gunwale and over it.

Another was trying to blow on his match. I put a point through his arm before he got it lit, and he dropped the piece and fell back over the thwart behind him.

I heard Crumble land in the bow behind me. I heard Balam’s blade go in.

The spearmen were dropping down off the bank now. Some came down into the boat and some into the shallows along the side. The grey fisherman went into the water and came up by the stern with his spear, and the steersman screamed at the sight of his face and let go of the oar.

I was moving down the boat.

I knew where every man was. I knew it the way you know the pitch of a deck under you in the dark, without having to look. A man rose up on my right and I had cut him before I saw his face. Another came from the left with a short sword and I turned it with one blade and took him under the arm with the other.

My heart was going like a drum at the capstan. My mouth was open. I was grinning.

I had not felt so good in a year.

A soldier came up out of the bottom of the boat in front of me. He was young. His steel cap had fallen off, and he had a musket in his hands but no match in it.

He looked at me.

He threw the musket down into the water and went down on his knees in the bilge, with his hands up in front of his face.

“Mercy,” he said, in Spanish. “Mercy, señor, for the love of God.”

My right sword was already moving.

⸻

The deck was white in the sun, and the merchant’s crew had thrown down their arms.

There were nine of them. They stood along the rail by the mainmast with their hands up, and their mate stood in front of them. He was a big grey man with a gold ring in one ear. He had his hands up too.

“Enough,” he was saying. “Enough, señor. We yield. The ship is yours.”

I heard him say it.

I was still moving.

I do not know why. I have gone back to it many times since and I still do not know. The fight was over. The ship was ours. Crumble had already lowered his blade. And something in me was not finished, and the mate was in front of it.

I killed him. Then the man behind him, who had a hand on the mate’s shoulder. Then the one who tried to run.

When I stopped there was nobody left by the mainmast standing.

I turned round.

Crumble was standing by the hatch, looking at me. He did not say anything. His face was not angry. It was something else, and I have never had a word for it.

Bonesy was sitting up on the rail behind him with his hat in his hands. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the mate.

Neither of them said anything that day. Or the next. Or ever.

⸻

The boy was looking up at me with his hands in front of his face.

My sword had stopped a hand’s breadth from his neck.

I did not remember stopping it. It was simply there, not moving, with the point trembling a little. The boy’s breath went in and out against it. I could see the pulse beating in his throat.

I could feel the rest of the cut in my arm. It wanted to finish.

I held it.

I held it a long time. Longer than I have held anything.

Then I took the point away.

The boy made a sound and bent over his knees in the bilge water.

I looked up.

Crumble was in the bow. His blade was down. There was blood on it and blood on his forearm to the elbow, and he was standing still, looking at me.

He had seen it. All of it. The sword going and the sword stopping.

He did not say anything.

He looked at me a moment longer. Then he turned away and went to help the grey fisherman drag a wounded soldier up out of the shallows.

⸻

It was over fast.

Nine of the Spaniards were dead. Four more were wounded and had thrown down their arms. The boy made five. Nobody had got away, because the launch had been their only way to go, and Bonesy was still underneath it holding the steering oar.

He came up when the shouting stopped. His hat came out of the water first, then his skull, and then the rest of him walking up the bank with weed in his ribs.

He looked at the boat.

“Did it work?”

“It worked,” I said.

“Good.” He looked down at his hands. “That oar didn’t want to stay still.”

One of ours was hurt. A spearman had taken a musket ball through the meat of his thigh in the first volley. He was lying at the top of the bank with his teeth set and his hands pressed to it, and blood coming out between his fingers.

Amara was already kneeling beside him with the linen out.

She did not hurry and she did not hesitate. She cut the cloth away with her knife and looked at the hole on both sides and said something to him that made him laugh through his teeth. Then she packed it and bound it, tight, and sat back on her heels.

She looked up and saw me watching.

I looked away first.

The officer was dead in the stern with an arrow in his chest. His feathered hat had come off and floated a little way downstream before it caught in the roots.

I went through his coat.

There was a purse with silver in it, and a flask, and a little book of prayers. In an inside pocket there was a packet of oiled cloth, tied with tape. Inside were two papers.

I unfolded the first one.

I had not read Spanish written in that hand since I was a boy at school. It came back to me as if I had never left. The tall letters, the flourishes. The seal at the bottom in red wax, with an eagle in it.

I read it through twice.

Crumble had come up beside me. He waited.

“It’s an order,” I said. “From Cárdenas. To every officer commanding a boat on this coast.”

“Saying what?”

I read it to him in English.

“You will search the mouths of every river between such and such a point and such another for a sloop of English build, carrying two men and a walking skeleton. You will take them living if you can, and the cargo in any case. It is believed they go to the daughter of the Indian Balam, who is said to live up one of these rivers, and who reads the old writing. This is upon the testimony of the Indian called Domingo, taken at Petit-Goâve.”

Crumble did not say anything.

I looked at him. His hands had closed. Both of them, at his sides, so hard that the linen I had bound round them on the sloop had gone red again at the knuckles.

“Taken,” he said.

“That’s the word.”

He looked down at the river for a long time.

“Read the other one,” he said.

The second paper was a letter, not an order. It was addressed to the dead officer by name, from somebody in Campeche who signed himself his cousin. Most of it was family. A wedding. A dead horse. A debt.

At the bottom there was a paragraph I read three times.

“What is it?” Crumble said.

“News from Spain.” I folded the letter. “There’s a peace. Made at Madrid, between our King and theirs.”

“Peace.”

“England’s promised to put down the brethren. No more commissions against Spain. Port Royal’s to hang anybody it catches.”

Crumble was quiet.

I thought of Port Royal. The taverns along the waterfront and the ships in the harbour and the men who drank there. There had always been Port Royal. Whatever we did on the Main, there had always been somewhere on the English side to come back to.

I put the papers in my coat.

The boy was sitting on the bank with his hands tied in front of him. He had not stopped shaking. When I came over and crouched down in front of him, he flinched as if I had raised a hand.

I asked him in Spanish how they had known to come up this river.

He looked at me. He looked at my face for a long time, and I saw him puzzle at it. A Spaniard’s face on a man who had come out of the trees with the Indians.

“A fisherman,” he said. “At the river mouth. We stopped him three days ago. He said he saw an English sloop go in over the bar. Ten days before.”

“And the others? The other boats?”

“Every river on the coast. The captain said Don Mateo wants them all searched.”

“Does Cárdenas know where we are?”

“He knows this river now.” He swallowed. “He will know it better when we do not come back.”

I stood up.

Amara was standing a little way off. She had heard all of it. She was looking down the river, the way the launch had come.

Behind her Ah Pech was wiping his club on the grass. He did it slowly, both sides of the blade, and he did not look up.

⸻

We sank the launch in the deep water under the bank, with stones in her, and carried the dead into the forest. The wounded Spaniards and the boy we took back across the lake with their hands tied. Nobody said yet what would be done with them.

That night I sat in the doorway of our house and cleaned my right sword.

I cleaned it with sand from the shore and then with a rag and then with the oil from my pack. I worked the oil into the steel along the whole length of it and wiped it off, and held it up to the firelight and looked along the edge.

It was clean. It had been clean for an hour.

I started again at the hilt.

Crumble came up from the water and stood in front of me. He looked at the sword. He looked at me.

He did not say anything.

After a while he sat down on the step beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and looked out at the lake.

I kept cleaning.
