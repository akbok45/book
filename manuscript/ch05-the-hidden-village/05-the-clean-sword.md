Nobody spoke to me.

They spoke to each other. Low, in Maya, with their backs to me. The spearmen climbed down into the launch and stripped her. They threw the muskets up onto the bank, and the swivel, and the powder kegs, and the officer’s sword. They took the knives off the dead. They did not take anything else.

They did not ask me to help. I did not offer.

I sat down on a root at the edge of the water with my sword across my knees.

My fingers had opened at last. They would not stop shaking. I held them flat against the blade to keep them still and watched the rain run off the trees.

At the top of the bank Amara was kneeling beside the spearman who had been shot.

The ball had gone through the meat of his thigh. It came from the one piece in that boat that fired all day. He lay on his back with his teeth set and both hands pressed to it, and blood was coming out between his fingers. She cut the cloth away with her knife and looked at the hole on both sides. She said something to him, and he laughed through his teeth.

Then she packed it and bound it, round and over and tight.

She did not look down the bank at me. Not once.

⸻

When they had stripped the launch they filled her with stones from the river.

They passed them down hand to hand from the shallows, big ones, and stacked them in the bilge on top of what was lying there. When she was low enough they pushed her out into the deep water under the bank. She went down slowly, the stern first, with the rain still falling on her. Then the brown water closed over the bow and the swivel’s fork, and she was gone.

The officer they had carried up onto the bank before they sank her.

I went up and knelt beside him.

His blue coat was black with blood and rain. I went through it. There was a purse with silver in it, and a flask, and a small book of prayers with a ribbon in it. In an inside pocket there was a packet of oilskin, tied up tight with tape.

I tried to untie the tape. My fingers would not do it.

I tried again. Then I put the packet in my coat and stood up.

Nobody had watched me do it except Ah Pech. He was standing at the edge of the trees with his club in his hand. When I looked at him, he looked away.

They carried the dead into the forest, two men to each, and came back without them. I did not ask where they had put them.

⸻

We came back across the lake in the last of the light.

The rain had stopped. The water was flat and grey and still, and the paddles went in without a sound. Nobody in my canoe spoke. The two spearmen who had laughed at Bonesy’s ribs that first day sat in the bow now, as far from me as the canoe allowed.

The women had brought the children back from the high ground. They were standing along the shore when we came in. They looked at the men climbing out of the canoes, and counted them, and when the count came right some of them sat down on the shore where they were.

Then they looked at me.

I was still red to the elbows. I had washed my hands in the lake and my arms, but my shirt was stiff with it, and there was more in my hair.

The boy who had slept against my knee was standing at the front with his mother. He saw me and started forward.

His mother caught him by the arm and pulled him back behind her skirt.

She did not say anything. She just kept her hand on him, and looked at me over his head, until I had gone past.

I walked up the shell path toward our house.

The old man with the roots was sitting outside his door on his stool. His grandchildren were on his roots. They went quiet when they saw me coming.

I waited for him to shout.

He did not shout. He watched me go by, all the way past his house, and did not say a word.

⸻

They lit the fire under the great tree that night as they did every night. I could see it from our doorway, through the houses. People sat round it. They were not telling stories.

Crumble was there. He sat beside Ah Pech, with Amara on his other side. Once Ah Pech said something to him and he nodded. Once Amara put her hand on his arm, and he let her leave it there.

He did not look toward our house.

I sat in the doorway and cleaned my sword.

I cleaned it with sand from the shore and then with a rag and then with the oil from my pack. I worked the oil into the steel along the whole length of the blade, and wiped it off, and held it up to the firelight and looked down the edge.

It was clean. It had been clean for an hour.

I started again at the hilt.

Bonesy came up from the water after a while and sat down on the step beside me. He had his hat on his knees. He did not say anything. He watched me work.

The packet was still in my coat. I could feel the corner of it against my ribs every time I moved my arm.

I did not take it out.

Over at the fire somebody put on more wood, and the sparks went up into the dark. Crumble’s face showed in the light for a moment, and then the fire settled and it was gone.

He did not come down to the house.

I kept cleaning.
