Crumble was at the water before light.

I had slept badly again. I came out of the house in the grey with my swords in my hand and saw him standing in the lake up to his knees, with his back to the island and his face to the east.

He did not hear me. Or he heard me and did not turn round.

The sky over the far trees went from grey to yellow. When the first edge of the sun came over them, he said something under his breath. Two words. I had heard him say them every morning for a year. On the sloop, in taverns, once with a pistol in his face. I had never asked what they were.

He said them, and he bent and touched the water, and that was all.

Then he came out of the lake and drew Balam’s blade and began his forms.

I sat down on an upturned canoe and watched him.

I had seen him do them on deck a hundred times. Here it was different. On the sloop he did them small, with the boom over his head and the rail at his hip. Here he had the whole shore. He went down it slowly, step and cut and turn, so slowly that I could see the weight go from one foot to the other. Then back up it fast, so fast the blade sang.

He did not look like a man practising. He looked like a man saying something he had learned by heart.

Somebody came down the path behind me.

It was Ah Pech. He had a gourd of water in one hand and nothing in the other. He stopped at the edge of the shore and watched Crumble go down it slowly again.

Crumble saw him and did not stop.

Ah Pech watched him through the whole of it. When Crumble came round into the turn at the bottom, he walked out onto the sand. He put his own bare foot against the side of Crumble’s left foot and pushed it.

A hand’s width. No more.

He said nothing. He walked back to the edge of the shore and stood with his water.

Crumble stood where he had been put. He looked down at his foot. Then he went back to the top of the shore and did the whole form again, slowly, and when he came round into the turn at the bottom his left foot went where Ah Pech had put it.

The blade came round faster. I heard the difference.

Ah Pech nodded once and went back up the path.

Crumble stood breathing for a while with the point of the blade in the sand. Then he wiped it and put it up and came and sat on the canoe beside me.

“He fought beside him,” he said.

“He knows your feet better than you do.”

Crumble looked at his feet.

“Balam used to hit that ankle with a stick,” he said. “Every morning. For a year.”

“Didn’t take.”

“It took.” He rubbed the ankle. “Then I went back to sea. Decks move.”

I had seen him fight a dozen men on a dozen decks. I had never once seen anything wrong with his feet.

“What were you saying?” I said. “In the water.”

He looked at me.

“Every morning. Before the sun. Two words.”

He was quiet for a while.

“The day,” he said. “Its name. He made me stand in the lake at first light till I could say it without counting on my fingers.”

“How long did that take?”

“The first time he made me stand there till noon.”

He got up. He looked out at the lake, at the place where he had stood.

“I’d say it to him,” he said. “He’d say if I was right.”

Then he went up the path after Ah Pech.

Out in the lake the water where he had stood had gone smooth again.

The grey fisherman’s canoe had a split in it.

I saw it that morning from the shore. He had her turned over on two logs and he was crouched beside her, picking at the crack with his thumbnail. It ran a forearm’s length along the bottom, from a knot in the wood, and he had been patching it the whole time I had been on the island. I could see the old patches round it.

I went down to look before I had decided to.

He looked up at me with those black eyes. Then he looked back at the crack.

I crouched down on the other side of the hull. It was a good canoe, cut from one cedar log with an adze. The wood was sound. It was only the knot that had let go.

“You want it stopped properly,” I said.

He did not understand a word. He watched me.

I went back to our house. I had a little oakum in my pack, picked from old rope on the sloop, and a pot of pitch I had brought up from the creek in case. I came back with them, and a knife, and a stone to drive the oakum with.

I raked out his old patch with the knife. I rolled the oakum between my palms into a thread and drove it into the crack with the stone, the way you caulk a seam on a deck, and then I warmed the pitch over his fire and paid it over the top.

He watched me do all of it with his arms folded.

When I had finished I sat back on my heels and looked at it. It was a good seam. I had caulked worse on a man-of-war.

The fisherman reached down and put his thumb on the pitch, and pressed.

The oakum went down into the crack under his thumb. The pitch split round it.

He looked at me.

“Spaniard,” he said.

It was the first word I had heard him say that I knew. I stared at him.

“Ship,” he said, and pointed at my seam. He said it in Spanish too, badly. Then he knocked on the hull with his knuckle. “No ship.”

“You speak Spanish.”

He held up his finger and thumb a little way apart.

“Salt,” he said. “Coast. Buy salt.” He pointed down the river, and then at his mouth, and showed me his teeth. “They don’t want talk. Want salt, go.”

I could believe it.

“It’s a good seam,” I said, in Spanish. “On a ship.”

“On a ship, good,” he agreed. “On a log, bad. Log moves.” He bent his hands together and apart, like a man wringing a cloth. “Ship is many woods. Log is one wood. One wood moves all together.”

He took my knife from me. He did not ask.

He dug out every bit of my oakum and threw it in the lake. Then he went up the bank and came back with a handful of grey bark from some tree I did not know, and a clay pot of something gummy and red that he had been warming at the back of his fire. He chewed the bark. He chewed it till it was soft and stringy, and spat it into his hand, and pressed it into the crack with his thumb. Then he paid the red gum over it with a stick and smoothed it with a wet palm.

He sat back and looked at me.

I put my thumb on it and pressed. It gave a little and came back, like the skin on the back of your hand.

“Huh,” I said.

“Huh,” he said.

He splashed water up onto his neck from the lake with one hand. The slits along it opened and shut. He did it every little while, without looking, the way a man wipes his brow.

“Does that hurt?” I said, and touched my own neck.

He thought about the Spanish.

“When dry,” he said.

“And the teeth?”

He grinned at me with all of them.

“Fish don’t complain,” he said.

I laughed. It came out of me before I knew it was there.

He looked pleased with himself. Then he looked at me again, at my face, the way the old man with the roots did. He tapped his own cheek.

“Spaniard face,” he said.

“My face is a liar.”

He considered that. He showed me his teeth again.

“Mine also,” he said.

When the gum had set he turned the canoe over and ran her down into the shallows. He stood in the water to his knees and pushed down on her gunwale with both hands, and held her there, and looked at the place inside where the crack was.

It stayed dry.

He climbed in and pushed off and paddled away along the shore without looking back.

The coral man’s wife fed me that night.

I did not know she meant to. I was walking back from the canoes at dusk, and she was standing in her doorway, and she called something and pointed at me and then at her fire. When I did not move she came out and took me by the sleeve and walked me in.

Bonesy was already there. He was sitting by the fire with two of her children on his knees, and he looked up when I came in as if he had been waiting for me.

“She says sit,” he said.

I sat.

It was a small house, one room, with the hammocks rolled up and hung on the poles for the evening. A pot was on three stones over the fire. The coral man sat on the far side of it with his shoulder bare. The binding I had put on it was gone. The skin underneath had closed pink and new, and there was no coral on it yet.

He saw me look at it and touched it, and said something to his wife, and she said something back. They both laughed.

“He says you bind like a man tying up a pig,” Bonesy said.

“She said that last time.”

“She says it again.”

She put a gourd bowl in my hands. Beans, black and soft, with something hot in them that made my eyes run. A stack of flat maize cakes off the griddle stone, so hot I had to pass them from hand to hand. Fish from the lake, cooked in leaves. Then she sat down across the fire from me with her own bowl and watched me eat the way my mother used to watch a guest, to see that he did.

I ate all of it. She filled the bowl again.

The children had questions. They asked them in Maya, both at once, and Bonesy put them into English for me.

“She wants to know why your head’s got no hair.”

“Tell her I shave it.”

Bonesy told her. The girl thought about it and asked something else.

“She wants to know why.”

“Lice.”

He told her. She looked at my head with great interest. Then she said something else, and touched her own hair where it hung down her back.

“She says Spaniards have long hair,” Bonesy said. “Down to here.” He laid his finger bones along his collarbone. “She says you’re a bad one.”

“Tell her that’s the idea.”

It was not lice. At the academy every boy wore his hair long to the shoulder, the way gentlemen wore it. The night I left I took my father’s razor to mine. I have kept it off since.

The girl looked at Bonesy’s skull and asked him something. He answered her in English, and she was satisfied with it anyway.

“What did she ask you?”

“If I had lice.”

“What did you say?”

“Nothing for them to hold on to.”

When I had eaten the second bowl I put my hand in my coat. I did it without thinking. My fingers were on the purse before I knew what they were doing.

The wife was looking at me. She looked at my hand inside my coat.

I took it out empty.

I could not leave it, though.

I lay in my hammock that night and thought about the bowl, and the second bowl, and the fish in the leaves. You do not eat a family’s food for nothing. My father taught me that before I could walk. You pay, or you work, or you go hungry. Nobody ever has to say it.

In the morning I went back to their house.

There was a place on the roof at the back, above the hammocks, where the thatch had gone thin. I had seen the light through it the night before. The rain had come through it too, because there was a dark patch on the floor under it.

There was a stack of fresh palm by the next house. I took an armful of it. Nobody stopped me.

The roof was too steep to climb in a sword belt. The swords swung and caught.

I stood at the bottom of the wall and unbuckled it.

I stood there with it in my hands. I looked round for a place to put it. There was a post by the door, with a peg on it for a water gourd.

I hung the belt on the peg.

I left it there and went up the wall.

The thatch was old and dry and full of beetles. I pulled the rotten palm out by the handful and threw it down. I laid the new in its place, overlapping, the way palm is laid on every roof in the islands. I tied it to the rafters with the cord the wife handed up to me without being asked. She did not say anything. She stood at the bottom of the wall and handed up cord.

It took most of the morning. The sun came up hot on my back.

From up there I could see half the island.

I could see Bonesy. He was sitting in the shade outside the old man’s house, with the old people.

The old people came to the old man with the roots, since he could not go to them. He sat on his stool against his wall. The woman with the claw was there, and two old men I did not know, and an old woman with a face like a dried fig who smoked a pipe.

They were complaining. I could tell that from the roof without knowing a word. The old man with the roots would say something and hold up his legs, or what was left of them, and the others would shake their heads. Then the old woman would say something and point at her knees. Then Bonesy would say something and hold up his hand and work the fingers, so that the bones clicked, and point at his elbow.

The others looked at the elbow and nodded. Then the old man with the roots said something else, louder, and held up his legs again.

He was one more old body in the shade with a grievance about its joints.

The woman with the claw had a basket of nuts in her lap. She was cracking them one at a time with the claw, and passing the meat round. She passed one to Bonesy.

He took it and held it.

After a while he put it in his mouth. I watched it fall out through his jaw onto his knee.

He picked it up and gave it to the old man with the roots, and the old man ate it without looking.

I finished the roof.

When I climbed down my sword belt was still on the peg, where I had left it. Nobody had touched it.

I took it down and put it on. My hand went to the hilt by itself.

The wife came out and looked up at the roof. She looked at it a long time. Then she looked at me and said something.

I did not have Bonesy, but I did not need him. She was telling me I had laid it too tight.

There was a lamp burning in Amara’s house that evening, and the door cloth was tied back.

I could see her from the path. She was sitting on the floor with the leaf open in front of her on its oiled cloth, and the lamp beside it, and a stick of charcoal in her fingers. She was not drawing with it. She was holding it.

She looked up and saw me on the path.

I should have kept walking. I did not.

“You sailed that coast,” she said, in English.

“For years.”

“Come here.”

I went in and crouched down on the other side of the leaf.

It was the same leaf we had brought out of the cave, the length of my forearm, painted on bark gone the colour of old bone. She had it turned the way I had turned it that day, with the red edge away from us. Down the middle ran the long blue band of the coast, with its bays and points. Toward one end sat the yellow half moon, the island with the pool, where we had found it. From the half moon a line of tiny black footprints went north along the blue, a step at a time. They stopped beside the long island lying off the shore.

Where they stopped there was the sign. A face, side on, with its mouth open and curls coming out of it.

She put the end of the charcoal beside the face. Up close, by the lamp, I could see the paint was cracked in a fine net like old varnish.

“It’s a place that speaks,” she said. “That much I know. My father taught me the face.”

“Speaks how?”

“That’s what I don’t know.” She ran the charcoal back down the footprints to the half moon, and up them again to the face. “A stone that speaks. A spring. A cave where the sea comes in and goes out and makes a noise. Every one of them is a place that speaks. There are a hundred on that coast.”

“And you don’t know which.”

“I know it’s by the long island. I know it’s where the road ends.” She sat back. “That’s all the leaf says to anybody who just looks at it.”

She looked up at me. The lamp was on the side of her face. She was tired, and there was charcoal on her fingers and a smear of it at the corner of her mouth where she had touched it without knowing, and she was the prettiest woman I had seen in a year.

“You’ve been along that coast,” she said. “Where does a stone talk?”

I thought about it. I thought of every cove and point between the half moon and the long island, every reef and every gap in it I had ever heard breathe.

“Everywhere,” I said, “if you’ve been at sea long enough.”

She did not laugh. She looked at me as if I had wasted her lamp oil.

I thought again.

“There’s a place on the mainland,” I said. “Three or four days south of the long island. The sea comes in under a cliff there and blows up through a hole in the top of it. You hear it a league off. It sounds like a cow being killed.”

“Where?”

I put my finger on the blue band, on the mainland shore, below the long island.

She looked at where my finger was. Then she ran the charcoal along the footprints again, slowly, to the place where they stopped. They stopped beside the long island, out in the blue. They did not touch the shore.

“Not there,” she said.

“Then it’s on the island.”

“I had that much before you came in.”

Then the corner of her mouth went up. She was smiling, and trying not to, and she looked back down at the leaf so I would not see it.

“Go to bed, Spaniard,” she said.

I went.

I looked back from the path. She had picked up the charcoal again. She was still not drawing with it.
