Nobody moved for a long time.

Then the spear came away from Crumble’s neck, and the man holding it stepped out of the trees.

He was not tall. He wore a cloth round his waist and nothing else, and his skin was grey. Not the grey of a sick man. It was the grey of a shark’s back, rough and dull, and it did not shine where the light touched it. Along each side of his neck were three slits that opened and closed when he breathed.

He looked at me. His eyes were black all the way across, with no white in them.

He opened his mouth to speak, and I saw his teeth. There were too many of them, small and pointed and set in rows one behind the other, the way they sit in a shark’s jaw when you cut it out and hang it up to dry.

I had both swords out before he closed it.

I had seen skin like that once before, on a man in Cayona on Tortuga. He hired out to kill, and he was good at it.

Two more men came out of the trees behind him. Their spears came round to me, and the grey man’s with them. Three points, near enough that I could have touched any of them with a blade.

“Put them up,” Crumble said.

He had not moved. His hands were still raised to his shoulders.

“He’s got a spear on you.”

“I know.”

“So put yours down,” Crumble said quietly. “And wait.”

I did not put them down. I did not go forward either. We stood like that, the six of us, with the forest roaring behind us and the sweat running into my eyes.

Bonesy looked from the spears to me.

“This is nice,” he said.

Then somebody called out from the water.

It was a woman’s voice, sharp and carrying. A canoe had come round the point of the shore while we stood there, a small one, low in the water with baskets. The woman in the stern had stopped paddling. She was looking at us.

She called again, in Maya. The two men behind the grey man lowered their spears at once. The grey man took longer. He looked at my swords and then at her.

She said one more word.

He lowered his spear.

She drove the canoe in through the reeds with three hard strokes and stepped out into the shallows. She did not look at the men or the spears. She looked at Crumble.

“Crumble,” she said.

He put his hands down.

Then she looked at me.

“Put those away, Jigsaw,” she said in English. “You still draw first.”

I had seen her once, years ago, hanging over Crumble’s shoulder on a burning deck with the smoke all round her. I would not have known her. She was not a girl any more.

She was pretty. I saw that first, with three spears still within reach of me, and I am not proud of it. She had a long plait down her back and dark eyes, and a smear of mud along one cheekbone where she had wiped her face with a dirty hand. Her hands were black with earth to the wrists.

I put the swords away.

She came up out of the water to Crumble. She did not hurry. She looked at his face first, for a long time. Then she looked down at Balam’s blade on his hip.

She put out her hand and touched the hilt.

Then she took her hand away.

She said something to him in Maya. It was not long.

Crumble answered her. His voice was lower than I had ever heard it. The words came up slowly, one at a time, the way a full bucket comes up a well.

I did not know the words. I knew the sound of them. I had heard Crumble talk in every weather in the years we had been together. I had never heard him speak like that.

She listened to all of it. When he was done she nodded once.

Crumble did not wait any longer than that.

“We found the jar,” he said, in English. “The one he was looking for.”

Her eyes went to the jar under his arm.

“There’s a leaf in it,” he said. “A map. There’s a sign at the end of it I can’t read.”

She looked at the jar a long while. She did not reach for it.

“Who followed you?” she said.

“Nobody.”

“Somebody always follows.”

“A Spanish ship picked us up off the coast four days ago,” Crumble said. “We lost her inside the reef. It might have been Cárdenas.”

The grey man turned his head at the name. So did the other two.

Amara did not turn hers. She was still looking at the jar.

“I need to talk to you,” Crumble said. “Alone.”

She looked at him. Then she looked at me, and at Bonesy.

“Come to the house,” she said.

There were two canoes pulled up in the reeds below the trees, each cut from a single log. Amara took Crumble and the jar in her own. I went in one of the others with Bonesy and the grey man and the two spearmen. One of the spearmen moved over to make room for Bonesy, and looked at him no longer than he looked at me. Less, if anything.

The lake was flat and brown and still. The paddles went in without a sound.

The spearmen talked to each other in low voices across Bonesy’s head. Every so often one of them looked at me.

Bonesy listened to them.

“They think you’re rude,” he said.

“You understand them?”

“Mostly.”

“Since when?”

He thought about it.

“Since always.”

“Why am I rude?”

“The swords.”

I kept my hand on my hilt the rest of the way across.

The island was low and thick with trees. Dogs ran down to the water to bark at us. Behind the dogs came children, and behind the children came everyone else.

I have been stared at before. I have walked into towns where half the men in the street knew my face from a reward. This was different. Nobody here knew my face. They were looking at what I was, a stranger with a Spaniard’s face and a sword, and they had good reason to dislike it.

I looked back at them.

Bonesy climbed out of the canoe beside me.

The children stopped.

Every one of them looked at him. He stood on the shore with lake water running out between his ribs and the ruined hat on his skull, and he looked back at them.

Then the smallest came forward, a boy no higher than my belt. He came right up to Bonesy and tipped his head back to look at his face.

He reached out and knocked on Bonesy’s shin with his knuckles.

Bonesy looked down at him.

“Hello,” he said, and grinned at him.

The boy ran off shrieking. Two others ran in to knock on him in his place.

Amara went up from the shore without waiting. Crumble went after her with the jar, and Bonesy and I followed.

A path of crushed shell ran up from the landing between the houses. They were built of poles and mud, each with a roof of palm thatch that came down nearly to the ground. Smoke leaked out through the thatch, so that every roof on the island smoked like a damp fire.

Behind the houses, on the higher ground, there were gardens. The maize stood taller than a man, with beans climbing the stalks and squash vines sprawled in the earth between them. Hollow logs hung under the eaves of some of the houses, stopped at both ends with clay, and small black bees went in and out of a hole in the middle. Fish lay split and drying on racks of sticks over slow fires. Two women knelt at flat stones outside a doorway, grinding maize with a stone roller. They did not stop when we passed. They only lifted their eyes.

A turkey walked across the path in front of me as though it owned it.

It was a village like a hundred I have seen from the sea on that coast. That was what I thought for the first twenty paces.

An old man sat in front of the next house, on a stool with his back against the wall. He did not get up when we passed. He could not. Below the knee his legs were not legs any more. They were roots, grey and brown and twisted together like the roots of the mangroves at low water, and they went down into the mud under the house and did not come out.

A woman walked past him with a water jar on her shoulder. Her right arm was a claw.

It was the claw of a land crab, red and black, and as big as a man’s thigh. She carried it bent against her body with the joint resting on her hip, the way you would carry a heavy basket. Her shoulder sagged on that side.

A girl stood in a doorway with her hand on the post. The skin of her arms was brown and ridged and split like bark. Thin green vines grew out of her shoulders and hung down her back.

I stopped on the path.

I had seen people changed before. Not many. There was the man in Cayona with the shark’s hide, and a Frenchman on the beach at Tortuga whose hands had gone hard and black as turtle shell, who broke a man’s neck for a bet. There was a woman in Petit-Goâve who could hear a whisper through a wall, and sold what she heard. Every one I had met had used what was done to them to kill or to get rich, and most of them both.

So I watched their hands, the way I would on Tortuga.

The old man had a bowl of beans in his. The woman with the claw had her water jar. The girl had nothing in hers but the doorpost.

Crumble walked past them all as though they were anybody.

An old woman came out of the crowd and took his face in both her hands. She said something and patted his cheek, hard, the way you would pat a horse. A man clapped him on the shoulder. Two of the children called his name.

He had been here before. He had lived here, or near enough. In all the years I had known him he had never said so.

I stood on the path with my hand on my hilt while they welcomed him.

Amara’s house was at the far end of the island, the last before the trees, with a garden behind it and a canoe pulled up on the bank below. It was no bigger than the others. Bundles of dried leaves and roots hung from the rafters inside the door, and there were pots stacked along the wall, more pots than one woman needs.

She held the door cloth aside. Crumble ducked in with the jar.

She looked at me once. Then she let the cloth fall.

I stood outside. I could hear their voices through the wall, low, in Maya. Hers and then his, and then a long time of nobody speaking at all.

Bonesy sat down on the step with his back against the doorpost.

“What are they saying?” I said.

He did not look up.

“Not ours.”

I went down and sat by the canoe.

When Crumble came out the sun was down behind the trees. He still had the jar under his arm. He did not say what they had talked about, and I did not ask.

There was an open place in the middle of the island with a great tree in it. That night a fire burned beneath the tree, and among the roots somebody had set a clay bowl with a lump of resin smoking in it. The smoke was white and smelled sweet and sharp, like pine pitch on a hot deck.

An old man was sitting by the fire.

He was older than the man with the roots, but there was nothing wrong with him that I could see. He sat with his back straight and his hands on his knees. His forearms were crossed with old white scars, the kind a man gets from blocking a blade with whatever he has.

Crumble stopped in front of him. He took the jar from under his arm and set it down. Then he bowed his head.

The old man stood up. He took Crumble by the forearm and held it. His eyes went to Balam’s blade.

He said Balam’s name.

Crumble said something back, and the old man nodded slowly, and let go.

“Ah Pech,” Crumble told me afterwards. “He fought beside Balam when they were young.”

They brought us food. Fish wrapped in leaves and cooked in the ashes, and flat maize cakes, and a gourd of something sour. I had not eaten a hot meal since the cave. I ate with my back to the tree, where I could see the whole of the open ground.

When we had eaten Crumble cut the cord from the jar.

He took the packet out and unwrapped it on a mat by the fire, one fold at a time, and laid the leaf open in the light.

People came to look. They stood round the mat and did not touch it.

Amara knelt beside Crumble. She looked along the footprints, from the half moon to the long island, and stopped at the sign. The face in profile, with curls coming out of its mouth.

She looked at it a long time.

Ah Pech leaned in over her shoulder. He looked at it longer.

Amara opened her mouth to speak.

A woman called her from the edge of the firelight. I could not understand the words, but I understood the voice. Somebody was hurt or sick and it would not wait.

Amara got up at once.

“Not tonight,” she said to Crumble. She folded the cloth back over the leaf with quick hands and gave it to him, and she was gone into the dark between the houses.

Crumble put the leaf back in the jar.

Ah Pech sat down again by the fire. He sat looking at the place on the mat where the leaf had been.

They gave us a house at the edge of the island, close to the water, with three hammocks slung from the poles. I lay in mine that first night with my swords beside me and listened.

The forest was loud on the far shore. Closer, under the noise of it, a man was crying out in one of the houses. Not screaming. It was the noise a man makes through his teeth when he will not scream. It went on a long time and then it stopped.

In the morning I found out who it was.

He was sitting on a log outside his house with his back bare. His shoulder and the back of his neck were crusted over with coral, white and pink and hard, the way coral grows on the timbers of a wreck. His wife knelt behind him with a knife. She was working the point in under the edge of the crust and levering it away a piece at a time.

It came off with skin on it. Where it came away the shoulder bled.

He had a stick between his teeth. He did not make a sound.

I stood and watched. I did not mean to. I could not stop.

She got the last piece off and threw it in the dirt with the others. Then she wiped the blood with a rag that had been washed so often it was grey. The bleeding did not stop.

I went back to our house and came out again with the linen and the vinegar I had brought up from the surgeon’s chest.

I knelt down beside her and held them out.

She looked at the linen. Then she looked at me.

I poured the vinegar on the shoulder. The man bit the stick so hard I heard it crack. I bound the shoulder up the way I had bound Crumble’s hands, round and over and tight, and tied it off.

The wife watched me do all of it.

When I was done she said something to her husband. He laughed round the stick.

Later I asked Bonesy what she had said.

“You bind too tight.”

“That’s what Crumble said.”

“She’s right.”

It took me two days to understand that nobody on the island gave orders.

There was no headman that I could find, no council, nobody who sat in the big house and sent for people. There was not even a big house. When a canoe needed caulking, the men who used it caulked it. When the old man with the roots needed his food, his daughter brought it.

And when anybody needed anything else, they went to Amara.

She was everywhere. I would see her at first light at the water with her baskets, and at noon up to her waist in the lake mending somebody’s fish trap, and at dusk in a doorway with a sick child on her hip. Women stopped her on the path to show her a cut or a rash. Old men stopped her to complain. She listened to all of them as if they were the first.

I asked her once why so many of them were here, the ones who had been changed.

She was carrying water at the time. She did not put it down.

“Nobody hunts them here,” she said.

Then she went on up the path.

The old man with the roots was at me from the first morning.

Every time I went past his house he shouted. He had a high cracked voice that carried right across the island, and he pointed at me while he shouted, and his grandchildren sat on his roots and watched me to see what I would do.

On the second day Bonesy was with me.

“What’s he saying?”

Bonesy listened.

“He wants to know why the Spaniard walks about with his hand on his sword.”

“Tell him I’m not Spanish.”

Bonesy said it to him in English, loud and slow, and pointed at me.

The old man shouted something back.

“He says then stop standing like one,” Bonesy said.

His daughter brought him his food twice a day and put it into his hands. He argued with her too. He argued with the grandchildren and the dogs and the man next door. In the evenings he told stories, long ones, and the children sat on the roots to hear them and he slapped their hands when they picked at the bark.

The grey fisherman I saw on the second afternoon.

He was down at the water’s edge on a flat stone, gutting fish. A boy of about fifteen sat beside him with a knife of his own. The boy’s skin was plain brown. He had his father’s shoulders and nothing else of him.

The boy cut too deep and spoiled the fish. His father took the knife out of his hand and showed him again, slowly, the point going in under the gills and running down to the vent. Then he gave the knife back.

The boy did it again. It was better.

The father talked the whole time, low and steady, and the boy kept his head down and did not answer.

Amara came down the bank behind me with a basket on her hip. She stopped and listened.

“There’s a girl on the other side of the island,” she said. “The boy wants to marry her.”

“And?”

“His father thinks she’s too clever for him.”

“Is she?”

“Yes.”

She said it without smiling. Then the corner of her mouth went, and she went on along the shore with her basket.

I watched her go farther than I needed to.

When I looked back the father had his hand on the back of the boy’s neck and was shaking him gently, the way you shake a dog you are fond of.

I was watching his hands by then, and not his teeth.

There was a house near the tree that nobody went into except Amara and one woman.

I saw inside it on the third day. A man lay in a hammock with his eyes shut and his hands folded on his chest. He was thinner than the fever had made my father, so thin the bones of his face stood out under the skin. Every so often his eyes moved quick under the lids, side to side, the way a dog’s do when it dreams of running.

The woman sat beside him. She had a wooden bowl in her lap with raw meat in it, cut in pieces.

“He’s been asleep two months,” Amara said behind me.

I had not heard her come up.

“Asleep.”

“His way is out walking.”

“His what?”

“His way.” She said it as if it were a plain word. “Everyone has one. An animal that carries half of your soul. Most people never meet theirs. It stays in the forest and you stay in your house, and you live your lives apart.”

“And his?”

“His came out of the forest. It’s a jaguar. While it walks, he sleeps.” She looked at the man in the hammock. “It hasn’t stopped walking.”

“What’s the meat for?”

“She takes it to the edge of the trees every evening. In the morning it’s gone.”

“Can’t you wake him?”

“We’ve tried.”

“Is there nothing anybody can give him?”

She looked at me.

“No.”

She went on to the next house.

I put my hand inside my coat. The coins were there in the purse where I had put them before we left the sloop. I held them for a while.

Then I took my hand out again.

That night I went down to the water and looked across at the far shore. There was something at the edge of the trees. I could not see what it was. I saw its eyes take the light from our fire, two green sparks low to the ground.

They stayed there a long time. Then they were gone.

Bonesy had a better time of it than any of us.

He had not been on the island half a day before the children owned him. They sat on his shoulders and climbed his ribs like rigging. They put flowers through the holes in his skull. They took his hat in turns and argued over the crease in it, and in the end the girl with the bark skin pressed it out over her knee while he sat beside her and waited.

The crease came back the next morning. She pressed it out again.

At night he sat by the fire with whoever was there. They talked to him in Maya and he answered them in English. Nobody minded which was which.

Crumble I hardly saw.

He was with Ah Pech most of the day, or with the old women, or down at the canoes with the men. When I did see him he was talking Maya. He talked it well, better than I had known. But once I saw an old woman stop him and say a word back to him, the way you correct a child, and he said it again her way and did not smile.

He was not easy here either. I could see that much. He ate with his back to a post, the same as I did. Twice I woke in the night and his hammock was empty, and both times I found him down by the canoes, looking across the lake toward the river.

On the third evening I saw him at the water with Amara.

They were standing a little apart, looking out at the lake. She said something, and he laughed.

It was not the laugh he gives Bonesy when Bonesy is being foul, or the one he gave me inside the reef. I had not heard it before. It was short and surprised, as if it had got out before he could stop it.

She looked up then, past him, to where I was standing.

I looked away first.

That evening the old man with the roots shouted at me again.

I stopped. I was tired of walking past.

He shouted again and pointed at the ground beside his stool.

I looked for Bonesy. Bonesy was not there.

The old man pointed at the ground again.

I sat down.

The children were already on his roots. One of them, a boy of about five, looked at me for a long time and then came and sat against my leg instead. The old man said something sharp to him, and he ignored him.

Then he started his story.

I did not understand one word of it. It went on a long time. His voice went up high and came down low. He made noises like a bird and like a jaguar, and once like a man falling out of a tree. The children laughed at that every time. He did it three times.

The boy against my leg went heavy. When I looked down he was asleep.

He slid sideways.

I took my hand off my hilt to hold him up.

The old man kept on with his story. The fire burned down. The boy slept with his face against my knee and his mouth open.

I sat there until it was finished.

His mother came for him when the fire was down to coals. She lifted him off me without waking him and carried him away into the dark.

I stood up. My leg had gone dead where he had lain on it.

By the time I reached our house my hand was back on my hilt.
