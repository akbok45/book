Bonesy carried the jar out.

He did not need to swim, so he did not try. He held it against his ribs with both arms and walked down the steps into the water. He walked along the bottom of the passage and through the wall and up the slope of the pool, with the pull dragging at the water all around him and the tarpon turning out of his way. Crumble and I went through the wall after him on the line, fighting the water the whole way. When I came up into the daylight the pull had me by the legs, and Crumble had to reach down from the rock and haul me out by the wrist.

Bonesy was sitting on the rock with the jar in his lap.

“Took your time,” he said.

We were away from the island by noon.

I took her north, up along the coast, the way the footprints went. The jar was lashed in the stern locker under a piece of sailcloth. Crumble checked it twice before dark and once more in the night, when he thought I was asleep.

I went below and opened the strongbox.

Vaz had paid us well, but we had spent a good part of it at Petit-Goâve, and more getting out. I counted what remained, sorted the coins into little stacks, and counted them again.

There was no more the second time.

I put them back and shut the lid. An hour later I opened it and counted them a third time.

Crumble caught me at it. He looked at the coins, then at me, and went back to checking the jar.

Neither of us said anything.

On the second morning there was a sail astern.

It was only a fleck when I saw it, a white chip on the edge of the sea to the south. By the middle of the morning it was a ship. By noon I could see she had two masts and every sail on both of them set and drawing.

Crumble came aft and stood beside me with the glass.

“Spanish,” he said.

“I know.”

“His?”

“Could be. Or a guardacosta out of Campeche, looking for Englishmen trading where they shouldn’t.” I watched her come on. “Either way, she’ll want to stop us.”

“She’s not coming alongside to ask after our health.”

“No.”

She was faster than us in that wind. Not by much, but enough. By the middle of the afternoon she had closed to two leagues, and I could see the white water under her bow.

Bonesy came up from below and looked at her over the rail.

“That one’s keen,” he said.

“She is.”

“What do we do?”

“We go to Port Royal.”

Crumble turned his head toward me.

“We’re not going to Port Royal,” I said. “But she’s going to think we are.”

He looked back at the Spanish ship.

Then he smiled.

It was the first proper smile I had seen from him since we came out of the cave. I felt a rush of excitement rise from my chest, the same feeling I used to get when the two of us were about to attempt something particularly foolish.

I knew that smile. I had seen it across the decks of prizes, in taverns where we were badly outnumbered, and on a hundred nights when the odds were against us.

“How long?” he asked.

“Until she’s certain.”

He nodded and went to the sheets.

I put the helm over and brought the sloop round to the east, away from the coast and out into open water. I set her on a course that any fool with a glass could follow, straight as a rope, toward Port Royal and the English.

The Spanish ship came round after us.

All afternoon we ran east, and all afternoon she followed. She did not gain on us much after that. I eased the sheets now and then to keep her hopeful. I wanted her captain to be sure. I wanted him to see where we were going so plainly that he would stop thinking about it.

The sun went down behind her. The sea went dark, then black. Her lantern came on, a small yellow star astern.

I kept our course.

Crumble came aft once to look at the light. He measured its distance, looked at the sky, and went forward again without asking.

By midnight it had grown so faint that I could find it only when the sloop lifted on a swell. Clouds had covered the stars, and there was no moon. I could no longer make out the ship herself.

Crumble came aft and stood beside me.

“Far enough?” he asked.

I looked back at the lantern.

“Far enough.”

He reached up and put out our stern light.

I turned toward him. In the darkness I could just make out his face, and he was grinning again.

I grinned back.

We had played that trick a hundred times. For a moment everything that had followed us out of the cave was gone: the missing gold, the village that might turn us away, and Cárdenas’s ships somewhere behind us.

We knew what we were doing. More importantly, we knew what the other would do.

I put out the lamp in the cabin.

We waited in darkness.

Then I put the helm over and brought her round to the west.

Crumble eased one sheet and hauled another. The boom came across, the sail filled, and the sloop leaned into the wind.

We ran back the way we had come.

The Spanish lantern passed slowly off our port side, far away. I watched it until it was a pinprick and then until it was nothing.

“She’ll know by morning,” Crumble said.

“She’ll know by morning that we’re not ahead of her. She won’t know where we went.”

“The coast is that way.” He pointed into the dark. “So’s the reef.”

“I know where the reef is.”

He looked at me.

“Of course you do.”

I could hear him smiling.

I had sailed that coast enough to know it by its sounds. The reef ran along it a league or two out, like a wall with a few gates in it. Inside was a long stretch of sheltered water, shallow and full of coral heads that would tear the bottom out of a ship.

No Spanish captain, nor any other captain in these seas, would dare follow Crumble and me through those reefs at night and live to tell the tale.

The wind carried us west until I heard the breakers.

At first it was only a low sound beneath the water against the hull. Then it grew into a steady roar, stretching across the darkness ahead of us. The reef was still out of sight, but I could hear it breaking for miles in either direction.

I took some way off the sloop.

Crumble came aft.

“Can you find it?”

“I think so.”

He looked toward the sound.

“That’s comforting.”

We crept closer.

The reef showed itself in pieces. A long white line appeared ahead of us, vanished behind a swell, and appeared again. Farther north another line of surf broke against the dark. Between them the water was black.

I knew there was an opening somewhere along that stretch, a break in the coral where the sea ran deep enough to carry a boat through. I had passed it in daylight years before.

Finding it now was another matter.

I brought us north along the outer edge, keeping enough water between us and the breakers to turn away if I had to. The roar came from our starboard side, close enough that spray drifted across the deck.

Then I heard a gap in it.

Not silence. There was still water moving over stone, but the hard crash of the breakers stopped for a short distance before beginning again.

I turned toward it.

Ahead, two banks of white water rose and fell on either side of a dark opening. The swell ran between them, lifting in a long smooth hump before breaking farther inside.

There was our gate.

“Crumble.”

“I see it.”

He went to the leeboard and hauled it up clear of the water. We would have less hold against the wind, but a little more room beneath us.

“Bonesy. Bow.”

He went forward and lay on his front along the bowsprit, with his skull out over the water.

Bonesy can see in the dark. None of us knows how. He has no eyes to see with, but put him in a black hold and he will tell you where every rat is. He lay on the bowsprit that night and looked down into the water.

“Deep in the middle,” he called.

“Can you see the bottom?”

“Not yet.”

I held our course.

The white water rose on either side of us. For a moment it seemed that the two banks were closing together, and I could see the broken coral beneath the foam.

Then a swell lifted the stern.

I kept the tiller steady and let it carry us forward.

The sloop ran between the breakers with spray coming over the rail. I heard the roar close on both sides, felt the hull lift once more, and then the water beneath us settled.

We were through.

Crumble threw back his head and gave a great roar of triumph.

I felt it too. My heart was hammering, my hands were shaking on the tiller, and I was grinning so hard my face hurt. For a moment I wanted to shout with him.

Then Bonesy called from the bowsprit.

“There’s more.”

Beyond the reef the water spread out into a shallow lagoon, black under the night sky. Coral heads rose from the bottom everywhere, some broad as tables, others no bigger than a man’s skull. In daylight the water would have been clear enough to see them. Now they showed themselves only when a swell drew back and left a pale shape just beneath the surface.

I laughed and tightened my grip on the tiller.

“Of course there is.”

“Keep looking, Bonesy.”

“I am.”

Crumble took up the lead and went forward. He sounded the bottom while Bonesy watched ahead, calling the depth back to me in a low voice.

“Four fathoms.”

“Left a bit,” Bonesy said.

I moved the tiller.

“Three fathoms.”

“Bit more.”

I moved it more.

“There’s one coming. Big. Right side. Go left.”

I went left.

Something pale slid past under the hull, so close that I heard it scrape along the keel.

Crumble laughed.

“That was close.”

“Not close enough,” I said.

“Want another?”

“Not particularly.”

Bonesy looked back at us.

“There’s another.”

“Where?”

“Right in front.”

Crumble hauled on the lead.

“Two fathoms!”

I brought the tiller across.

The sloop turned so sharply that the boom swung and the sail snapped. Crumble caught the sheet before it could run, hauled it tight, and shifted his weight against the heel.

A great pale head of coral passed down our starboard side, close enough that I could have reached over and touched it.

“Missed that one,” Bonesy said.

“Very observant,” Crumble called.

Bonesy looked down at it as we passed.

“Ugly thing.”

We went on.

For the next hour there was hardly a moment when one of us was not speaking. Bonesy called the coral. Crumble sounded the bottom, tended the sheets, and watched the leeboard. I steered between the pale shapes as they appeared, sometimes with scarcely a boat’s width to spare.

There was no need to explain ourselves. Crumble knew when I wanted the sail eased before I called for it. I knew when he was about to shift his weight by the movement of the deck beneath me. Bonesy had learned the difference between a turn we could make and one we could not, and he called the hazards early enough for me to choose.

Once Crumble looked back at me from the bow, the lead line dripping through his hands.

He was smiling.

I smiled back.

I had spent the last two days thinking about the gold we had not found, the Spanish ships hunting us, and the village that might refuse to take us in. Somewhere between the reef and the coral heads, all of it had fallen away.

There was only the next turn, the water beneath us, and the two men who knew exactly what I needed from them.

All night we went like that, creeping north inside the reef with the sails half down. Twice I felt the keel touch sand. Once we ran so close to the breakers that the backwash rocked us from side to side.

Nobody complained.

When the sky went grey in the east, I looked astern.

There was nothing behind us but sea.

I climbed up on the rail and looked again, all round, as far as I could see. No sail. No smoke. Nothing but the reef and the sea and the green coast going away to the north.

I came down and sat by the tiller.

My hands had been on it all night, and I could not open them straight.

Crumble came aft and sat down across from me. He looked at the empty sea for a long time.

“Well,” he said.

“Well.”

“That was a good night’s work.”

I smiled, though I was too tired to answer.

For a while we sat there looking at the water. The sun was coming up behind the clouds, and the reef we had passed through lay white and harmless in the morning light.

The excitement was going out of me. I could feel it leaving in little stages, until my shoulders ached and my eyes burned and the tiller seemed too heavy to hold.

Crumble leaned back against the cabin.

“I’d forgotten how much I enjoy that,” he said.

“So had I.”

He looked at me.

“Nearly got us killed.”

“Nearly.”

He shook his head, smiling.

Then he looked down at the stern locker where the jar was lashed.

“The river’s two days north of here,” he said.

I waited.

“She might not see us.” He rubbed his jaw. “Her people might not let us in. They don’t much care for strangers.”

“I know.”

“And if Cárdenas comes after us there.”

“He won’t. He’s on his way to Port Royal.”

The corner of Crumble’s mouth moved.

“Unless he turns round.”

“Then we’ll lose him again.”

He looked at me for a moment.

“All right,” he said. “We go.”

He got up and went forward without another word.

A little while later I saw him at the bow with a bucket, washing out his red sash in seawater. He wrung it and washed it again, though it was no dirtier than it had been for a month.

Bonesy saw it too. He looked at me over the cabin top.

I shook my head at him.

For once in his life he kept quiet.

It was Crumble’s hands I noticed at noon.

He had swung the lead all night, wet line running through his palms, and the skin across both of them had come away in strips. He was hauling on the main halyard with them as if nothing were wrong.

“Christ, Crumble. Look at your hands.”

He looked down at them.

“They’ve been worse.”

“Not by much.”

I went below and brought up the surgeon’s chest we had taken off the wreck.

He sat on the cabin top while I cleaned the cuts and bound his palms in linen. He complained about the vinegar, complained about the wrappings, and complained that I was taking too long.

“You’re welcome,” I said.

“I haven’t thanked you.”

“I noticed.”

He laughed.

When I had finished, he tested his grip on a length of rope.

“That’ll do,” he said.

“Next time let Bonesy swing the lead.”

Bonesy looked up from his hat.

“Happy to.”

Crumble looked at his bare fingers.

“On second thought, perhaps not.”

I shut the chest.

Crumble had hauled me out of the pool by one wrist, with the tide trying to pull me under. He had never stopped to ask whether I was worth the trouble.

A few strips of linen were the least I could do.

That night it was my turn to rest.

Crumble took the tiller, his hands wrapped in clean linen. Bonesy sat on the cabin top with his hat in his lap, trying to punch the crease out of the crown with his fist. It went out and came back. He punched it again.

Before turning in, I went below and opened the strongbox.

I counted the coins once. There was no more than before, though I had half hoped there might be. I put them back and shut the lid. Then I came back up and lay down by the tiller with my coat beneath my head.

I could hear Crumble moving about on deck and Bonesy still working at his hat. The sloop rocked gently beneath me.

I closed my eyes.

“It isn’t enough,” the apothecary said.

He said it gently. He had counted the coins twice, pushing each one across the counter with one stained finger, and now he looked at me over the top of his spectacles.

The shop smelled of bitter medicine, and of the fish and tar from the street outside. Behind him the shelves went up to the ceiling, crowded with jars. One of them was brown glass with a paper label. I had watched him take it down when I came in and put it back when I told him what I had.

“How much more?” I said.

He told me.

It was more than twice what lay on the counter.

I had counted it all the way down the hill. The coins I had saved since I was small. What was left in the box under my father’s bed. What the bookseller had given me for my books, which was not much.

“I can bring the rest,” I said. “In a week.”

“Who is it for?”

“My father.”

He looked at my coat. It was the academy coat, the one that had been let out twice at the shoulders, with the cuffs going at the edges.

“I know that coat,” he said.

“I don’t go there any more.”

He looked at me again, then at the money on the counter.

“I would have thought a family able to send a son to the academy could afford a little bark.”

I felt my face grow hot.

“We could, once.”

He said nothing.

“There was a man came in a month ago,” he said after a while. “A big fellow. Paid me with a sword.”

“My father.”

“How is his wife?”

“It ran out on the fourth day,” I said. “She died on the ninth.”

He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes and put them back on.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

I looked at the brown jar.

“I can work for it. I can sweep the shop. I can carry for you. Whatever you want.”

“Son.”

He did not say it unkindly. That was the worst of it. He looked at me as if he were truly sorry, and I believe now that he was. But he did not turn round to the shelf.

“It comes from Peru,” he said. “I have to pay for it myself.”

“I’ll pay you back.”

“I can’t give it to you.”

I looked past him at the brown jar.

He followed my eyes.

For a moment neither of us spoke.

I could have gone over the counter and had it before he turned round. I thought about it. I thought about it long enough that he saw me thinking it, and he did not move, and he did not call for anyone.

He only waited.

I picked up my coins and went out.

I walked home the long way, up the hill, past the big houses. Their windows were lit. Somebody in one of them was playing a guitar, badly, and people were laughing.

I stopped outside one of the houses.

Through the open shutters I could see a table laid for supper, with candles burning at either end. A servant was carrying in a dish, and a man in a fine coat sat with his back to the window, talking to someone I could not see.

I stood there with the coins in my fist.

The man laughed at something. A woman reached across the table and took his hand.

I looked at the house for a long time.

Then I went home.

The two hooks on the wall above the door were empty.

The sword had hung there my whole life. I had stopped seeing it years ago, and now I could not stop seeing the place where it had been.

My father was awake.

He was in the quiet part of the fever, lying still, with his face grey on the pillow and his eyes open. He watched me come in and put the coins down on the table.

“Well?” he said.

“He had none left,” I said. “There’s a ship due. Next week.”

My father looked at me for a long time.

He knew.

I could see that he knew. He had always been able to tell when I was lying, ever since I was small, and I had never been able to work out how.

He did not say anything about it.

“Sit with me,” he said.

I pulled the stool over to the bed and sat down.

After a while he put out his hand, and I took it. It was hot and dry, and much thinner than I remembered.

He closed his eyes.

I sat there in the dark and listened to him breathe. In and out, slow, the way the sea comes in on a beach at night.

I listened to it for a long time.

I listened to it until I could not tell it from the water.

It was the water.

It was going past the hull a foot from my ear, and the deck was hard under my shoulder, and the sky over the mast had begun to go grey.

Crumble was still at the tiller. His bound hands were resting on it.

He was looking at me.

I sat up.

He glanced at my face, then back at the sea.

“Bad one?” he said.

“Old one.”

He nodded and let it alone.

Bonesy was sitting on the cabin top where I had left him, with his hat on his knees. The crease was still in it. He did not say anything either.

I sat for a while listening to the water.

Then I got up and took the tiller from Crumble.

We came to the river two days later in the afternoon.

You could see it long before you reached it. The sea changed colour from blue to green to a thick dull brown. There were branches floating in it, and whole trees with their roots still on them. The coast was low there and covered in mangroves, and the river came out of them in a wide brown mouth with a sandbar across it.

We waited for the flood and went over the bar on it.

Then we were in the river, and the sea was behind us.

I did not like it.

I knew it would be bad. Crumble had told me, and I had been up a river or two in my time, chasing water or running from somebody. But I had never been this far up one, or for this long.

The trees came right down to the water on both sides. They leaned out over it, so that by the second day there was only a strip of sky above us, narrow and white and far away.

Below it the river was brown and slow and smooth, and you could not see a hand’s depth into it.

Shapes moved under the surface sometimes. A log on the bank opened its eyes and slid into the water as we passed.

There was no wind.

We had to take the sails down and row. The air was hot and wet and thick and smelled of mud and rotting leaves, and it did not move at all. Mosquitoes came off the water in clouds.

And there was no horizon.

I had not known how much I needed one until it was gone.

At sea I can always see where I am. I can look out and see the weather coming two days off, and see a ship before she sees me, and know where the land is by the colour of the sky above it.

On that river I could see nothing past the next bend.

Every bend looked like the last one. The trees closed in behind us as we went, and after a while I could not have told you which way the sea was.

The noise was worse.

At sea the noise is wind and water, and it comes from far away. Here everything was close. Birds screamed in the trees right over our heads. Animals we never saw crashed through the branches.

On the first evening something roared in the forest, a deep rising roar like a man being killed slowly. It went on for half an hour.

I had my hand on my hilt before I knew I had moved.

“Howler monkeys,” Crumble said, without looking round.

“Monkeys.”

“Little black ones. About so big.” He held his hands apart the length of a cat. “They do that every evening.”

I took my hand off my hilt.

A little while later I noticed it was back.

Crumble was at home there in a way I had never seen him.

He sat in the bow and watched the banks, and he knew the river. He knew which side of each bend the deep water was on. He knew where the sandbanks were before we struck them. When the river split, he pointed without hesitating. He smelled the air sometimes, as if it told him something.

I rowed and watched his back.

Every few minutes he would look round at me. Not for long. Just a glance over his shoulder, to see that I was there.

Each time he did it I felt my shoulders come down a little, though I had not known they were up.

Bonesy loved it.

He hung over the side and talked to the logs.

Once, when a great dark shape moved beneath the boat, he leaned so far over that his hat fell off.

He went after it.

There was a splash, and then nothing.

Crumble looked back at me.

I kept rowing.

A little while later Bonesy’s skull appeared above the water beside us, the hat held in his teeth.

He climbed aboard and shook himself like a dog.

“Got it,” he said.

“I can see that,” I told him.

He took the hat from his mouth, looked at the water dripping off the brim, and put it back on.

The crease was still there.

On the third day the river grew too narrow and too shallow for the sloop.

Crumble found a creek running back into the mangroves on the left bank, and we poled her into it as far as she would go and made her fast to the roots.

We cut branches and laid them over her deck until, from the river, she looked like another heap of drift.

Then we took what we could carry, and the jar, and went on foot.

Crumble led.

There was a path, though I would not have found it on my own. It went up away from the river through the trees, and it was so narrow that the leaves brushed both my shoulders.

It was darker under the trees than it had been on the river.

I walked with one hand on my hilt and the other pushing branches out of my face, and I kept looking up for a sky I could not see.

Late in the afternoon the trees thinned, and I saw water through them.

It was a lake.

Not a big one. The forest came down to the water all the way round it, dark green and still. The lake lay in the middle like a piece of the sky that had fallen down.

Out in the middle of it there was an island, low and green.

Above the island rose thin blue smoke from cooking fires.

I let out a breath I had been holding for three days.

Crumble had stopped at the edge of the trees.

He was standing stock still.

“Is that it?” I said.

He did not answer.

Bonesy came up beside us and looked out at the island.

“Smoke,” he said. “Somebody’s cooking.”

Then a voice spoke out of the trees behind us.

It said something short and hard in Maya. I did not understand the words, but I understood the tone. I had heard it in a dozen languages.

It meant don’t move.

I did not move.

Out of the corner of my eye I could see the tip of a spear, no more than a foot from Crumble’s neck.

Crumble did not turn round.

He raised his empty hands slowly to his shoulders.

Then he answered, in the same language, quiet and careful.

He said Balam’s name.

The spear did not move.

But nobody used it.
