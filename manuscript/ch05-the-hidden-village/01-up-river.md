Bonesy carried the jar out.

He did not need to swim, so he did not try. He held it against his ribs with both arms and walked down the steps into the water. He walked along the bottom of the passage and through the wall and up the slope of the pool, with the pull dragging at the water all around him and the tarpon turning out of his way. Crumble and I went through the wall after him on the line, fighting the water the whole way. When I came up into the daylight the pull had me by the legs, and Crumble had to reach down from the rock and haul me out by the wrist.

Bonesy was sitting on the rock with the jar in his lap.

“Took your time,” he said.

We were away from the island by noon.

I took her north, up along the coast, the way the footprints went. The jar was lashed in the stern locker under a piece of sailcloth. Crumble checked it twice before dark and once more in the night, when he thought I was asleep.

On the second morning there was a sail astern.

It was only a fleck when I saw it, a white chip on the edge of the sea to the south. By the middle of the morning it was a ship. By noon I could see she had two masts and every sail on both of them set and drawing.

Crumble came aft and stood beside me with the glass.

“Spanish,” he said.

“I know.”

“His?”

“His, or one of the Campeche guard ships looking for anybody at all.” I watched her come on. “Doesn’t matter which. She’s seen us.”

She was faster than us in that wind. Not by much, but enough. By the middle of the afternoon she had closed to two leagues, and I could see the white water under her bow.

Bonesy came up from below and looked at her over the rail.

“That one’s keen,” he said.

“She is.”

“What do we do?”

“We go to Jamaica.”

Crumble looked at me.

“We’re not going to Jamaica,” I said. “But she’s going to think we are.”

I put the helm over and brought the sloop round to the east, away from the coast and out into open water. I set her on a course that any fool with a glass could follow, straight as a rope, toward Jamaica and the English and the only safe harbor a buccaneer has left on this side of the sea.

The Spanish ship came round after us.

All afternoon we ran east, and all afternoon she followed. She did not gain on us much after that. I let out a little sheet now and then to keep her hopeful. I wanted her captain to be sure. I wanted him to see where we were going so plainly that he would stop thinking about it.

The sun went down behind her. The sea went dark, then black. Her lantern came on at her stern, a small yellow star two leagues back.

“Now,” I said.

Crumble put out our stern light. I put out the lamp in the cabin. We sat in the dark with no light on the sloop at all.

I waited another hour, until there was no moon and the clouds had come over the stars. Then I put the helm hard over and brought her round to the west.

We ran back the way we had come, in the dark, with the wind on the other side of us. I could not see the Spanish ship any more, only her lantern, going east and away from us. I watched it until it was a pin-prick and then until it was nothing.

“She’ll know by morning,” Crumble said.

“She’ll know by morning that we’re not ahead of her. She won’t know where we went.”

“The coast is that way.” He pointed into the dark. “So’s the reef.”

“I know where the reef is.”

He said nothing.

I had sailed that coast enough to know it by its sounds. The reef runs along it a league or two out, like a wall with a few gates in it. Inside the reef the water is calm and shallow and full of coral heads that will rip the bottom out of a ship. No Spanish captain in his right mind would follow a small boat in there at night. That was why I meant to go.

I found the gate by the sound of it, the place where the long roar of the surf on the reef stopped for a few hundred yards and then started again. I took her through on the swell, with the white water close enough on either side that I could feel the spray on my face.

Then we were inside, and it was quiet.

“Bonesy,” I said. “Bow.”

He went forward and lay on his front on the bowsprit with his skull out over the water.

Bonesy can see in the dark. None of us knows how. He has no eyes to see with, but put him in a black hold and he will tell you where every rat is. He lay on the bowsprit that night and called the coral to me.

“Left a bit.”

I moved the tiller.

“Bit more.”

I moved it more.

“There’s one coming. Big. Right side. Go left.”

I went left. Something pale slid past under the hull, so close that I heard it scrape along the keel. Crumble swore under his breath.

“That was close,” Bonesy said cheerfully.

All night we went like that, creeping north inside the reef with the sails half down. Bonesy called the heads and I steered, and Crumble swung the lead and called the depth in a whisper. Nobody else spoke. Twice I felt the keel touch sand. Once we ran so close to the reef that the backwash off the breakers rocked us.

When the sky went grey in the east, I looked astern.

There was nothing behind us but sea.

I climbed up on the rail and looked again, all round, as far as I could see. No sail. No smoke. Nothing but the reef and the sea and the green coast going away to the north.

I came down and sat by the tiller. My hands had been on it all night, and I could not open them straight.

Crumble came aft and sat down across from me. He looked at the empty sea for a long time.

“Well,” he said.

“Well.”

“That was a good night’s work.”

I did not say anything. I was too tired to say anything, and besides I liked hearing it.

Crumble looked out at the sea again. Then he looked down at the stern locker where the jar was lashed.

“The river’s two days north of here,” he said.

I waited.

“She might not see us.” He rubbed his jaw. “Her people might not let us in. They don’t much care for strangers.”

“I know.”

“And if Cárdenas comes after us there.”

“He won’t. He’s on his way to Jamaica.”

The corner of Crumble’s mouth moved.

“All right,” he said. “We go.”

He got up and went forward without another word. A little while later I saw him at the bow with a bucket, washing out his red sash in seawater. He wrung it and washed it again, though it was no dirtier than it had been for a month.

Bonesy saw it too. He looked at me over the cabin top.

I shook my head at him.

For once in his life he kept quiet.

His hands were what I noticed at noon. He had swung the lead all night, wet line running through his palms, and the skin across both of them had come away in strips. He was hauling on the main halyard with them as if nothing were wrong.

“Let me see.”

“It’s nothing.”

“Show me.”

He showed me. I went below and dragged out the surgeon’s chest we had taken off the wreck, and I made him sit on the cabin top while I washed his hands in vinegar and bound them in clean linen. He told me twice that it was nothing. I told him to keep still. When I had finished he flexed his fingers inside the wrappings and looked at me in a way I did not care for.

“You’d have made a good nursemaid,” he said.

I shut the chest and did not answer.

That night I had the watch. Crumble slept in the bow with his bound hands folded on his chest. Bonesy sat on the cabin top with his hat in his lap, trying to punch the crease out of the crown with his fist. It went out and came back. He punched it again.

I sat by the tiller with the purse open on my knee and counted what was left of the finger money.

It did not take long. Vaz had paid us well, but we had spent a good part of it at Petit-Goâve, and we had spent more getting out. I made little stacks of the coins on the deck by the light of the binnacle and counted them and put them back. Then I took them out again.

“You’ve counted that twice,” Bonesy said.

“Three times.”

“Did it grow?”

“No.”

“Might on the fourth.”

I did not answer. I put the coins back in the purse and pulled the string tight.

The surgeon’s chest was still at my feet where I had left it. I had not put it away. The lid had come open a crack, and the smell of it came up to me in the warm dark. Vinegar, and old linen, and something sharp and bitter under both of them.

I knew that smell.

I opened the lid and looked. Among the jars and the folded linen there was a twist of brown paper, and inside it a little heap of reddish powder, dry as dust. I did not need to taste it. I had smelled it once before, on the counter of a shop in the port where I grew up, and I had never forgotten it.

Jesuits’ bark.

The apothecary’s shop was down near the harbour, in a street that smelled of fish and tar. It had a green door and a little window of thick glass with jars behind it. I had walked past it a hundred times as a boy and never once gone in.

I was seventeen. My mother had been dead three weeks.

Most of what my father had went on my schooling. The academy, the masters, the fencing room, the coat let out twice at the shoulders. What was left after that went on the physician who came to see my mother. He came once. He bled her and wrote out what she needed and took his fee, and the medicine he wrote for cost more than we had. My father sold his good sword to buy a little of it. It ran out on the fourth day. She died on the ninth.

Now my father had it.

I had seen what it did to her. The cold fits that shook the bed, so hard her teeth knocked. Then the heat, and the sweating, and the days in between when she lay quiet and grey and asked for water. And then the next fit, a little worse each time.

I had everything I could gather in a cloth in my fist. The coins I had saved. What my father had left in the box under the bed. What I had got for my books.

The apothecary was an old man with spectacles and stained fingers. He listened to me. He looked at the cloth when I opened it on his counter. He counted the coins with one finger, slowly, pushing each one aside.

Then he told me the price.

It was more than twice what I had.

“I can bring the rest,” I said. “In a week. Two weeks.”

He shook his head.

“I can work for it. I can sweep the shop. I can carry for you.”

“Son,” he said.

He did not say it unkindly. That was the worst of it. He looked at me over his spectacles as if he were truly sorry, and I believe now that he was. But he did not take the jar down from the shelf.

“It comes from Peru,” he said. “Across two oceans. I pay for it before it ever reaches me. If I gave it to every boy who came in here with a sick father, I would have none to sell, and then nobody would have it at all.”

He pushed the cloth back across the counter to me with the coins still in it.

I stood there for a long time. The jar was on the shelf behind him. I could see it. Brown glass with a paper label. I could have reached over the counter and taken it.

I picked up my coins and went out.

I walked home the long way, up the hill, past the big houses. Their windows were lit. There was music coming from one of them. I stood in the street outside it for a while and looked at the light. Somewhere in that house there was a jar like the one in the shop. It was sitting on a shelf, and nobody inside was even sick.

Then I went home.

My father was awake when I came in. He was in the quiet part of the fever, lying still, with his face grey on the pillow and his eyes open. He watched me come in and put the cloth down on the table.

“Well?” he said.

“He had none left,” I said. “He says there’s a ship due. Next week.”

My father looked at me for a long time.

He knew. I could see that he knew. He had always been able to tell when I was lying, ever since I was small, and I had never been able to work out how.

He did not say anything about it.

“Sit with me,” he said.

I pulled the stool over to the bed and sat down. After a while he put out his hand, and I took it. It was hot and dry and much thinner than I remembered.

He closed his eyes.

I sat there in the dark and listened to him breathe.

Bonesy had stopped punching his hat.

I did not know how long I had been sitting there with the twist of paper in my hand. The binnacle light had burned low. Forward, Crumble turned over in his sleep and his bound hands fell open on the deck.

Bonesy was looking at me.

He did not ask. He looked at the paper in my hand, and then at my face, and then he went back to his hat.

I folded the paper closed again. I did not put it back in the chest. I put it in my coat, in the inside pocket, next to the purse.

Then I took the purse out and counted the coins one more time.

We came to the river two days later in the afternoon.

You could see it long before you reached it. The sea changed colour from blue to green to a thick dull brown. There were branches floating in it, and whole trees with their roots still on them. The coast was low there and covered in mangroves, and the river came out of them in a wide brown mouth with a sandbar across it.

We waited for the flood and went over the bar on it. Then we were in the river, and the sea was behind us.

I did not like it.

I knew it would be bad. Crumble had told me, and I had been up a river or two in my time, chasing water or running from somebody. But I had never been this far up one, or for this long.

The trees came right down to the water on both sides. They leaned out over it, so that by the second day there was only a strip of sky above us, narrow and white and far away. Below it the river was brown and slow and smooth, and you could not see a hand’s depth into it. Shapes moved under the surface sometimes. A log on the bank opened its eyes and slid into the water as we passed.

There was no wind. We had to take the sails down and row. The air was hot and wet and thick and smelled of mud and rotting leaves, and it did not move at all. Mosquitoes came off the water in clouds.

And there was no horizon.

I had not known how much I needed one until it was gone. At sea I can always see where I am. I can look out and see the weather coming two days off, and see a ship before she sees me, and know where the land is by the colour of the sky above it. On that river I could see nothing past the next bend. Every bend looked like the last one. The trees closed in behind us as we went, and after a while I could not have told you which way the sea was.

The noise was worse. At sea the noise is wind and water, and it comes from far away. Here everything was close. Birds screamed in the trees right over our heads. Animals we never saw crashed through the branches. On the first evening something roared in the forest, a deep rising roar like a man being killed slowly. It went on for half an hour.

I had my hand on my hilt before I knew I had moved.

“Howler monkeys,” Crumble said, without looking round.

“Monkeys.”

“Little black ones. About so big.” He held his hands apart the length of a cat. “They do that every evening.”

I took my hand off my hilt. A little while later I noticed it was back.

Crumble was at home there in a way I had never seen him. He sat in the bow and watched the banks, and he knew the river. He knew which side of each bend the deep water was on. He knew where the sandbanks were before we struck them. When the river split, he pointed without hesitating. He smelled the air sometimes, as if it told him something.

I rowed and watched his back.

Every few minutes he would look round at me. Not for long. Just a glance over his shoulder, to see that I was there. Each time he did it I felt my shoulders come down a little, though I had not known they were up.

Bonesy loved it. He hung over the side and talked to the logs.

On the third day the river grew too narrow and too shallow for the sloop. Crumble found a creek running back into the mangroves on the left bank, and we poled her into it as far as she would go and made her fast to the roots. We cut branches and laid them over her deck until, from the river, she looked like another heap of drift.

Then we took what we could carry, and the jar, and went on foot.

Crumble led. There was a path, though I would not have found it on my own. It went up away from the river through the trees, and it was so narrow that the leaves brushed both my shoulders. It was darker under the trees than it had been on the river. I walked with one hand on my hilt and the other pushing branches out of my face, and I kept looking up for a sky I could not see.

Late in the afternoon the trees thinned, and I saw water through them.

It was a lake. Not a big one. The forest came down to the water all the way round it, dark green and still. The lake lay in the middle like a piece of the sky that had fallen down. Out in the middle of it there was an island, low and green. Above the island rose thin blue smoke from cooking fires.

I let out a breath I had been holding for three days.

Crumble had stopped at the edge of the trees. He was standing stock still.

“Is that it?” I said.

He did not answer.

Bonesy came up beside us and looked out at the island.

“Smoke,” he said. “Somebody’s cooking.”

Then a voice spoke out of the trees behind us.

It said something short and hard in Maya. I did not understand the words, but I understood the tone. I had heard it in a dozen languages. It meant don’t move.

I did not move.

Out of the corner of my eye I could see the tip of a spear, no more than a foot from Crumble’s neck.

Crumble did not turn round. He raised his empty hands slowly to his shoulders. Then he answered, in the same language, quiet and careful.

He said Balam’s name.

The spear did not move. But nobody used it.
