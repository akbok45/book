Ah Pech came down to the shore again the next morning.

Crumble was at his forms. The old man stood at the edge of the sand with his gourd of water and watched him go down the shore slowly and come back up fast. Once he walked out and touched Crumble’s elbow with two fingers, lower, and walked back. Crumble did the form again with the elbow where it had been put.

I sat on the upturned canoe and watched the old man watch him.

I have never been able to leave a man alone who knows something I do not. That is how I learned what I know. A fencing master at the academy who had taught two princes and drank before noon. A Portuguese on Tortuga who fought with a knife in his left hand and talked the whole time. A Dutch gunner with a boarding axe. A Spaniard on a burning deck who turned his wrist in a way I had never seen, once, before I killed him. I have turned mine that way ever since. I took something from every one of them, and most of them never knew.

When Ah Pech turned to go back up the path, I stood up.

“Ask him,” I said to Crumble.

Crumble was wiping the blade. “Ask him what?”

“If he’ll fight me.”

Crumble looked at me. Then he looked at the old man’s back, going up the path.

“He’s old.”

“He moved your foot a hand’s width and your blade came round twice as fast. Ask him.”

Crumble called after him in Maya.

Ah Pech stopped on the path. He turned round. He looked at me for a while, and then down at the two swords on my hips, and he said something short.

“He says not with those,” Crumble said.

“Sticks, then.”

Crumble told him. The old man stood there a moment longer. Then he went off into the trees at the edge of the shore, and I heard him cutting.

He came back with three sticks, green and stripped, with the bark still on the grips. Two were short, the length of my swords. One was long, the length of his war club. He threw the two short ones down on the sand at my feet.

He had watched me, then. He knew I fought with two.

Bonesy had come down from the house. He sat on the canoe where I had been sitting and put his hat on his knee.

“Go on, then,” he said.

⸻

I went in fast.

That is how I fight. I went in the way I go over a rail, both sticks at once, one high and one low. A man has to choose which to stop, and the other one hits him.

I hit nothing.

He was not where I had aimed. He had not stepped back. He had turned, a quarter turn on his back foot, so that both my sticks went past his body by a finger’s width. And as he turned, the end of his stick came round underneath my arms and tapped me on the ribs. Not hard. Hard enough.

I stepped back.

“One,” Bonesy said.

I went in again. I feinted high with the left, the way the master at the academy taught us, and came under with the right.

He turned. The stick tapped my ribs on the other side.

“Two.”

I tried everything. I tried the Portuguese’s trick of talking, which does not work when the other man does not know your language. I tried the Dutchman’s, which is to charge and keep charging. I tried tricks I had never shown anybody. Every time he was not there. Every time the end of his stick found my ribs or my wrist, and once the back of my knee, so that I sat down in the sand.

“Five,” Bonesy said. “Six.”

I was breathing hard. My shirt was wet through. The old man was not breathing hard at all.

And I saw something.

He had not once come forward. Not one step. He stood where he had stood at the start. He let me come to him every time, and turned. He waited for me the way a crab waits in a hole for your hand.

So I stood still.

I stood there with both sticks down and my chest going, and I waited.

He waited too.

We stood like that a long time. Crumble had stopped wiping his blade. A dog came down the shore and looked at us and went away again.

Then the old man came.

He came forward one step, fast, the long stick coming round at my head. I did not step back. I turned a quarter turn on my back foot, the way he had, and his stick went past my ear by a finger’s width. My right stick came round underneath as I turned.

It touched his hip.

Not hard. Hard enough.

He stopped.

He looked down at his hip where the stick had touched it. Then he looked at me.

He said something to Crumble without taking his eyes off me.

“He says that was his,” Crumble said.

“Tell him it’s mine now.”

Crumble looked at me. He did not tell him. He did not need to. The old man had heard it in my voice.

Ah Pech looked at me a while longer. His face did not change. Then he picked up the two short sticks out of the sand, and walked off up the path with all three of them under his arm.

“Six,” Bonesy said. “One.”

I sat down on the canoe beside him. My ribs ached on both sides. I was grinning so hard my face hurt.

Crumble put Balam’s blade back in its scabbard and stood looking at me.

“You stole that off him in a morning,” he said.

“That’s how you learn.”

He was quiet.

“Balam made me earn it,” he said. “Three years.”

He went up the path after the old man. I sat on the canoe with my ribs aching and turned my wrist over slowly, a quarter turn and back.

Amara sent for us that afternoon.

She sent the boy who had slept against my knee. He stood in the doorway and said something, and pointed, and ran off before Bonesy could put it into English. We went anyway.

She had the leaf open on the floor of her house, on its oiled cloth, in the light from the door. She was sitting cross legged in front of it with her elbows on her knees. She looked as though she had not slept.

Crumble sat down across from her.

I sat down at the end of the leaf. Bonesy stayed standing in the door with his hat in his hands.

“This is what I have,” Amara said.

She did not explain it slowly, for me. She said it the way you report what you have found to someone who already knows the ground.

She touched the half moon. “Here. Where you found it.” She ran her finger along the footprints. “The road. On the water, along the coast. North.” She touched the bar and the two dots beside it. “Seven. Seven days.” She touched the long island where the footprints stopped. “There. On the island, not the shore.” She glanced at me. “He found that out last night.”

“I had help,” I said.

She did not smile this time. She touched the face with the curls coming out of its mouth.

“And this. A place that speaks. On the island.” She sat back. “That is everything the leaf says to me. I have looked at it for six days. It will not say any more.”

She looked at Crumble.

“What did he tell you about the long island?”

Crumble was quiet a moment.

“That it was holy,” he said. “That women went there in canoes from all along the coast, to the goddess. To ask her for children. That the Spanish knocked down her house and put up a cross.”

“That’s what everybody knows.”

“That’s what he told me.”

“Nothing else? Nothing about a stone? About a place that makes a sound?”

“No.”

Amara turned back to the leaf.

“It will not give me any more by looking,” she said. “I can sit here till I go blind. It will not.”

“Then what?” I said.

She was quiet. She was choosing how to say it.

“There is an old way,” she said, “of going to ask.”

“Ask who?”

“The ones who know.” She touched the edge of the leaf with one finger. “The people who made this are dead. My father is dead. Everybody who could tell us what this face means is dead. So you go to where the dead are, and you ask.”

I looked at her to see if she was making a joke. She was not.

“And you come back,” I said.

“And you come back.”

“Will you go?”

“No.” She said it at once. “Somebody has to keep you. Somebody has to sit in the canoe and keep the drum and be here when you wake. That is mine.”

Crumble said, “I’ll go.”

He said it to the leaf, not to her.

Amara looked at him. She did not argue.

“Not alone,” she said. “Nobody goes down that road alone.”

There was a silence.

I looked at Crumble. He was looking at the leaf.

“Then I’m going,” I said.

That brought his head up.

He looked at me across the leaf. Then he put his hand out over it.

I took it.

He gripped hard. He has gripped hard since we were boys on our first ship, each trying to make the other let go first, and he has never once let go first. Neither have I. We sat there over that old painted bark with our knuckles going white, looking at each other.

“When you two are finished,” Amara said.

We let go at the same time. I could not feel my fingers.

Amara was watching me.

“What does it cost?” I said.

I had not meant to say it. It came out by itself, the way my hand goes to my purse.

She considered the question as if it were a fair one.

“Honey,” she said. “Bark. Three days. A night.” She paused. “Sometimes people don’t come all the way back.”

“What does that mean?”

“The man asleep in the house by the tree,” she said. “He went down the road alone.”

I thought about the man in the hammock, with his eyes moving under the lids.

Behind me in the doorway Bonesy put his hat on.

“How far down is it?” he said.

Amara looked up at him. She looked at him a long time, at the skull and the empty eyes under the brim, as if she were seeing something in him she had not thought to look for.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Further for some.”

I woke before light the next morning and went down to the shore.

Crumble was in the lake to his knees with his face to the east.

I sat down on the upturned canoe and waited.

The sky went grey, then yellow. When the first edge of the sun came over the trees he said the two words, the day’s name, under his breath.

Then he went on.

He said a great many more words. They were Maya and I did not know them. They came out low and steady and did not stop, the way water comes out of a cracked jar. He stood in the lake with the sun coming up in his face and talked and talked. Once his voice went up as if he were asking something. Then it went on again.

Then he stopped.

He stood there a long time with his hands at his sides.

He did not bend down and touch the water.

He turned round and came out of the lake, and saw me on the canoe, and stopped.

I did not say anything.

He sat down on the canoe beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. We sat there and watched the sun come up over the trees on the far shore.

After a while he said, “You ever drink anything that showed you the dead?”

“Port Royal rum,” I said. “Once.”

“Worse than that.”

“Nothing is worse than that.”

The corner of his mouth moved. He did not say anything else.
