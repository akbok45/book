The man with the jaguar’s shadow needed poles.

I did not know that was what he wanted. He came to our house in the morning with an axe over his shoulder and stood in the doorway and looked at me. The grey fisherman was behind him with another axe.

“Wood,” the fisherman said in Spanish. “You come.”

“For what?”

He thought about the word.

“House,” he said. He made a roof with his two hands. “New house. For the girl.” He grinned at me with every tooth. “The boy who cuts too deep. He marries.”

I had watched the boy spoil a fish on the stone by the water while his father showed him again how to cut. Somebody had said yes to him anyway. I picked up my swords and went.

⸻

We crossed to the shore in a canoe and went up into the forest on the far side.

I had not been under the trees since the day we came. I did not like it any better. The light came down green and thin, and there was no wind, and the noise of insects was so thick I felt it on my skin. The man with the jaguar’s shadow went first. He did not follow a path that I could see. He walked straight in among the trunks as if he were walking down a street he had lived on all his life.

I watched his shadow. Under the trees there was not enough sun to make one. Now and then he crossed a patch of light and it lay on the ground behind him for a moment, and it was only the shadow of a man with an axe.

I watched it anyway.

The fisherman walked beside me. He kept wetting his neck from a gourd he had hung round his shoulders on a cord.

“Dry here,” he said. “Bad.”

They wanted straight young trees about as thick as my arm. The man with the jaguar’s shadow chose them. He would stop and look up a trunk and put his hand on it, and then step back, and the fisherman would cut. He did not let me cut the first one. When I had watched them fell three, he handed me the axe without a word.

I cut the fourth. It came down where I meant it to.

He looked at the stump and nodded once, and walked on.

We worked our way along a slope until the poles were stacked like cordwood and my shirt was wet through. Then the man with the jaguar’s shadow stopped at the foot of a tree that was not like the others.

It was a ceiba. A big one, old, with a trunk so wide three men could not have joined hands round it. Its roots came out of the ground on every side like the walls of a house. High up, the branches went out flat and wide.

He put his hand on the bark at the height of his chest.

There were marks there. Four long scores in the bark, deep, fresh enough that the sap had run out of them and gone hard and red. Something had raked the tree with its claws, and pulled down, and the bark had come away in strips.

He touched them the way you touch a horse you know.

The fisherman stood beside me and looked at the marks, and then at the man, and then at me. He tapped his own chest and pointed at the man’s back.

“His,” he said.

I looked at the marks. They were as long as my forearm.

“His way,” I said.

The fisherman nodded.

Then he put his hand on my shoulder and turned me, and pointed higher up the trunk.

There was another set of marks there. Above the first, higher than I could have reached with my arm stretched up. These were old. The bark had grown over them in thick grey ridges, so that they were hard to see until you knew they were there. Four scores again, but longer, and set wider apart.

Whatever had made them had been bigger than the first. A good deal bigger.

“Whose are those?” I said.

The fisherman looked at the man with the jaguar’s shadow. The man had heard. He did not turn round.

“Balam,” the fisherman said.

I stood there looking up at the marks.

“Balam had a way.”

“Everybody,” the fisherman said. He waved his hand at the forest, at himself, at me. “Everybody has. Balam walked with his.” He held his hand up high, above his own head, as high as the marks. “Big. Black. Like night.”

“Black.”

“Black,” he agreed. He touched the old scores with one finger. “Long before. Balam come here to visit, his way come with him. Same tree.” He nodded at the man with the jaguar’s shadow. “That one was small boy. Follow Balam. Learn.”

The man with the jaguar’s shadow was standing with his hand still on the fresh marks, at the height of his own chest. He was looking up at the old ones.

He said something in Maya, quietly, not to us.

“What did he say?”

The fisherman shrugged.

“He says long time.”

We carried the poles down to the canoe. It took us three trips. On the last one I stopped at the edge of the trees and looked back up the slope.

From there I could not see the ceiba at all.

⸻

Crumble was sitting in the doorway of our house when I came back, with Balam’s blade across his knees and a rag and a pot of oil beside him.

He did that every evening. He did it the way he did his forms: the same strokes, in the same order, from the hilt to the point and back.

I sat down on the step below him and watched him do it for a while.

I should have waited. I know that now. I did not.

“Did Balam have a way?” I said.

His hand did not stop. The rag went down the blade.

“Everybody has a way.”

“That’s what the fisherman said.” I watched the rag. “He said Balam walked with his. A black one. It used to come out of the forest to him. There’s a tree on the far shore with its marks on it, higher than I can reach.”

The rag stopped.

He sat with it in his hand, halfway down the blade. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the oil on the steel.

“Who told you that?”

“I just did. The fisherman told me. The man with the shadow was standing right there.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“Balam didn’t,” he said.

He stopped. He did not finish it.

I waited.

“He’d have told me,” Crumble said. “Three years. He told me the names of the stars. He told me which leaves you can eat and which ones kill you in a day and which ones kill you in a week. He told me how his grandfather died. He’d have told me that.”

I did not say anything.

He looked at me then. His jaw was set.

“A fisherman,” he said. “A fisherman who sells salt.”

“And a tree.”

He looked down at the blade. Then he wiped the rag down the last of it, all the way to the point, and set the blade back in its scabbard and stood up.

“Where are you going?”

He did not answer. He went down the path toward the water, with the scabbard in his hand and not on his hip, and I did not follow him.

Bonesy was sitting inside the door behind me. I had not known he was there.

“You could’ve waited,” he said.

“I know.”

He did not say anything else. After a while he got up and went down the path after Crumble, slowly, the way you would walk up to a dog that has been kicked.

⸻

Amara sent for us in the morning.

She sent the boy who had slept against my knee. He stood in the doorway and said something, and pointed, and ran off before Bonesy could put it into English. We went anyway.

She had the leaf open on the floor of her house, on its oiled cloth, in the light from the door. She was sitting cross legged in front of it with her elbows on her knees. She looked as though she had not slept.

Crumble sat down across from her. He had come back in the night. He had not said where he had been, and I had not asked. He did not look at me now.

I sat down at the end of the leaf. Bonesy stayed standing in the door with his hat in his hands.

“This is what I have,” Amara said.

She did not explain it slowly, for me. She said it the way you report what you have found to someone who already knows the ground.

She touched the half moon. “Here. Where you found it.” She ran her finger along the footprints. “The road. On the water, along the coast. North.” She touched the bar and the two dots beside it. “Seven. Seven days.” She touched the long island where the footprints stopped. “There. On the island, not the shore.” She glanced at me. “He found that out last night.”

“I had help,” I said.

She did not smile this time. She touched the face with the curls coming out of its mouth.

“And this. A place that speaks. On the island.” She sat back. “That is everything the leaf says to me. I have looked at it for six days. It will not say any more.”

She looked at Crumble.

“What did he tell you about the long island?”

Crumble was quiet a moment.

“That it was holy,” he said. “That women went there in canoes from all along the coast, to the goddess. To ask her for children. That the Spanish knocked down her house and put up a cross.”

“That’s what everybody knows.”

“That’s what he told me.”

“Nothing else? Nothing about a stone? About a place that makes a sound?”

“No.”

She looked at him a long time.

“Did he tell you about his way?”

I saw it go into him. He did not move. His hands were flat on his knees and they stayed there. But the colour went out of his face under the brown, and his eyes went to the leaf and stayed on it.

Amara saw it too.

She had not known. I could see her understand it as she watched him. She had grown up with the black jaguar in the stories the old people told her, the way you grow up with the colour of your father’s eyes. It had never crossed her mind that he might keep it from anybody.

“He didn’t tell you,” she said.

“No.”

She did not say she was sorry. She looked down at her hands.

“I never saw it,” she said. “I was too small. They say it stopped coming before I can remember. They say he called it once, the first year after we came here to live, and it did not come.” She was quiet. “He never called it again.”

Nobody spoke.

Out on the lake somebody was calling to somebody else, and a dog was barking, and the sun was coming in through the door and lying across the leaf.

“There are things he knew,” Amara said at last, “that he did not teach either of us.”

Crumble looked up at her then.

“Either of us,” he said.

“Either of us.”

He looked at her a while longer. Then something in his shoulders let go, a little. Not all the way.

Amara turned back to the leaf.

“It will not give me any more by looking,” she said. “I can sit here till I go blind. It will not.”

“Then what?” I said.

She was quiet. She was choosing how to say it.

“There is an old way,” she said, “of going to ask.”

“Ask who?”

“The ones who know.” She touched the edge of the leaf with one finger. “The people who made this are dead. My father is dead. Everybody who could tell us what this face means is dead. So you go to where the dead are, and you ask.”

I looked at her to see if she was making a joke. She was not.

“You go,” I said.

“You drink, and you lie down in a canoe on the lake at night, and you go down the road. The village has always kept it. Most people go once in their life. Some never.” She looked at Crumble. “My father went. Before I was born.”

Crumble did not say anything.

“And you come back,” I said.

“And you come back.”

“Will you go?”

“No.” She said it at once. “Somebody has to keep you. Somebody has to sit in the canoe and keep the drum and be here when you wake. That is mine.”

Crumble said, “I’ll go.”

He said it to the leaf, not to her.

Amara looked at him. She did not argue.

“Not alone,” she said. “Nobody goes down that road alone.”

There was a silence.

I looked at Crumble. He was still looking at the leaf. He had not looked at me since he sat down.

“Then I’m going,” I said.

That brought his head up.

He looked at me for a long time. I do not know what he saw. Then he nodded once, the way he nods at sea when I have called the right sail, and looked back down.

Amara was watching me.

“What does it cost?” I said.

I had not meant to say it. It came out by itself, the way my hand goes to my purse.

She considered the question as if it were a fair one.

“Honey,” she said. “Bark. Three days. A night.” She paused. “Sometimes people don’t come all the way back.”

“What does that mean?”

“The man asleep in the house by the tree,” she said. “He went down the road alone.”

I thought about the man in the hammock, with his eyes moving under the lids.

Behind me in the doorway Bonesy put his hat on.

“How far down is it?” he said.

Amara looked up at him. She looked at him a long time, at the skull and the empty eyes under the brim, as if she were seeing something in him she had not thought to look for.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Further for some.”

⸻

I woke before light the next morning and went down to the shore.

Crumble was in the lake to his knees with his face to the east.

I sat down on the upturned canoe and waited.

The sky went grey, then yellow. When the first edge of the sun came over the trees he said the two words, the day’s name, under his breath.

Then he went on.

He said a great many more words. They were Maya and I did not know them. They came out low and steady and did not stop, the way water comes out of a cracked jar. He stood in the lake with the sun coming up in his face and talked and talked. Once his voice went up as if he were asking something. Then it went on again.

Then he stopped.

He stood there a long time with his hands at his sides.

He did not bend down and touch the water.

He turned round and came out of the lake, and saw me on the canoe, and stopped.

I did not say anything.

He sat down on the canoe beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. We sat there and watched the sun come up over the trees on the far shore.

After a while he said, “Three days.”

“Three days,” I said.
