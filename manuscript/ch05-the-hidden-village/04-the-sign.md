The grey fisherman came down to the shore the next morning with two spears.

They were not his fishing spears. They were poles the length of a man and a half, with no heads on them, and the ends bound in a fist of leather. He stopped in front of the upturned canoe where I was sitting and held one out to me.

“You,” he said in Spanish. “Spaniard. You fight good, they say.”

“Who says?”

He nodded down the shore, at the place where the canoes were pulled up. The spearmen who had been with him the day we came. They were sitting on the canoes, watching.

“They say you are fast,” he said. “I say we see.”

I looked at the pole he was holding out to me.

I have never been able to leave a man alone who fights some way I do not know. That is how I learned what I know. A fencing master at the academy who had taught two princes and drank before noon. A Portuguese on Tortuga who fought with a knife in his left hand and talked the whole time. A Dutch gunner with a boarding axe. A Spaniard on a burning deck who turned his wrist in a way I had never seen, once, before I killed him. I have turned mine that way ever since. I took something from every one of them. Most of them never knew.

I had never fought a man with a shark’s teeth.

I stood up and took off my sword belt and laid it on the canoe.

“Not with a pole,” I said. “Give me two sticks.”

He grinned at me with every tooth. He had known I would say it. He went to the canoes and came back with two short sticks, the length of my swords, and threw them to me one after the other. I caught them.

Bonesy came down from the house with his hat in his hand. He sat on the canoe beside my belt.

“Go on, then,” he said.

He did not stand on the sand. He walked backward into the lake until the water was over his knees, and stood there with the pole low, and waited for me.

I knew what he was doing. I went in anyway.

The water took my legs. It was like wading through wet sand. I went at him the way I go over a rail, both sticks at once, high and low. I was a heartbeat slower than I should have been. He was not there.

He had gone down. Not back. Down into the water to his chest, low and sideways, the way a shark turns under a boat. The end of the pole came up out of the lake and hit me in the belly.

I sat down in the water.

On the canoes the spearmen roared.

“One,” Bonesy said.

I got up. Water was running out of my nose. I was laughing. I had not laughed like that in a fight in a long time.

I went in again, slower. He went down again under my sticks and came up on my left, and the pole cracked against my hip. He moved in the water as if it were his. It was his. It was where he had lived his whole life, and I was a man standing in it up to his knees.

“Two.”

I stopped coming to him.

I backed up out of the deep water until it was only round my ankles, and stood there on the hard sand under it with both sticks down, and waited.

He looked at me. He showed me his teeth.

“Spaniard is not stupid,” he said.

He came out after me. He had to. Nobody wins a fight by standing in a lake.

In the shallows he could not go down. He was only a man now, a strong one, with a long reach and a pole. He thrust at my chest. I turned it with the left stick and stepped in along it, inside his reach, where a pole is no use to anybody. He tried to go down and twist away, the way he had in the deep water, and there was not enough water to go down into.

I hit him on the wrist, the ribs and the side of the neck, one after the other. I did it as fast as I can, which is fast. Then I hooked the pole out of his hands with the right stick and laid the left one across his throat, just under the gills.

He stood still.

The spearmen on the canoes had gone quiet.

The slits in his neck opened and closed against the stick. He looked down at it, and then at me.

Then he laughed, the whole mouthful of teeth, and the spearmen roared again.

“Two,” Bonesy said. “And one, but it was the one that counts.”

I took the stick away.

The fisherman rubbed his wrist. He looked at it and then at me.

“Again,” he said. “Tomorrow. In the water.”

“In the water you win.”

“In the water I win,” he agreed. “So you learn.”

He picked up his pole and walked off up the shore to the spearmen, and they made room for him on the canoes, and one of them hit him on the shoulder.

I stood there in the shallows with my shirt soaked and my belly aching where the pole had caught me, and the stick still in my hand. I went back into the lake to my knees, alone. I bent my legs and went down sideways and low, the way he had gone, under the stroke of a blade that was not there.

I came up too slow. I tried it again. Better.

Crumble was sitting on the canoe beside Bonesy, with Balam’s blade across his knees. I had not seen him come down.

“You’re learning to swim like a shark now,” he said.

“I’m learning to fight like one.”

“In a morning.”

“That’s how you learn.”

He looked out at the lake for a while.

“Balam made me earn everything,” he said. “Three years.”

He got up and went back to the house. I went down sideways into the water again, and came up, and did it again until I had it.

Amara sent for us that afternoon.

She sent the boy who had slept against my knee. He stood in the doorway and said something, and pointed, and ran off before Bonesy could put it into English. We went anyway.

She had the leaf open on the floor of her house, on its oiled cloth, in the light from the door. She was sitting cross legged in front of it with her elbows on her knees. She looked as though she had not slept.

Crumble sat down across from her.

I sat down at the end of the leaf. Bonesy stayed standing in the door with his hat in his hands.

“This is what I have,” Amara said.

She did not explain it slowly, for me. She said it the way you report what you have found to someone who already knows the ground.

She touched the half moon. “Here. Where you found it.” She ran her finger along the footprints. “The road. On the water, along the coast. North.” She touched the bar and the two dots beside it. “Seven. Seven days.” She touched the long island where the footprints stopped. “There. On the island, not the shore.” She glanced at me. “He found that out last night.”

“I had help,” I said.

She did not answer that. She touched the sign at the end of the footprints, the face with the curls coming out of its mouth.

“And this. A place that speaks. On the island.” She sat back. “That is everything the leaf says to me. I have looked at it for six days. It will not say any more.”

She looked at Crumble.

“What did he tell you about the long island?”

Crumble was quiet a moment.

“That it was holy,” he said. “That women went there in canoes from all along the coast, to the goddess. To ask her for children. That when the Spanish came, they smashed her statues and pulled down her temple, and put up a cross where it had stood.”

“That’s what everybody knows.”

“That’s what he told me.”

“Nothing else? Nothing about a stone? About a place that makes a sound?”

“No.”

Amara turned back to the leaf.

“It will not give me any more by looking,” she said. “I can sit here till I go blind. It will not.”

“Then what?” I said.

She was quiet. She was choosing how to say it.

“There is an old way,” she said, “of going to ask.”

“Ask who?”

“The ones who know.” She touched the edge of the leaf with one finger. “The people who made this are dead. My father is dead. Everybody who could tell us what this face means is dead. So you go to where the dead are, and you ask.”

I looked at her to see if she was making a joke. She was not.

“And you come back,” I said.

“And you come back.”

“Will you go?”

“No.” She said it at once. “Somebody has to keep you. Somebody has to sit in the canoe and keep the drum and be here when you wake. That is mine.”

Crumble said, “I’ll go.”

He said it to the leaf, not to her.

Amara looked at him. She did not argue.

“Not alone,” she said. “Nobody goes down that road alone.”

There was a silence.

I looked at Crumble. He was looking at the leaf.

“Then I’m going,” I said.

That brought his head up.

He looked at me across the leaf. Then he put his hand out over it.

I took it.

He gripped hard. He has gripped hard since we were boys on our first ship, each trying to make the other let go first, and he has never once let go first. Neither have I. We sat there over that old painted bark with our knuckles going white, looking at each other.

“When you two are finished,” Amara said.

We let go at the same time. I could not feel my fingers.

Amara was watching me.

“What does it cost?” I said.

I had not meant to say it. It came out by itself, the way my hand goes to my purse.

She considered the question as if it were a fair one.

“Honey,” she said. “Bark. Three days. A night.” She paused. “Sometimes people don’t come all the way back.”

“What does that mean?”

“The man asleep in the house by the tree,” she said. “He went down the road alone.”

I thought about the man in the hammock, with his eyes moving under the lids.

Behind me in the doorway Bonesy put his hat on.

“How far down is it?” he said.

Amara looked up at him. She looked at him a long time, at the skull and the empty eyes under the brim, as if she were seeing something in him she had not thought to look for.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Further for some.”

I woke before light the next morning and went down to the shore.

Crumble was in the lake to his knees with his face to the east.

I sat down on the upturned canoe and waited.

The sky went grey, then yellow. When the first edge of the sun came over the trees he said the two words, the day’s name, under his breath.

Then he went on.

He said a great many more words. They were Maya and I did not know them. They came out low and steady and did not stop, the way water comes out of a cracked jar. He stood in the lake with the sun coming up in his face and talked and talked. Once his voice went up as if he were asking something. Then it went on again.

Then he stopped.

He stood there a long time with his hands at his sides.

He did not bend down and touch the water.

He turned round and came out of the lake, and saw me on the canoe, and stopped.

I did not say anything.

He sat down on the canoe beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. We sat there and watched the sun come up over the trees on the far shore.

After a while he said, “You ever drink anything that showed you the dead?”

“Port Royal rum,” I said. “Once.”

“Worse than that.”

“Nothing is worse than that.”

The corner of his mouth moved. He did not say anything else.
