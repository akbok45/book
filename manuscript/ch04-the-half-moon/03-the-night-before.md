We went back over the scrub as the light went. It took the skin off us a second time. The sloop lay where we had left her in the lee, swinging to her cable over the sand. We could hear the sea on the windward rock from the deck. There is nowhere on that island you cannot hear it.

Crumble boiled the last of the salt beef with biscuit broken into it, in the copper kettle Bonesy had claimed off the wreck. Bonesy allowed it. A man shares his kettle.

“First light,” Crumble said.

“Slack water’s an hour after.”

“Then an hour after first light.” He scraped the kettle with his spoon. “Sleep.”

“I’ve the watch.”

“There’s nobody for forty leagues.”

“Then it’ll be a short one.”

He looked at me over the kettle. Then he put his back to the mast and shut his eyes. With Crumble that is the end of an argument, not the end of the thinking.

Bonesy went forward and sat on the bitts with his heels knocking the planking. He took up with the anchor cable.

“Long day for you.”

The cable took the weight of the boat each time she lifted, and said nothing.

“All day you held. Nobody said.” He patted it. “Nobody ever says, with a cable.”

I sat aft with my arm over the tiller. The captain’s voice was still in my ear, the way he had said Rodrigo’s name with his point on my chest. Somewhere between one swell and the next I slept.

\* \* \*

“My father has my commission,” Rodrigo said. “Who’s writing yours?”

He said it low, at the edge of the chalk, so only I heard. Then he stepped back and saluted, and smiled at the room.

It was the last bout of the term. The whole school was along the walls of the fencing room, twenty boys with their backs to the plaster. The master sat in his chair under the high window with his cane across his knees. The black swords were iron, blunt on both edges, with the point beaten flat into a button. They weighed what a real blade weighs. They broke fingers every year.

I saluted him back. My hand was steady. I was sixteen and I was the best in that room with a sword, and every boy on those walls knew it.

We came onto the circle.

He was good. People forget that when they tell it now. He was strong and patient and he did the forms exactly as they were taught, and he never reached. I took the first touch off him anyway. I bound his blade, turned it out, and put my button under his arm before his feet had caught up.

“One,” the master said.

We came back to the edge. Rodrigo rolled his shoulder where I had hit him.

“She came to supper on Sunday,” he said. “At our house.”

I knew who. There was only one she. Her family had the house with the blue shutters by the cathedral steps, and for a year I had walked past those shutters every evening on my way home.

The heat started at the back of my neck.

We closed. I went at him harder than I needed to and he took it on his forte and gave nothing. I went again. He stepped round the circle and let me come, and when I came too far he hit me across the ribs. It went through me like a cramp. I heard a boy on the wall suck in his breath.

“One,” the master said. “One each.”

Rodrigo did not look at me while we walked back. He looked at the floor.

“My mother asked about your father,” he said. “Where he came from. Before. Nobody at the table could say.”

The heat came up into my face. My ears filled with it. It sounded like surf on a reef, a long way off and then close, and under it I could hear my own heart going. The room got smaller. The boys on the walls went away. The master went away. There was the chalk under my feet and Rodrigo’s mouth and the button of his blade, and nothing else in the world.

“Her father had a letter from mine this week,” he said. “About her.”

I do not remember the master saying to begin.

I went through his guard. I did not bind it or turn it. I beat it aside with all my weight behind my blade, and his wrist gave, and I put my button into the hollow of his throat hard enough to rock his head back.

“Two to him,” the master said. “Enough.”

Rodrigo stopped. He lowered his point and took one step back to the edge of the chalk, as you are meant to. He lifted his blade to salute.

I hit the arm he was saluting with.

The iron made a flat sound on the bone. His blade went sideways. He looked at me, and his face had nothing in it yet, the way a man looks when a rope parts in his hands. I hit him on the shoulder. He got his blade up late and I beat it down and hit him above the ear.

He went back off the circle. His heel caught the rack on the wall and the black swords came down around him, ringing on the boards. He fell into them. I went after him.

I could not hear anything now. Not the boys. Not the swords still rolling on the floor. I could see him on his back with his arms up over his face, and I could feel the grip in my hand, and the iron had gone light as a cane. I hit his arms. I hit them again. Every time it landed I wanted it to land harder.

The cane caught me across the wrist. The pain went up to my elbow and I did not let go. Two boys had my arms. A third got me round the neck and hauled, and my heels went out from under me on the sand, and still I swung at the air where Rodrigo had been.

They held me down on the floor until I stopped.

It took a while. When the roar went out of my ears the room came back all at once: twenty boys breathing, a sword still turning slowly on the boards, the master standing over me with the cane in his hand. My chest was going like a pump. My wrist had begun to swell. I opened my hand and the black sword fell out of it.

Rodrigo got up slowly out of the fallen blades. There was blood coming from his hairline into one eye. He did not wipe it. He held his left arm against his body and looked at me with the other eye.

“It was over,” he said. “You had it. You’d already had it.”

The master looked at me a long time.

“Not in this room,” he said. “Not again.”

Rodrigo picked up his own sword with his good hand. He set it back in the rack, and saluted the master, and walked out past the boys on the wall. He did not salute me.

\* \* \*

“Jigsaw.”

It was grey. The land had come up black against it in the east, and there was dew on the rail and on my arm. My hand was on the tiller, and it was clamped on the wood so hard I had to tell the fingers to let go one at a time.

Crumble was crouched by me. He had his hand on my shoulder and he took it away.

“Light’s coming,” he said.

“I see it.”

Bonesy was sitting on the cabin top with his hat on his knees. I do not know how long he had been there.

“You were talking,” he said.

“Was I.”

“Said a name.” He turned the hat round once on his knees. “Rodrigo.”

Crumble had gone still beside me.

“Who’s Rodrigo,” Bonesy said.

“Nobody.”

“Nobody’s got a name.” He waited. I gave him nothing, and he let it sit a long while, and then he put the hat on. “All right.”

Crumble stood. He looked at me a moment more. Then he went forward and started laying out the line for the pool, because there would be slack water in an hour and the line had to be ready.

I got up and helped him. Over the ridge the sea was still going at the windward rock. In an hour the pool would stop pulling. My hands were steady on the line. I did not look at them to check.
