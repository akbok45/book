We went back over the scrub as the light went. It took the skin off us a second time. The sloop lay where we had left her in the lee, swinging to her cable over the sand. We could hear the sea on the windward rock from the deck. There is nowhere on that island you cannot hear it.

Crumble boiled the last of the salt beef with biscuit broken into it, in the copper kettle Bonesy had claimed off the wreck. Bonesy allowed it. A man shares his kettle.

“First light,” Crumble said.

“Slack water’s an hour after.”

“Then an hour after first light.” He scraped the kettle with his spoon. “Sleep.”

“I’ve the watch.”

“There’s nobody for forty leagues.”

“Then it’ll be a short one.”

He looked at me over the kettle. Then he put his back to the mast and shut his eyes. With Crumble that is the end of an argument, not the end of the thinking.

Bonesy went forward and sat on the bitts, his heels knocking against the planking. He took off his hat and turned it over in his hands, examining it as though he had found something new about it.

I sat aft with my arm over the tiller. The captain’s voice was still in my ear, the way he had said Rodrigo’s name with his point on my chest. Somewhere between one swell and the next I slept.

The fencing room was at the far end of the academy, beyond the chapel and the master’s offices. Its ceiling was high, with dark beams crossing above us, and tall windows let the afternoon sun fall in long strips across the boards. Portraits of Spanish admirals hung between the windows. At the far end, beneath the royal arms, the fencing master sat with his cane across his knees.

Twenty boys stood along the walls for the last bout of the term. Most wore their best coats and waistcoats, with clean stockings and buckled shoes. The richer boys had lace at their cuffs and buttons of silver or polished brass. Rodrigo wore a dark blue coat that fitted him perfectly. Mine had been let out at the shoulders twice, and the cuffs had begun to fray.

The swords stood in racks along the wall. They were iron, blunt on both edges, with a rounded button of metal at the tip to keep a thrust from running a boy through. They weighed what real blades weighed, and every year they broke somebody’s fingers.

A circle had been drawn in chalk across the middle of the floor.

I stood at its edge.

I had spent most of my life learning where I was allowed to stand. There were houses I could enter through the front door and houses where I was expected to wait at the back. There were boys whose fathers would see them commissioned regardless of how they performed, and boys like me, who were told that excellence might be enough.

The chalk was another line. But inside it, at least, I knew what I was worth.

Rodrigo came up beside me.

“My father has my commission,” he said quietly. “Who’s writing yours?”

I looked at him.

He stepped over the chalk and saluted the master. Then he turned and smiled at the boys along the wall.

I followed him in.

I was sixteen. I had beaten every boy in that room, Rodrigo included, and I knew I could beat him again.

Rodrigo was good. Strong, patient, and careful with his feet. He had an excellent guard and rarely reached farther than he needed to. When we practiced together, he was one of the few boys who could make me work for a touch.

We saluted.

The master lowered his hand.

Rodrigo came forward with a cut toward my shoulder. I caught it on the stronger part of my blade and answered toward his chest. He turned my point aside and pressed me back a step.

I let him come.

He struck again, this time low, and I moved my foot just beyond his reach. His blade passed in front of my knee. Before he could recover, I turned my wrist and brought my point beneath his arm.

“One to Jigsaw,” the master called.

Rodrigo lowered his sword and rolled the shoulder where I had struck him.

We returned to the edge.

“She came to supper on Sunday,” he said. “At our house.”

I knew who he meant.

Her family lived in the house with the blue shutters beside the cathedral steps. For a year I had walked past those shutters on my way home, sometimes slowing when I thought I saw movement behind them.

I looked at Rodrigo.

He was examining the tip of his sword as though he had said nothing worth remembering.

The master called us forward.

I attacked first. Rodrigo caught my blade and turned it aside. I struck again, harder, and he gave ground toward the chalk.

I thought I had him.

He let his guard open just enough to show me his shoulder. I lunged for it, and he stepped across my line, bringing his point against the outside of my thigh.

The blow stung through my breeches.

“One each,” the master said.

Rodrigo stepped back and saluted.

I could hear two boys whispering along the wall. I knew they had seen me fall for the opening. I also knew Rodrigo had left it deliberately.

He had scored fairly.

That did not make me feel any better.

We returned to the edge.

“She’s a fine girl,” he said.

I tightened my grip.

The master gave the signal.

This time I waited.

Rodrigo advanced, testing my guard with two quick cuts. I caught the first and stepped away from the second. He followed, keeping his point toward my chest, trying to draw me into another mistake.

I let him think it was working.

When he came forward again, I stepped into him instead of away. Our blades met near the hilts. I turned mine over his, pushed his point aside, and struck him cleanly across the chest.

“Two to Jigsaw.”

It was the best touch of the bout.

Rodrigo looked down at the place where I had struck him. He had not expected me to come inside his guard.

I smiled.

My heart was hammering, and a wonderful heat had spread through my chest. I could feel it in my arms, in my fingertips, even in the soles of my feet. The room seemed brighter, every sound sharper. The sting in my thigh was still there, but now I liked it. I liked the way it reminded me that I was in a fight.

I wanted another touch. Then another. I wanted to see that look on Rodrigo’s face again, the moment he realized I had beaten him.

I had spent years training for the satisfaction of winning. This was different. Winning gave me a rush that lasted only a moment, and already I could feel it slipping away. I wanted it back before it had even gone.

Rodrigo saw me smiling.

He did not smile back.

We walked back to the chalk.

“My mother asked about your father,” he said. “Where he came from. Before.”

I turned my head.

“Nobody at the table could say.”

My jaw tightened. I felt my teeth press together, the hard scrape of one against another.

He knew where my father had come from. Everyone at the academy knew enough to whisper about it. Rodrigo had heard the stories as often as I had, and he knew which parts of them I hated.

He stood opposite me in his fine coat, with his commission already promised and his father’s name behind him. He had never earned any of it. He had been born, and the world had made room.

I felt my pulse in my neck.

The master called us forward.

I went at Rodrigo hard.

He caught my first cut, then my second. I pressed him backward, striking high and low, trying to break through his guard by force. Each time our blades met, the shock traveled into my wrist.

He was near the edge.

I saw his shoulder open and went for it.

He had shown me the same opening before.

I knew that even as I moved.

He stepped aside and drove his point into my ribs.

The pain went through me like a knife. My breath stopped. I staggered sideways and nearly crossed the chalk before catching myself.

“Two each,” the master called.

I bent forward, one hand against my side.

For several moments I could not draw a proper breath. My ribs seemed to close around something sharp. When I finally managed to inhale, the pain came again.

I heard Rodrigo moving back toward the edge.

I straightened.

My heart was pounding so hard that I felt it in my throat. Heat had climbed from my chest into my neck and face. Sweat ran beneath my collar. My sword hand trembled, and I closed it harder around the grip.

The room had begun to sound strange. I could hear the scrape of Rodrigo’s shoes, the rustle of his coat, the air going in and out of my own nose. The boys along the walls seemed very far away.

I looked at Rodrigo.

He was watching me carefully.

I wanted to hit him.

Not score against him. Not prove I was the better swordsman.

I wanted to hurt him badly enough that he would remember it.

We returned to the edge for the last touch.

“Her father had a letter from mine this week,” Rodrigo said. “About her.”

He spoke softly. I almost missed the words.

Then I understood them.

The heat in my face seemed to spread behind my eyes. My jaw clenched until it hurt. I could feel my pulse in my fingertips, and for a moment I thought I might be sick.

I looked at Rodrigo’s mouth.

I imagined him sitting at her family’s table, speaking to her father as though he already belonged there. I imagined the two families discussing their future while mine was being decided in rooms I would never be invited into.

His father had secured him a commission. Now he was arranging the girl as well.

Rodrigo stood across from me with his sword raised, waiting.

I wanted him to say something else.

Anything.

The master lifted his hand.

I watched it fall.

Rodrigo gave ground as soon as I came forward. He expected me to rush him, and I did.

He kept his blade between us and retreated toward the edge, waiting for me to reach too far. I struck toward his head. He caught it. I struck toward his shoulder, and he turned that aside as well.

Then he showed me an opening at his chest.

I saw it.

I saw the trap behind it too.

For a moment I wanted to laugh. He thought he was about to catch me the same way he had before.

I wanted him to believe it. I wanted him to see me take the opening, to feel that little moment of triumph when he thought he had finally outsmarted me.

And then I wanted to take it away.

I lunged.

Rodrigo thrust as I came in. I turned my body just enough that his point passed beside my ribs, close enough to catch the cloth of my coat. I stepped through and drove my own point into the hollow of his throat.

His head snapped backward.

He staggered, coughing, and one hand went to his neck.

“Three to Jigsaw,” the master called. “The bout is finished.”

I heard him.

Rodrigo lowered his sword.

I stood looking at him.

My breath was coming hard through my nose. My ribs ached. My heart was beating so violently that I could feel it behind my eyes.

I had won.

But the feeling was already going.

The heat in my chest had begun to fade, and I hated it. Rodrigo was standing there with his commission waiting for him, his father’s name, and the girl whose family had invited him to supper.

I had beaten him in front of everyone.

Tomorrow he would still have everything.

Rodrigo straightened slowly and raised his blade to salute.

I stepped forward and struck him across the side of the head.

The iron caught him above the ear.

He went down hard, one knee striking the boards before the rest of him followed. His sword clattered beside him.

Nobody moved.

For a moment I stood over him with my sword still raised.

I could feel the rush returning.

Then the master was on his feet.

“Enough!”

His cane struck the floor.

I looked toward him.

The room came back around me. The boys along the walls, the portraits, the afternoon sun. Rodrigo was on one knee, holding the side of his head. Blood had begun to run between his fingers.

I lowered my sword.

The master crossed the room and took it from my hand.

“Outside,” he said.

I looked at Rodrigo once more.

He was staring at me with an expression I had never seen on his face before.

Not fear. Not quite.

Disbelief.

He had beaten me twice that afternoon, and I had beaten him three times. We both knew the result.

But I had struck him when he wasn’t defending himself.

And I had enjoyed it.

I walked out past the boys along the wall.

Nobody spoke.

By the following morning, I was no longer a student at the academy.

“Jigsaw.”

I opened my eyes.

It was grey. The island lay black against the eastern sky, and dew had gathered along the rail.

My hand was wrapped around the tiller.

I had been gripping it so tightly that my fingers hurt. I had to loosen them one at a time.

Crumble was crouched beside me, his hand on my shoulder.

“Light’s coming,” he said.

“I see it.”

He took his hand away.

For a moment I could still feel the iron in my fist.

My ribs no longer hurt, of course. The bruise had gone before I left the academy. But I could remember exactly where Rodrigo had struck me, and the strange pleasure that had followed the pain.

Ten years had passed.

I could still remember the sound of the sword against his arm.

Bonesy sat on the cabin top with his hat resting on his knees.

“You were talking,” he said.

“Was I?”

“Said a name.” He turned the hat once in his hands. “Rodrigo.”

Crumble went still beside me.

“Who’s Rodrigo?” Bonesy asked.

“Nobody.”

“Nobody’s got a name.”

I said nothing.

Bonesy watched me for a moment, then settled his hat on his head.

“All right.”

Crumble stood and went forward to prepare the line for the pool.

I remained beside the tiller.

I had spent years telling myself Rodrigo had brought it on himself. He had known exactly what to say, and he had kept saying it until I could bear no more.

I still believed that, some mornings.

But I had heard the master call the final touch. I had seen Rodrigo lower his sword. I had known the bout was over.

And I remembered how badly I had wanted to hit him again.

Crumble called for help with the line.

I got up and went forward.

The sea was still breaking against the windward rock. In an hour the pool would stop pulling, and we would have our chance to go down.

I took the line from Crumble and began laying it out.

My hands were steady now.
