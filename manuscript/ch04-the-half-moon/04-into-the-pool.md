We crossed the ridge with the line coiled over Crumble’s shoulder and the sun not yet clear of the sea.

The pool was grey in that light. The tarpon were in it, circling slow, nose to tail. Every few turns the big one rolled up to the surface and gulped air with a loud pop, the way tarpon do, and sank back into the line. They were slower now. The pull was going out of the water as the tide came to the top. I could see it on the surface, where the small ripples off the rock had stopped leaning one way.

“How long,” Crumble said.

“Half an hour. Less.”

He put the coil down on the rock and found the end of it.

Bonesy took off his hat. He held it out to me, and I took it, and he stood at the edge of the pool while Crumble passed the line twice round his ribs and made it fast.

“Three pulls, you come up,” Crumble said. “One pull, you’re all right.”

“And no pulls.”

“Then we haul.”

Bonesy looked down at the knot on his ribs. He looked at the water.

“Fish don’t bite, do they.”

“Not you,” I said. “Nothing to bite.”

“That’s a comfort.”

He stepped off the edge and went straight down.

He did not swim. He sank like a stone, upright, with his arms loose at his sides. When his feet found the bottom he walked. The tarpon opened round him. The big one turned off and went round the far side of the pool and came back, and went past Bonesy close enough to touch, with its eye on him. Bonesy put out a hand to it. It did not stop.

He walked toward the low side, where the rock goes down under the pool and the light stops. Crumble paid out the line hand over hand. I watched the skull go into the shadow under the rock, and then the shoulders, and then I could not see him at all.

The line kept going out.

Crumble let it run through his palms. He did not take his eyes off the water. Twenty fathoms of line, we had brought. When a little under half of it was gone, it stopped.

Nothing happened.

I stood with the hat in my hands and counted. A living man can hold his breath for perhaps a hundred, if he is good at it and not frightened. I got to two hundred. Crumble had the line across his palm, light, feeling for him.

Then it twitched once.

Crumble let his breath go.

It was a long while before the line came back. Crumble took it in slowly and coiled it at his feet. The skull came up out of the shadow first and then the rest of him, walking up the slope of the bottom toward us with the water running out between his ribs. He came up the side of the pool and sat down on the rock and let the water drain out of him.

“Well,” Crumble said.

“There’s a wall.” Bonesy shook his head and water came out of his eye holes. “Down under the low side. Goes in a way, then a wall.”

“Rock,” I said.

“Looks like rock.” He held up his hand. “Put my hand on it. Went in to the wrist.”

Neither of us said anything.

“Then the arm. Then the rest of me.” He looked at the hand as if it might tell him something more. “Other side’s air. Dark. Dry stone. Steps.”

“How far,” Crumble said. “From where the light stops to the air.”

Bonesy thought about it.

“For you?” He looked at me. “Two breaths. If you don’t stop to look.”

The surface of the pool had gone flat. The tarpon were barely moving now, hanging in the water with their fins working slowly. It was as near slack as it was going to get.

I gave Bonesy his hat back.

I took off my coat and my shirt and my boots and set them on the rock. I took off the belt with my swords on it and laid it on top of the coat. My hands did all of this on their own, the way they do the small work on a deck. While they did it my stomach went tight and then tighter, until it felt like a fist under my ribs.

Crumble passed me the end of the line.

I tied it round my middle. I had tied that knot ten thousand times. It took me two tries. Crumble watched me do it and said nothing at all.

I went down the rock to the edge of the pool and stood where I had stood at twenty.

The water was clear all the way down. I could see the bottom, pale and bare, and the low side where it went under the rock and turned black. I looked at the black for a long time. I could feel my heart in my neck. I could feel the old wish to turn round and walk back over the scrub, and every reason I had used for five years to do it was standing there ready for me.

Behind me Crumble had the line in both hands. I heard Bonesy get up off the rock and come down behind me. He put his hand on my shoulder, bone and nothing else, and left it there.

Nobody told me to go.

I took one breath and let it out. I took another, as deep as it would go, and I went in.

The cold took my chest first. I kicked down and the tarpon parted round me, great silver sides going past on both hands. I swam down as I felt the power of the fish in the water brush against my chest. The big one turned and came with me for a few strokes, his eye level with mine. Then he went away into the pale water and I was alone, going down the slope of the bottom toward the black.

The light went green, then grey. I put my hand out in front of me.

There was rock. I could see it. A wall of it, grey and pitted, filling the whole of the passage from floor to roof. It looked exactly like every other piece of stone on that island. My hand came up against it and I braced for the scrape.

It did not come.

My fingers went into the rock and my hand went after them. I felt nothing at all. No stone, no cold, not even the water change. The wall was simply there around my wrist and then around my elbow, as if my arm had never had anything in its way.

Every part of me wanted to pull back.

I kicked forward and put my face into it.

For one moment it was dark, darker than the bottom of a hold with the hatch on. My chest burned. I kicked again and again, and felt the line drag at my middle, and kept my hand out in front of me.

My hand broke the surface into air.

I came up after it, too fast, and struck my head on stone above me and went down again. Then I found a step under my feet and got my face clear and breathed.

It was black. The air was cold and dry and smelled of nothing living. Somewhere close by water was dripping. I stood on a stone step up to my waist in water and breathed until my chest stopped heaving, and my heart slowed down, and I could hear something besides it.

“Took your time,” Bonesy said, out of the dark.

He had come down after me without a line. I had not heard him.

“How long have you been here.”

“Came down behind you.” I heard him shift on the stone. “Didn’t want you sitting here on your own.”

I did not answer that.

Behind me the water stirred. A hand came up out of it and found the step beside me, and then Crumble’s head broke the surface with a gasp. He came up blowing like a whale and shook the water out of his eyes. He reached out and found my shoulder in the dark and held it, the way you hold a rail in a seaway, until he had his breath.

“Well,” he said.

“Well.”

Behind us, under the water, the passage we had come through glowed faintly green with the morning outside. In that light I could just make out stone steps going up in front of us, cut square and even, and above them the black mouth of a doorway.

Crumble let go of my shoulder.

We stood there in the water and looked at the steps.
