We crossed the ridge with the line coiled over Crumble’s shoulder and the sun not yet clear of the sea.

The pool was grey in that light. The tarpon were in it, circling slowly, nose to tail. Every few turns the big one rolled up to the surface and gulped air with a loud pop, the way tarpon do, and sank back into its place in the circle. They were slower now. The pull was going out of the water as the tide came to the top. I could see it on the surface, where the small ripples off the rock had stopped leaning one way.

“How long till it stops pulling?” Crumble said.

“Half an hour. Less.”

He put the coil down on the rock and found the end of it.

Bonesy took off his hat. He held it out to me, and I took it. Then he stood at the edge of the pool while Crumble passed the line twice around his ribs and made it fast.

“Three pulls, you come up,” Crumble said. “One pull, you’re all right.”

“And no pulls?”

“Then we haul.”

Bonesy looked down at the knot on his ribs. He looked at the water.

“Fish don’t bite, do they?”

“Not you,” I said. “Nothing to bite.”

“That’s a comfort.”

He stepped off the edge and went straight down.

He did not swim. He sank like a stone, upright, with his arms loose at his sides. When his feet found the bottom, he walked.

The tarpon opened around him. The big one turned off and went around the far side of the pool before coming back past Bonesy, close enough to touch, with its eye on him. Bonesy put out a hand to it. It did not stop.

He walked toward the low side, where the rock went down under the pool and the light stopped. Crumble paid out the line hand over hand. I watched Bonesy’s skull go into the shadow beneath the rock, then his shoulders, and then I could not see him at all.

The line kept going out.

Crumble let it run through his palms. He did not take his eyes off the water. We had brought twenty fathoms of line. When a little under half of it was gone, it stopped.

Nothing happened.

I stood with the hat in my hands and counted. A living man can hold his breath for perhaps a hundred counts, if he is good at it and not frightened. I got to two hundred. Crumble held the line across his palm, feeling for any movement.

Then it twitched once.

Crumble let out his breath.

It was a long while before the line came back. Crumble took it in slowly and coiled it at his feet. Bonesy’s skull came out of the shadow first, then the rest of him, walking up the slope of the bottom toward us.

He climbed out of the pool and sat on the rock, letting the water drain between his ribs.

“Well?” Crumble said.

“There’s a wall.” Bonesy shook his head, and water came out of his eye holes. “Down under the low side. Goes in a way, then a wall.”

“Rock?” I said.

“That’s the funny part. Looks like rock. Feels like rock, too, until you put your hand in it.”

“You put your hand into solid rock?”

“Didn’t know it was going to let me.”

Neither of us said anything.

“Then I put the rest of me through. Other side’s air. Dark. Dry stone. Steps.” He looked at his hand. “Never knew rock could do that.”

“How far?” Crumble said. “From where the light stops to the air?”

Bonesy thought about it.

“For you?” He looked at me. “Two breaths. If you don’t stop to look.”

The surface of the pool had gone flat. The tarpon were barely moving now, hanging in the water with their fins working slowly. It was as near slack as it was going to get.

I gave Bonesy his hat back.

I took off my coat and shirt and boots and set them on the rock. I took off the belt with my swords on it and laid it on top of the coat. My hands did all of this on their own, the way they do the small work on a deck. While they did it, my stomach went tight and then tighter, until it felt like a fist beneath my ribs.

Crumble passed me the end of the line.

I tied it around my middle. I had tied that knot ten thousand times. It took me two tries.

Crumble watched me do it and said nothing.

“When you reach the air, give us one pull,” he said. “Then untie yourself. I’ll follow the line down.”

I nodded.

I went down to the edge of the pool and stood where I had stood at twenty.

The water was clear all the way down. I could see the bottom, pale and bare, and the low side where it went under the rock and turned black.

I knew how cold the water would be. I could see how the bottom fell away beneath the rock, and how quickly the daylight disappeared. Five years had passed since I first stood here, and I could still remember kneeling at the edge, my hand beneath the surface, trying to convince myself to go farther.

My feet would not move.

I had stood before men with loaded pistols and felt less afraid than I did looking down into that pool.

There was nothing stopping me from turning around. I could walk back over the ridge, put my coat on, and tell Crumble we had come far enough. I had found reasons to leave before. I could find another.

Behind me Crumble had the line in both hands.

He waited.

Nobody told me to go.

Then I felt a hand on my shoulder.

I turned my head. Bonesy had come down beside me, his hat back on his skull. His fingers rested against my skin, light and hard.

He said nothing.

I looked past him at Crumble, standing with the line in his hands. He had followed me across the sea and over this island without once asking me to explain why it mattered so much. Bonesy had gone into the dark without being asked, as though it were the most natural thing in the world.

I was not going down there alone.

The tightness in my stomach eased a little. The water was still black beneath the rock, and I was still afraid of it, but there were two men behind me who had trusted me with worse.

Bonesy gave my shoulder a small pat and took his hand away.

I took one breath and let it out. I took another, as deep as it would go, and went in.

The cold took my chest first.

I heard Bonesy hit the water behind me.

I kicked downward, and the tarpon parted around me, their great silver sides flashing in the pale water. One passed so close that the water shoved against my chest, and I felt the force of its tail beating through the pool.

The big one turned and came with me for a few strokes, its eye level with mine. Then it went away into the pale water, and I was alone, swimming down the slope of the bottom toward the black.

The light went green, then grey.

I could see the rock above me now. It sloped down until it nearly met the bottom, leaving a narrow passage beneath it. I pulled myself forward along the stone, feeling the line trail behind me.

The daylight faded.

I kicked harder.

Then the passage ended.

There was rock in front of me. A wall of it, grey and pitted, filling the whole space from floor to roof. It looked exactly like every other piece of stone on that island.

I put out my hand.

My fingers went into the rock.

I stopped kicking.

I could see my forearm ending at the stone. There was no hole, no opening, no break in its surface. My hand was simply gone.

I pulled it back.

There it was again, whole and ordinary.

I put it through a second time.

I felt nothing at all. No scrape of stone, no pressure, not even a change in the water. My hand disappeared, then my wrist, then my forearm.

Every part of me wanted to pull back.

I had been afraid of the water. I had been afraid of the dark beneath the rock. I had not expected to find something worse than either.

My chest was beginning to ache.

I kicked forward and put my face into the wall.

For a moment there was nothing to see. Not the grey of the stone or the green of the water behind me. Only black.

I pushed farther.

My shoulders passed through, then my chest. I could feel the line dragging against my middle, but nothing touched my skin.

I kicked again.

There was still no light.

My lungs had begun to burn. I reached forward and felt nothing. I kicked harder, my fingers spread wide, searching for stone, for an edge, for anything I could push against.

My hand struck something solid.

I recoiled, then felt along it. Stone, rough beneath my fingers. I followed it upward and found the edge of a step.

I pulled myself forward.

My hand broke the surface into air.

I came up after it too fast, struck my head against stone above me, and went under again.

I found the step with my feet and pushed upward, keeping one hand over my head.

My face broke the surface.

I breathed.

It was black. The air was cold and smelled of dry stone. Somewhere close by, water was dripping.

I stood on a step with water up to my waist and breathed until the burning in my chest began to ease.

My heart was beating so hard I could hear it.

“Took your time,” Bonesy said, out of the dark.

I nearly lost my footing.

“Christ, Bonesy.”

“What?”

“Give a man some warning.”

“I was waiting for you.”

I could hear water dripping off him onto the stone.

“How long have you been here?”

“Not long.” He paused. “Long enough.”

I reached down and found the knot at my waist. My fingers were clumsy with cold, but I managed to work it loose.

I gave the line one hard pull.

Then I laid it across the step so Crumble could follow it through.

For a moment I stood still, listening.

The water moved softly against the stone. Behind me, somewhere beyond the wall, the pool was full of daylight and fish. Here there was nothing but darkness and the sound of dripping water.

“Can you see anything?” I asked.

“Steps,” Bonesy said. “Go up a way.”

“Anything else?”

“Door.”

“What’s beyond it?”

“Didn’t go.”

I turned toward his voice.

“Why not?”

“Thought we’d all go together.”

I said nothing.

From the water behind me came a faint disturbance.

Then a splash.

Crumble had reached the wall.

I listened to him moving through the passage. There was a scraping sound, then silence, and then a hand came up out of the water and caught the edge of the step beside me.

Crumble’s head broke the surface with a gasp.

He came up blowing like a whale, shook the water from his eyes, and reached blindly for something to hold.

I caught his arm.

He found the step with his feet and stood beside me, breathing hard. For a while neither of us spoke.

“Well,” he said at last.

“Well.”

“That’s not rock.”

“No.”

He took another breath.

“Didn’t much care for that.”

“Neither did I.”

Behind us, under the water, the passage we had come through glowed faintly green with the morning outside. In that light I could just make out the stone steps rising in front of us, cut square and even, and above them the black mouth of a doorway.

Crumble let go of my arm.

The steps were old. Their edges had been worn smooth in places, though I could not imagine who had walked them or how long it had been since anyone had come this way.

I looked back toward the green light beneath the water.

Five years I had known about that pool. Five years I had turned away from it.

And all that time there had been a doorway on the other side.

“Well,” Crumble said again.

I looked up at the steps.

“You first.”

“It’s your island.”

“And you’re the one who likes ruins.”

Bonesy had already started up.
