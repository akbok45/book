The doorway at the end of the passage was so low that we had to go through it bent double.

On the other side the air changed. It was dry, drier than anything I had breathed since the storm, and warmer. A single shaft came down through the roof and laid a square of daylight on the floor, and in the square stood a table cut from one block of stone.

On the table was the jar.

It was the size of a man’s head, round and fat, made of red clay gone dark with age. Its mouth had been stopped with a clay lid and sealed all round with something black and hard. Nothing else in the room. No gold, no bones, no carvings on the walls. Just the table and the jar and the light coming down on them.

Bonesy walked round the table twice.

“That’s it?”

“That’s it,” Crumble said.

“They said gold.” He looked at the bare walls. “The young ones. Gold with every piece.”

“They said a lot of things.”

“Liars, priests.” He sounded pleased about it.

The room was roughly round. On the far side from the doorway there was a well in the floor, a little wider than my shoulders, with a low stone lip around it. It was dark inside. I could hear water somewhere down in it, faint, the way you hear the sea in a shell.

I made myself look away from it.

Crumble put his hands on either side of the jar. He did not lift it. He stood there with his palms flat against the clay, as if he could feel something through it.

“Balam died looking for this,” he said.

Nobody answered him.

He took the knife from his belt and worked the point round the black seal. It was wax, old and hard as horn. It came away in flakes. When he had gone all the way round he put the knife down and lifted the lid with both hands.

There was no smell. I had expected rot, or damp. There was nothing but the dry smell of the room.

He reached in and brought out a packet of oiled cloth, folded small, and laid it on the table in the light. He opened the cloth one fold at a time. Inside was a single leaf, folded twice, the colour of old bone.

He opened it.

It was bark, beaten thin and coated with white plaster so the paint would take. It was a little longer than my forearm. The colours had barely faded: red and black, a blue like the shallows over sand, a yellow like dried grass. There were lines across it and small figures, and rows of black dots and bars. Down one side ran a long wavering band of blue.

I looked at it for some time. It meant nothing to me at all.

“It’s a map,” Crumble said.

“Of what?”

“I don’t know yet.” He leaned over it. “It’s laid out the old way. Balam showed me. They didn’t put north at the top the way we do. They put east at the top, where the sun comes up, and they painted that edge red.” He touched the red border along one end. “These are roads.” His finger followed a line of tiny black footprints, each one no bigger than a grain of rice. “Footprints mean somebody walked it, or paddled it. And this is how they counted.” He touched a row of dots and a black bar beside them. “A dot is one. A bar is five.”

I looked at the blue band. It was crooked, full of small bays and points. Islands sat off it like crumbs.

“That’s a coast,” I said.

“It’s nobody’s coast I know.”

“Nor me.”

Bonesy leaned over between us and looked at it, his hat dripping on the table. Crumble moved the leaf away from the drip without a word.

I stared at the blue band. Something about it was wrong. I had sailed every coast in these waters, and I had never seen one with that shape.

East at the top.

I reached out and turned the leaf on the table, so that the red edge faced away from me.

The coast came round.

It was not a strange coast at all. It was mine. The long bend of the shore running south, the bight where the reef stands off, the three small cays in a line where I had watered once in a dry year. A little out from the shore was a half moon, painted in yellow with a tiny blue spot in the middle of it.

The pool.

“Here,” I said, and put my finger next to it. “That’s us. That’s where we’re standing.”

Crumble bent close. He did not say anything for a long time.

“Yes,” he said at last.

The footprints began at the half moon. They went out across the blue in a line, north along the coast, and stopped beside a long island lying off the shore. Next to the line, in black, there was a bar and two dots.

“Seven,” Crumble said.

“Seven days.”

“By canoe. That’s how they’d have gone.”

I looked at the long island. I knew it. Every sailor on that coast knows it, lying low and green off the shore with a channel inside it that the Spanish use when the wind is foul.

“Cozumel,” I said.

“Cuzamil,” Crumble said, the old way. “The island of swallows.” He said it quietly, as if somebody might overhear.

Beside the island, at the end of the footprints, there was a sign painted in black and red. It was not a footprint and it was not a number. It looked like a face in profile with curls coming out of its open mouth. They might have been smoke, or breath, or wind.

Crumble looked at it for a long time.

“What does it say?” I asked.

“I don’t know.”

He said it flat. He bent over the leaf until his nose was a finger’s width from it. Then he straightened and stood with his hands on the edge of the table.

“Balam taught me the counts,” he said. “He taught me the roads and the numbers and a few hundred of the old signs. Not that one.”

“Somebody can read it.”

“Somebody can.”

He did not go on.

Bonesy looked at him, and then at me, and then back at him.

“Who?” he said.

Crumble took a long breath.

“His daughter,” he said.

I knew who he meant. I had seen her once, years ago. She was over his shoulder as he came out of the smoke on a burning deck. I had never heard him speak of her since.

“The girl from the raid,” I said.

“She’s not a girl now.” He was folding the oiled cloth back over the leaf, slowly, a corner at a time. “Balam taught her things he never taught me. If anyone can read that sign, she can.”

“Where is she?”

“Up a river. A long way up, on the mainland, past where any Spanish boat can go.” He laid the folded packet back in the jar. “Her people don’t let strangers in. The Spanish have been looking for them for a hundred years and never found them.”

I thought about that. Cárdenas had lost a ship to us. He knew there were three of us, and he knew what we looked like, and Domingo was somewhere in a Spanish room telling him whatever was left to tell.

“A place the Spanish can’t follow,” I said.

“Yes.”

“That would be a good place to be, for a while.”

Crumble pressed the lid back onto the jar. He took the end of the line from round his middle and bound the lid down, round and round, tight. He did not look up.

“If she’ll have us,” he said.

Behind me, something changed.

It was a small sound. I turned round. The water in the well had begun to move. It was drawing back down into the dark, slowly at first and then faster. It made a soft sucking noise, like a man breathing in through his teeth. Cold air came up out of the hole and touched my face.

The tide had turned. The pull was coming back.

I went to the edge of the well and looked down.

The water was going down and away from me into the rock, the way it went from the pool, only here I could see it go. It went down further than the light reached. It did not stop. Somewhere below me it was running on into the dark, the same as it had the day I knelt by the pool at twenty. It was running to a place I had never seen.

I stood at the lip and looked down for a long while.

“Jigsaw,” Crumble said.

I did not turn round.

“The pull’s coming back. If we don’t go now, we don’t get out through the wall.”

“I know.”

Bonesy came and stood beside me. He looked down into the well too, with his ruined hat on the back of his skull. He did not say anything. After a while he put his hand on my arm, the way he had on the rock.

I looked down into the dark one more time.

Then I stepped back from the edge, and went with them.
