The doorway at the end of the passage was so low that we had to go through it bent double.

On the other side the air changed. It was dry, drier than anything I had breathed since the storm, and warmer. A single shaft came down through the roof and laid a square of daylight on the floor, and in the square stood a table cut from one block of stone.

On the table was the jar.

It was the size of a man’s head, round and fat, made of red clay gone dark with age. Its mouth had been stopped with a clay lid and sealed all round with something black and hard.

Nothing else in the room. No gold, no bones, no carvings on the walls. Just the table and the jar and the light coming down on them.

I walked around the table, looking for something I might have missed. I even looked beneath it.

There was nothing.

I stood up and rubbed both hands over my face. My shoulders ached from the swim, and the soles of my feet were raw from the passage. I had spent five years thinking about what might be hidden beneath that pool. Now I was standing in front of a clay pot.

“All that,” I said.

Crumble had gone straight to the jar.

“All what?”

“The passage. The pit. Nearly getting ourselves killed.”

I looked at the jar.

“For a pot?”

“We haven’t opened it.”

“Unless they’ve folded a chest of gold inside, I don’t much like our chances.”

Bonesy came up beside me and looked at the jar.

“It’s a nice pot.”

“You can have it.”

“Already got Beatriz’s sister.”

I looked at him.

“Beatriz’s sister?”

“The lady in the floor.”

“The carved face?”

“She was handsome.”

“You nearly had your skull crushed standing beside her.”

He touched the crease in his hat.

“Wasn’t my skull I was worried about.”

Crumble put his hands on either side of the jar.

“Will you two be quiet?”

Bonesy looked at me.

“He’s cross.”

“So am I.”

The room was roughly round. On the far side from the doorway there was a well in the floor, a little wider than my shoulders, with a low stone lip around it. It was dark inside. I could hear water somewhere down in it, faint, the way you hear the sea in a shell.

I made myself look away from it.

Crumble stood with his palms flat against the clay, as if he could feel something through it.

“Balam died looking for this,” he said.

Nobody answered him.

He took the knife from his belt and worked the point round the black seal. It was wax, old and hard as horn. It came away in flakes. When he had gone all the way round he put the knife down and lifted the lid with both hands.

There was no smell. I had expected rot, or damp. There was nothing but the dry smell of the room.

He reached in and brought out a packet of oiled cloth, folded small, and laid it on the table in the light. He opened the cloth one fold at a time. Inside was a single leaf, folded twice, the colour of old bone.

He opened it.

It was bark, beaten thin and coated with white plaster so the paint would take. It was a little longer than my forearm. The colours had barely faded: red and black, a blue like the shallows over sand, a yellow like dried grass. There were lines across it and small figures, and rows of black dots and bars. Down one side ran a long wavering band of blue.

I looked at it for some time.

“That it?”

Crumble ignored me.

“No gold,” I said.

“Jigsaw.”

“Not even a little.”

“Would you let me look?”

I leaned against the table and folded my arms.

Bonesy peered over Crumble’s shoulder.

“Could sell the pot.”

“Thank you, Bonesy.”

“Welcome.”

“It’s a map,” Crumble said.

I straightened.

“Of what?”

“I don’t know yet.” He leaned over it. “It’s laid out the old way. Balam showed me. They didn’t put north at the top the way we do. They put east at the top, where the sun comes up, and they painted that edge red.”

He touched the red border along one end.

“These are roads.” His finger followed a line of tiny black footprints, each one no bigger than a grain of rice. “Footprints mean somebody walked it, or paddled it. And this is how they counted.”

He touched a row of dots and a black bar beside them.

“A dot is one. A bar is five.”

I looked at the blue band. It was crooked, full of small bays and points. Islands sat off it like crumbs.

“That’s a coast,” I said.

Crumble glanced at me.

“I can see that.”

“Which one?”

“I haven’t worked that out.”

I bent over the leaf.

The blue line turned sharply in two places, with a long shallow bend between them. There were three small islands near one end and a larger one farther along.

“I’ve sailed these waters long enough to know the coast,” I said. “That isn’t it.”

“It’s an old map. Shorelines change.”

“Not that much.”

“You’re looking at it the wrong way.”

“You said east was at the top.”

“Yes.”

“Then why would they draw the coast like that?”

“Because they weren’t making it for you.”

I looked at him.

He had bent over the leaf again.

“Balam could read these things,” he said. “I only know what he taught me.”

I looked back at the blue band.

Something about it troubled me. Not the shape of the coast itself, but the way the islands sat against it.

East at the top.

I reached out and turned the leaf on the table, so that the red edge faced away from me.

“Careful,” Crumble said.

The coast came round.

I stopped moving the leaf.

The long bend of the shore running south. The bight where the reef stands off. The three small cays in a line where I had watered once in a dry year.

I knew that coast.

A little out from the shore was a half moon, painted in yellow with a tiny blue spot in the middle of it.

The pool.

“Here,” I said.

I put my finger beside the half moon.

“That’s this island. And there’s the pool.”

Crumble leaned over the table.

“No.”

“Look at the reef.”

He followed my finger.

“And those three cays. They’re right where they ought to be.”

He said nothing.

“That’s the island we’re standing on. The pool’s marked in blue.”

Crumble bent closer. He looked from the half moon to the coast and back again.

Then he let out a breath.

“Yes,” he said.

The footprints began at the half moon. They went out across the blue in a line, north along the coast, and stopped beside a long island lying off the shore. Next to the line, in black, there was a bar and two dots.

“Seven,” Crumble said.

“Seven days?”

“By canoe. That’s how they’d have gone.”

I looked at the long island.

I knew it. Every sailor on that coast knows it, lying low and green off the shore with a channel inside it that the Spanish use when the wind is foul.

“Cozumel,” I said.

“Cuzamil,” Crumble said, the old way. “The island of swallows.”

He said it quietly, as if somebody might overhear.

Beside the island, at the end of the footprints, there was a sign painted in black and red. It was not a footprint and it was not a number. It looked like a face in profile with curls coming out of its open mouth. They might have been smoke, or breath, or wind.

Crumble looked at it for a long time.

“What does it say?” I asked.

“I don’t know.”

He said it flat.

He bent over the leaf until his nose was a finger’s width from it. Then he straightened and stood with his hands on the edge of the table.

“Balam taught me the counts,” he said. “He taught me the roads and the numbers and a few hundred of the old signs. Not that one.”

“Somebody can read it.”

“Somebody can.”

He did not go on.

Bonesy looked at him, then at me.

“Who?”

Crumble took a long breath.

“His daughter.”

I knew who he meant. I had seen her once, years ago. She was over his shoulder as he came out of the smoke on a burning deck. I had never heard him speak of her since.

“The girl from the raid,” I said.

“She’s not a girl now.”

He began folding the oiled cloth back over the leaf, slowly, a corner at a time.

“Balam taught her things he never taught me. If anyone can read that sign, she can.”

“Where is she?”

“On the mainland. Up a river, a long way past where any Spanish boat can go.”

“Then we ought to find her.”

Crumble stopped folding.

“No.”

I looked at him.

“No?”

“Not with Cárdenas looking for us.”

“He doesn’t know where we’re going.”

“He knows what we look like. He knows we’ve taken something from him. And if Domingo’s still alive, he may know more than that.”

I glanced toward the doorway.

“Then all the more reason to get somewhere he can’t follow.”

“You think because a Spanish ship can’t get up a river, the Spanish can’t get there?”

“I think we’re running short of places to hide.”

“And I think you’re forgetting what happens to the people who hide us.”

He folded the last corner over the leaf.

I said nothing.

“Her people have kept themselves alive by staying away from men like Cárdenas,” he said. “I’m not leading him to their door.”

“We don’t have to lead him anywhere. We can lose him before we go inland.”

“And how do you propose we do that?”

I had no answer ready.

I looked at the jar.

A few hours ago I had been certain there would be gold in it. Enough to buy a ship, perhaps, and men to sail her. Enough to stop running from one harbour to the next with the Spanish at our backs.

Instead we had a piece of painted bark and another place to go where we might not be welcome.

“Then what was the point of finding it?” I asked.

Crumble looked at me.

“Balam died for that leaf.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

I felt my jaw tighten.

I shoved off the table so hard the edge scraped my hip. I turned away from him and stood looking at the empty walls.

“I went through the same damned passage you did.”

For a moment I thought he would answer.

Instead he looked down at the folded packet.

I could hear the faint movement of water in the well. My hands were still trembling from the crossing, and I pressed them against my thighs to stop it.

Bonesy had been standing quietly beside the table.

“Well,” he said, “you did get to see me die twice.”

I looked at him.

“You didn’t die.”

“Then you got a very good bargain.”

Crumble made a small sound through his nose, though he kept his eyes on the leaf.

I wanted to be angry with Bonesy too, but the sight of his ruined hat made it difficult.

Crumble laid the packet back in the jar and pressed the lid into place.

“We’ll get away from Cárdenas first,” he said. “Then we’ll see.”

He took the end of the line from round his middle and bound the lid down, round and round, tight.

I watched him work.

“If she’ll have us,” he added.

Behind me, something changed.

It was a small sound.

I turned round.

The water in the well had begun to move. It was drawing back down into the dark, slowly at first and then faster. It made a soft sucking noise, like a man breathing in through his teeth.

Cold air came up out of the hole and touched my face.

The tide had turned.

I went to the edge of the well and looked down.

The water was going down and away from me into the rock, the way it went from the pool, only here I could see it go. It went down farther than the light reached. It did not stop.

Somewhere below me it was running on into the dark, the same as it had the day I knelt by the pool at twenty. It was running to a place I had never seen.

I stood at the lip and looked down for a long while.

“Jigsaw,” Crumble said.

I did not turn round.

“The pull’s coming back. If we don’t go now, we don’t get out through the wall.”

“I know.”

Bonesy came and stood beside me.

He looked down into the well too, with his ruined hat on the back of his skull.

He did not say anything.

After a while he put his hand on my arm, the way he had on the rock.

I looked down into the dark one more time.

Then I stepped back from the edge and went with them.
