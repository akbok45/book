The sharks were in the cove by the middle of the morning. They had come in through the gap on the flood and they did not leave. By the second hour we had stopped looking.

She was bilged forward and settled by the head, and her stern swung a little on every swell, working herself on the coral. She was going to come apart there whatever anybody did. So we took what would come off her, and we took it fast.

Powder in the dry cask. Shot for the one gun. Biscuit, a cask of salt beef gone over but not gone, cordage, canvas, a spare set of blocks, and a copper kettle Bonesy took a liking to that nobody argued about. Her glass, which was better than ours by a long way. Her charts, which were Spanish and careful and worth more than the rest of it together.

We took the water last and we took it slow. The spring under Ixchel came out of the rock the thickness of a man's finger and would not be hurried, and we stood a man at it in turn through the afternoon and into the dark, filling what we had.

“He'll know where we were,” Crumble said. He was on the stones with the chart across his knees and had not unrolled it yet.

“He'll know we took her with three.”

Crumble looked out at the wreck a while.

“Then the next one won't come in a boat.”

We went out through the gap on the morning tide with the cove behind us going about its business, and we did not look at that either.

It was eleven days west and south.

Eleven days is long enough that a boat stops being a boat and turns into a room three men cannot leave. Crumble had the tiller more than his share of it. Bonesy talked. I read the Spanish charts, which were better than anything I had ever had under my hand, and found on the third day that I had read the same soundings four times and could not have said what they were.

“Your pool,” Bonesy said.

“What about it.”

He let that sit a while.

“You never went in.”

“No.”

“Why.”

I did not give him one. He let it go, which he does about as often as a squall turns back.

What I had not said in the temple, and did not say on that deck, is that the pool had been mine for five years because I had not gone into it. I could take it out on a bad watch and look at it and put it back the way it was. I had been living off it the way a crew lives off a cask nobody has broached. Now Domingo was in a Spanish room somewhere with the rest of the course in his head, and the pool was the only road the three of us had.

Crumble did not ask for days. Then he asked.

“Jigsaw.”

“I know.”

“I've not said anything.”

“You've been going to since the cay.”

He shifted the tiller a hair to feel the wind.

“How sure are you of the water pulling.”

“Sure.”

“Sure the way a man is sure. Or sure the way you're sure of a current.”

“The second.”

He nodded and let it alone. That was the whole of what either of us said about the fact that everything we had left was a pool I had got out of at twenty and rowed away from.

On the ninth day Bonesy asked whether there was stone on it. I told him there was not, that I had seen none. He took it the way a man takes bad weather he has no say in, and did not ask again.

On the eleventh we raised it. It came up off the bow low and long and bent at the middle, green at the two ends and pale in the bight of it, the shape a man's thumb and finger make when he holds something small he does not want to crush.

I had it in my hands for the better part of an hour before Crumble said anything about the glass.
