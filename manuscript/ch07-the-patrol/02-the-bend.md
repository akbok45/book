It came down all at once.

One moment it was single drops, loud on the leaves. The next the whole river went white with it. It hammered on my back and ran down my neck and filled my ears. I could not hear the oars any more, or the forest, or anything but the rain.

I could not see the far bank. I could barely see the stone.

Crumble lay beside me with his cheek on the mud. Water ran off the end of his nose. He was smiling.

The launch came round the stone.

She came slowly, low in the water, a long black shape in the grey. The rowers were hunched over their oars with their heads down. The soldiers sat with their coats pulled up over their muskets, trying to keep the locks dry, and their steel caps ran with water like gutters. In the bow the gunner had the clay pot inside his coat, hugged against his belly. The leather cap was on the swivel.

The officer stood in the stern with his feathered hat ruined and dripping, shouting at the rowers to pull.

The current took the bow.

The steersman leaned on his oar to hold her out.

The oar did not move.

He leaned on it again. I saw his face change. He stood up and threw his whole weight on it, and it stayed where it was in the water as though it had been set in stone.

The bow swung in toward the bank.

The rowers on the near side saw the bank coming and pulled to bring her out. The far side caught the water wrong and fouled their oars. The launch came round broadside in the current and drove in under the bank beneath us, and her near gunwale struck the roots with a sound like a door slammed.

The arrows came out of the far bank.

I did not see them come. I saw them arrive. A rower went over backward off his thwart. A soldier stood up with an arrow through his arm. Every head in that boat turned to look across the river, at the trees on the other side where the arrows were coming from.

Crumble went over the edge.

He dropped onto the bow. The gunner had the lid half off the pot and was fumbling for the match inside it with wet fingers. He looked up and saw Crumble coming down out of the rain at him, and that was the last he saw.

I went over after him.

⸻

I landed in the middle of the boat on a soldier’s back, and we went down together among the thwarts in the bilge.

I came up first. I had both swords out.

A soldier in front of me had his musket up and his finger on the trigger. The lock snapped. Nothing. There was no match in the cock, only a wet black stub. He stared at it.

I cut him across the throat with my left sword.

Another swung his musket at my head by the barrel, like a man swinging a flail. I went under it and opened him from hip to ribs with the right.

A third had a pistol, a flintlock, out of his coat. He pointed it at my chest. The flint came down and sparked, and the powder in the pan went up with a fizz and a gout of white smoke, and the ball stayed in the barrel.

He was still looking at the smoke when I put my point in under his arm.

Behind me Crumble was working down from the bow. I heard Balam’s blade. I heard the men it found.

The spearmen were dropping down off the bank now. They came down into the boat and into the shallows along her side, shouting. Somewhere in the stern a musket went off, the only one that fired all day, and one of them fell back against the bank. The grey fisherman went into the water and came up at the stern with his spear. The steersman saw his face come up out of the river beside him and screamed and let go of the oar.

I was moving down the boat.

I knew where every man in it was. I knew it the way you know the pitch of a deck in the dark, without looking. A man came up on my right and I had cut him before I saw his face. Another came from the left with a hanger, and I turned it with one blade and took him under the arm with the other.

My heart was going like a drum at the capstan. My mouth was open. Rain was running into it.

I was grinning.

I had not felt so good in a year.

⸻

It was over faster than I wanted.

The rowers had thrown down their oars at the first arrows. Most of them were crouched in the bottom of the boat with their hands over their heads. The soldiers who were still standing threw down their muskets one after another, into the bilge, into the river.

The spearmen stopped.

They stood in the boat and the shallows, with their spears lowered, breathing hard. The grey fisherman had a rower by the collar, holding him up against the gunwale. The man was weeping. The fisherman was looking at him the way you look at a fish you have caught and do not know whether to keep.

A soldier came up out of the bilge in front of me.

He was young. His steel cap had gone, and his hair was plastered flat on his head with rain. He had a musket in his hands but no match in it.

He looked at me.

He threw the musket down into the water and went down on his knees in the bilge, with his hands up in front of his face.

“Mercy,” he said, in Spanish. “Mercy, señor, for the love of God.”

I looked down at him.

“God doesn’t come this far up the river,” I said.

I killed him.

Then I turned to the next.

He was sitting against a thwart with an arrow in his thigh. He put up his hands. I took the right one off at the wrist, and when he looked at the stump I put the left sword through his chest.

The man beside him tried to go over the side. I caught him by the belt and dragged him back into the boat, and held him down in the bilge water with my knee on his back until he stopped moving.

Somebody was shouting. I did not stop to hear who.

The steersman was hanging on the gunwale at the stern with his hands up. I went down the boat to him over the thwarts. He saw me coming. He let go of the gunwale and tried to swim. I followed him into the shallows and killed him in the water, and the water round him went brown and then red.

The rower the grey fisherman was holding was still weeping.

The fisherman looked at me as I came back up the boat. He did not let go of the man. He held him up against the gunwale with one hand on his collar, and looked at me with those black eyes.

I killed the rower under his hand.

The fisherman let go. He stepped back from me into the water.

There were three more crouched in the bottom of the boat with their hands over their heads. They did not try to run. There was nowhere to run. They looked up at me one after another as I came to them.

I killed them one after another.

The officer was the last. He was lying in the stern with an arrow through him under the collarbone and his feathered hat in his lap. He was still alive. He watched me come down the boat. He did not say anything. He did not ask.

I did not make it quick.

⸻

When I straightened up the rain had eased.

It was falling soft now, a drizzle that hung in the air like smoke. I could see the far bank again, and the bowmen standing at the edge of the trees with their bows lowered.

Nobody was moving.

The spearmen stood in the shallows along the boat. None of them had come any closer. Some of them had gone back a step without knowing it, up toward the bank, and stood with the water round their knees and their spears pointed at nothing. One of the young ones had turned away and was being sick into the river.

The grey fisherman stood where I had left him. He had his spear held across his body in both hands, the way you hold it when something has come out of the water that you did not expect.

At the top of the bank Amara was kneeling with the linen in her hands. She had stopped with it half unrolled. She was looking at me.

Ah Pech stood beside her with his club. He was looking at me too, and his face did not move at all.

I looked down at myself.

I was red to the elbows. My shirt was red. I could taste it. My left sword was still in my hand, and I could not open my fingers off the grip.

I looked up the boat.

Crumble was standing in the bow. Balam’s blade was down at his side with the blood running off it in the rain.

He was looking at me.

I had seen that look once before.

It was two years ago, on a Spanish merchantman taken off Cartagena. Her crew had thrown down their arms by the mainmast, nine of them, and their mate was standing in front of them with his hands up saying they yielded. I heard him say it. And I kept going, through him and through the men behind him, till there was nobody standing by the mainmast.

When I turned round Crumble was by the hatch. He was looking at me like this. Bonesy was sitting on the rail behind him with his hat in his hands, looking at the mate.

Neither of them said anything that day, or the next, or ever.

Crumble did not say anything now.

He looked at me a moment longer. Then he stepped down out of the bow into the river and waded to the bank, and he did not look back.

Behind me something moved in the water.

Bonesy’s hat came up beside the stern. Then his skull, and then the rest of him, walking up out of the river with weed in his ribs.

He stopped in the shallows and looked at the boat.

He looked at it for a long time.

He did not ask if it had worked.
