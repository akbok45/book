# 08. Scene Craft

The scene is the basic unit of fiction. Novels are not built out of chapters; they are built out of scenes. A novel that works is a sequence of scenes that each do their job. A novel that does not work usually has scenes that are not pulling their weight.

## What a Scene Is

A scene is a continuous unit of action in a single location (mostly) over a continuous span of time, in which the POV character pursues a goal against resistance, and which ends in a state different from how it began.

The "different from how it began" is essential. If the situation at the end of the scene is the same as at the start, the scene has not happened. It has been a passage.

## Scene Structure: Goal, Conflict, Disaster

Dwight Swain's classic frame. Every scene has three elements:

1. **Goal.** What the POV character wants in this scene. Specific, concrete, immediate.
2. **Conflict.** What opposes them.
3. **Disaster.** The scene ends with the goal denied, or achieved in a way that creates a worse problem.

Variations:
- **Yes, but...** they get what they wanted but with a complication.
- **No, and furthermore...** they fail, and things get worse.
- **Yes, but at a cost.** Pyrrhic victory.
- **No, but...** they fail at the immediate goal but gain something else.

The cleanest motor for narrative momentum: every scene ends worse than it began, in some dimension, until the climax. The reader keeps turning pages because the character keeps falling.

## Scene and Sequel

Swain pairs every scene with a "sequel": a quieter passage where the POV character reacts to what just happened.

Sequel structure:
1. **Reaction.** Emotional response to the disaster.
2. **Dilemma.** What now? What are the options?
3. **Decision.** Choose the next goal. This becomes the goal of the next scene.

Sequels are where character interiority happens, where the reader feels with the character, where stakes are reframed. Without them, a novel is exhausting; everything is action. With too much of them, a novel is sluggish.

Pacing rule: as the climax approaches, sequels shrink. Early in the book they can be chapters. By the climax they are sentences between bursts of action.

## Entering Late, Leaving Early

A scene should start as close to the moment of conflict as possible and end as soon as the disaster is delivered.

Cut:
- Establishing the location in detail before action begins.
- Small talk before the real conversation.
- Wrapping up after the turn.
- Transitions from previous scene that the reader can infer.

The reader should arrive in motion and leave with the question of "what now" already forming.

Example:
- Weak opening: "Sarah parked the car. She got out, walked into the building, took the elevator to the fourth floor, and entered the office. She greeted the receptionist and waited for ten minutes. Then her boss came out and asked her to step into his office."
- Strong opening: "Her boss closed the office door behind them. 'Sit down,' he said."

You have lost nothing essential. You have gained urgency.

## Scene Goals That Work

Strong scene goals share four properties:

1. **Concrete.** "Convince Marcus to come to the meeting" is concrete. "Understand my feelings about Marcus" is not.
2. **Active.** The POV character is pursuing, not just enduring.
3. **Resistible.** Something can plausibly stop them.
4. **Connected.** It serves a larger story goal.

If the goal is fuzzy, the scene will drift. Sharpen it before writing.

## Multi-Layered Scenes

A great scene works on multiple levels simultaneously. The surface conflict (do they get the goal) is the visible layer. Beneath:

- **Relational layer.** What is shifting between the characters present, beyond the surface conflict.
- **Internal layer.** What the POV character is wrestling with inside, beyond the immediate task.
- **Thematic layer.** What the scene argues about the book's central question.

A scene where two estranged siblings clean out their dead mother's house can be doing all four at once: surface (which items go where), relational (the unspoken grievances between them), internal (each one's grief and guilt), thematic (what we owe the people who raised us).

Aim for at least two layers per scene. Three or four is great.

## Scene Openings

The first sentence of a scene does outsized work. It should:

- Establish POV.
- Anchor the reader in time and place.
- Set the scene's tone.
- Pose a question or hook.

You can do all four in one sentence with practice.

"By the time Marcus reached the bridge, the sirens were already coming."

Establishes POV (Marcus), location (the bridge), time (a specific moment), tone (urgency), and hook (what are the sirens for, what is he doing at the bridge).

## Scene Endings

The last sentence of a scene tells the reader what to feel as they turn the page. Common effective endings:

- **The turn.** A surprising shift that recontextualizes everything in the scene.
- **The threat.** Something looming over the next scene.
- **The question.** An unanswered demand for resolution.
- **The image.** A specific picture that captures the emotional aftermath.
- **The line of dialogue** that opens a wound.

Avoid endings that tie up everything. The whole point of a scene ending is that you want the reader to keep reading.

## Scenes vs Summary

Not every event needs to be a scene. Some material is summary: compressed time, narrated rather than dramatized.

Dramatize:
- Events with high emotional or plot weight.
- Decisions that change the course.
- Confrontations.
- Turning points.

Summarize:
- Routine activity.
- Transitions between dramatic events.
- Backstory the reader needs as context but not as experience.
- Off-screen developments that affect the plot.

The mistake is dramatizing too much (bloated, slow) or too little (rushed, hollow). Decide for each moment which mode serves.

A novel-length sequence of dramatized scenes with no summary becomes exhausting. The summary passages are also where voice gets to expand and breathe.

## Setting in Scene

Setting is not a paragraph at the start of the scene. Setting is a series of specific details woven into the action.

Worst pattern: establish setting, then forget about it. Best pattern: setting appears as the POV character interacts with it. They lean on the railing. They smell the rain. The neon sign cuts across the wet street as they walk.

Setting should also change during the scene. The light shifts. People come and go. Weather worsens. The environment is alive.

## Time Compression Within a Scene

Within a scene, time is not uniform. The moments that matter slow down to the level of breath and gesture. The moments that do not get compressed to a sentence.

"They argued for forty minutes about the will. By the time Marcus stood up to leave, the rain had started. 'You always do this,' Eleanor said. He paused with his hand on the doorknob."

The first sentence summarizes forty minutes. The next two stretch the moment of departure. The rhythm decides what the reader feels.

## The Scene Audit

For each scene in your outline or draft, answer:

1. Whose POV?
2. What is their goal?
3. What opposes them?
4. How does the scene end (yes-but, no-and, etc.)?
5. What changes by the end (plot, relationship, internal state)?
6. What two layers are working at once?
7. What is the first image? The last?

If you cannot answer all seven, the scene is not designed yet.

## Cuts and Combinations

Scenes that cannot pass the audit should be:

- **Cut entirely** if nothing in them is essential.
- **Combined with adjacent scenes** if they do essential work but not enough of it.
- **Reshaped** by adding goal, opposition, or stakes.

A common revision pattern: identify the three or four key beats from a flabby chapter, compress them into one tight scene, cut the rest.

## Cliffhangers and Chapter Breaks

Chapter breaks are tools, not structural necessities. You can place them anywhere. Place them where they will do the most work.

Cliffhanger chapter endings pull readers forward. But constant cliffhangers exhaust. Vary:

- Some chapters end on a cliffhanger.
- Some end on a quiet image that lets the reader sit with what happened.
- Some end with a question raised.
- Some end with a small relief before the next storm.

A novel of only cliffhangers reads as desperate. A novel of only quiet endings reads as sluggish.
