# 05. Point of View and Narrative Distance

POV is the most consequential technical choice in a novel. It shapes everything: voice, what can be shown, how the reader bonds with characters, how the story unfolds. Choose it deliberately and stay consistent.

## The Main Options

### First Person ("I walked into the room")

Maximum intimacy. The reader is inside one consciousness. Strong for character-driven stories, voice-driven prose, unreliable narrators, memoir-shaped fiction.

Strengths:
- Direct access to interiority and voice.
- Easy to build deep reader identification.
- Natural for confessional tone.

Limits:
- Cannot show what the narrator does not witness or know.
- The narrator's voice colors everything, which can become claustrophobic over a long book.
- Describing the narrator (appearance, perception of self) requires care to avoid the mirror cliché.

Best for: thrillers, literary character studies, YA, noir, anything where voice is the point.

### Second Person ("You walked into the room")

Rare. Creates immediacy and discomfort or implicates the reader. Sustainable over a novel only with clear purpose. Examples: Bright Lights Big City (Jay McInerney), parts of If on a winter's night a traveler (Calvino), The Fifth Season (N.K. Jemisin).

Use only if you have a structural reason. As a stylistic flourish over a long form, it usually wears out.

### Third Person Limited ("She walked into the room")

The dominant mode in modern fiction. The narration follows one character, accessing their interiority but referring to them in the third person. Combines the intimacy of first with some flexibility of distance.

Strengths:
- Bonds reader to the POV character.
- Allows controlled shifts of narrative distance (see below).
- Can rotate POV across chapters in a multi-POV novel.

Limits:
- Same blind spots as first: cannot show what the POV character does not know.
- POV switches require chapter or section breaks to avoid head-hopping.

Default choice for most novels. Pick this unless you have a reason not to.

### Third Person Omniscient ("She walked into the room. He, watching from across, did not yet know who she was")

A narrator above all characters who can dip into any mind, comment on events, address the reader. Common in 19th century fiction (Tolstoy, George Eliot, Dickens), used effectively in some modern fantasy (Discworld, His Dark Materials).

Strengths:
- Total flexibility.
- The narrator becomes a voice and presence in its own right.
- Allows commentary, irony, scope.

Limits:
- Harder to build deep identification with any single character.
- Difficult to do well; the narrator must have a strong, consistent voice or it reads as sloppy.
- Modern readers can find it distancing.

Use only if you can hear the narrator's voice clearly in your head.

### Third Person Objective ("She walked into the room. Her hands were shaking")

Camera POV. No interiority for anyone. The reader sees only what could be filmed. Used by Hemingway in stories like "Hills Like White Elephants."

Mostly a short-story technique. Sustains a novel only in rare cases.

## Tense

Past tense is the default and the safest. Present tense creates urgency and immediacy but can become wearing over a long book and can make voice feel breathless. Use present if the story requires the reader to feel that the outcome is not yet decided, or for stylistic reasons. Otherwise, past.

Mixed tenses (a present-tense main narrative with past-tense flashbacks) are common and effective.

## Narrative Distance

Within any POV, the narrator's distance from the character's consciousness can move closer or further. This is one of the most under-taught and powerful tools in fiction. John Gardner laid out a useful five-level scale.

**Level 1 (most distant):** "A man stood on the beach."
**Level 2:** "Henry Smith stood on the beach."
**Level 3:** "Henry hated beaches."
**Level 4:** "God, how he hated these damn beaches."
**Level 5 (closest):** "Beaches. Goddamn it. Always the same dead glare, the sand getting into everything."

Each level moves closer to the character's mind. Level 5 is essentially free indirect discourse: the narration takes on the character's voice without quotation marks.

Skilled writers move fluidly along this scale within a single scene. They zoom out for setting and establishing shots, zoom in for emotional beats. Most amateur prose sits at level 2 or 3 throughout, producing flat affect.

## Free Indirect Discourse

The technique of letting third-person narration slide into the character's voice without marking the shift. Originated with Jane Austen, perfected by Flaubert, central to modern third limited.

Example:
"He stood at the window and looked out at the garden. The roses needed pruning. Always something. He had been meaning to call about the roof too, but it was Tuesday, and on Tuesdays his mother phoned, and he could not bear another conversation about Aunt Margaret."

The first two sentences are pure third person. "Always something" is the character thinking. "He could not bear another conversation" is back to third but colored by his judgment. The shifts happen without marking. The reader is in and out of his head in a single paragraph.

This is the engine of modern third limited. Master it.

## Multi-POV Novels

If the story requires more than one POV character, follow these rules:

1. **Limit the number.** Most novels work best with one to four POV characters. Game of Thrones is an outlier and even there, readers complain about the volume.
2. **Switch only at scene or chapter breaks.** Mid-scene head-hopping confuses readers and weakens identification.
3. **Give each POV character a distinct voice.** If they all narrate the same way, you have rotated nothing.
4. **Make each POV essential.** A POV that does not show the reader something only that character can show is dead weight.
5. **Decide the structural pattern in advance.** Rotating, weighted (one main, others occasional), or thematic (each character a different angle on the same event).

## Unreliable Narrators

A narrator the reader cannot fully trust. The unreliability can come from:

- **Limited knowledge:** they do not know enough.
- **Bias:** they see only what fits their worldview.
- **Self-deception:** they hide truths from themselves.
- **Active deception:** they lie to the reader.

Strong unreliable narrators reward rereading because the second pass reveals what the first concealed. Examples: Lolita, The Remains of the Day, Gone Girl, Pale Fire.

For unreliability to work, the text must contain enough signals that the reader gradually senses something is off, then suddenly understands. Pure deception without hints feels like a cheat.

## Common POV Errors

- **Head-hopping.** Switching POV mid-scene without warning. Confusing. Always break the scene or chapter.
- **Showing what the POV character cannot see.** "She had no idea that, behind her, he was smiling." In strict third limited, you cannot show this. Either acknowledge it as omniscient or restructure.
- **Distance whiplash.** Jerking from level 1 to level 5 and back without purpose. Movement should feel motivated.
- **Filtering.** "She saw the door open." "She heard footsteps." Mostly the filter (saw, heard, felt, thought) can be removed for closer effect: "The door opened. Footsteps." Use filtering when you specifically want to remind the reader they are inside a perceiver.
- **Author intrusion.** The narrator suddenly knowing more than the POV character or commenting in a voice that does not match. Common in late-draft sloppiness.

## Choosing for Your Book

Quick decision matrix:

- **Single protagonist, voice-driven, intense interiority:** first person past, or close third limited.
- **Multiple characters, ensemble plot:** rotating third limited.
- **Sweeping scope, multiple eras, narrator presence:** third omniscient.
- **Urgent, present-moment, breathless:** first or third present.
- **Mystery, deception, surprise:** consider what POV maximizes the gap between what the reader and protagonist know. Sometimes first; sometimes a tightly limited third.

Decide early. Changing POV mid-draft is one of the most expensive revisions in fiction.
