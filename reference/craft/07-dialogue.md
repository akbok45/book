# 07. Dialogue

Dialogue is the hardest thing in fiction. It looks easy because we all do it every day. Producing dialogue that sounds real, advances story, reveals character, and contains tension on every line is one of the rarest skills in writing.

## What Dialogue Is Not

Real conversation is not dialogue. Real conversation is repetitive, full of throat clearing, restarts, half thoughts, and chitchat that goes nowhere. Transcribe it and it reads as broken.

Fictional dialogue is the illusion of real speech, compressed and shaped for effect. It strips out the dead air and leaves the parts that matter.

## What Dialogue Does

A line of dialogue should be doing at least two of the following at once:

1. **Advance the plot** (information moves, decisions made, conflict escalates).
2. **Reveal character** (we learn something about the speaker through what they choose to say and how).
3. **Develop relationship** (the dynamic between speakers shifts).
4. **Develop theme** (the line gestures toward the book's larger questions).
5. **Sustain tension** (the line raises a question or deepens a conflict).
6. **Create rhythm or texture** (humor, beauty, voice).

If a line is only doing one, it can probably be cut or compressed.

## Subtext

The single most important concept. Subtext is what the characters mean but do not say. Almost every important conversation in life happens at this level.

A husband and wife are arguing about who left the milk out. They are not actually arguing about milk. They are arguing about who carries the household. Or about respect. Or about the thing he said three weeks ago that she has not forgiven. The milk is the surface. The marriage is the subtext.

Dialogue without subtext is on the nose: characters say exactly what they mean, the way they would in a textbook. Dialogue with subtext lets readers feel the gap between the words and the meaning, and that gap is where electricity lives.

How to write subtext:

1. **Decide what the character actually wants from this conversation.** Not the topic. The underlying need (validation, escape, reassurance, dominance, intimacy).
2. **Decide what stops them from saying it.** Fear, pride, social norms, the relationship's history.
3. **Let them talk around it.** Through the surface topic, through deflection, through aggression about something safer.

The reader fills in what is meant. Trust the reader.

## On the Nose Dialogue (and How to Avoid It)

On the nose dialogue says exactly what the character feels:

"I am angry with you because you forgot my birthday and it makes me feel unloved."

Real people almost never talk like this. Even people who think they are being direct deflect, exaggerate, joke. Replace with deflection, subtext, or behavior:

"It's fine. Really. I made other plans."
She sets the coffee mug down too firmly.

Now the reader feels the anger more than the line ever could.

## Tags and Beats

**Dialogue tags** identify the speaker:
"I won't go," she said.

**Action beats** show the speaker through behavior, often replacing tags:
"I won't go." She closed the book and set it on the table.

Use tags sparingly. They mostly disappear from the reader's awareness if they are just "said." Use beats to:

- Slow the pace.
- Show what the speaker is doing physically.
- Convey emotion through behavior rather than adverb.
- Reveal status, mood, hidden intent.

Pattern: every few lines, anchor the conversation with a beat. Pure dialogue for too many lines becomes weightless.

## On "Said"

Use "said" almost always. It is invisible. The eye skips over it. Replacing it with "exclaimed," "retorted," "ejaculated," "spat" calls attention to the tag and away from the line.

Exceptions:

- "Asked" for direct questions where it adds clarity.
- "Whispered," "shouted," "muttered" when volume genuinely matters to the moment.
- Avoid the rest. Let the words and beats carry the tone.

If you are constantly reaching for stronger tags, the dialogue itself is not carrying enough weight. Fix the lines, not the tags.

## Voice Differentiation

If you cover the tags and beats, can the reader still tell who is talking? If not, the voices are not differentiated enough.

Tools for differentiation:

- **Vocabulary level.** The professor uses Latinate words. The mechanic uses concrete ones.
- **Sentence length.** Some characters speak in fragments. Some in long, qualified clauses.
- **Speech habits.** A character who never asks questions. A character who answers questions with questions. A character who never says "I."
- **Topic preferences.** What each character pulls every conversation back to.
- **Idiom and dialect.** Used carefully, regional speech distinguishes voices. Used heavily, it becomes a caricature.
- **Self-presentation.** Some speak as if performing. Some as if hiding. Some as if confessing.

A simple exercise: write a dialogue scene where two characters disagree about lunch. If the dialogue is good, the reader should know which is which from the third line.

## Exposition Through Dialogue

The classic trap: two characters explaining things to each other that they both already know, because the reader needs the information.

"As you know, Bob, the war has been going on for ten years and our king has just died."

If both characters already know it, they would not say it. Solutions:

1. **Disagreement.** They know different versions and argue over which is right.
2. **A third party present.** Now you can naturally explain to someone who does not know.
3. **Emotional charge.** They both know it, but they have not talked about it, and one finally raises it. Now the information lands as confrontation.
4. **Implication.** Let the conversation reveal what was at stake without naming it directly. Trust the reader.
5. **Just narrate it.** Sometimes a line of summary handles in five words what a contorted dialogue handles in fifty.

## The Cardinal Rule of Dialogue Tension

Every conversation should have at least one character who wants something the other does not want to give. Even between allies. Even between lovers. Even between strangers in line at the post office.

Without that wanting, you have chitchat. With it, you have a scene.

The want can be anything: reassurance, agreement, a piece of information, a confession, attention, distance. As long as something is being pursued and resisted, the dialogue has friction.

## Silence

The unsaid is part of dialogue. A character who does not respond is making a choice. A pause is a line.

Use silence and ellipsis sparingly enough that they retain weight, but use them. A character who refuses to answer can be the most powerful moment in a scene.

"Did you do it?"
He looked at her. He did not look away.

The non-answer is the answer.

## Argument Scenes

Most beginner argument scenes are too clean. People take turns making points. They escalate predictably. They land their best lines.

Real arguments are messy. People interrupt. They repeat themselves. They get sidetracked. They suddenly bring up something from three years ago. They say things they do not mean and then defend them. They make accusations and then realize they are about themselves.

Lean into the mess. Let one character get the better line and the other character just stare instead of replying. Let an argument about one thing become an argument about something else. Let someone say something true and refuse to take it back.

## Confessions

Confession scenes are where the protagonist or a key character finally says the thing they have been avoiding the whole book. They are climactic moments and they have to land.

Common failures:
- The confession comes too easily. There should be visible resistance even at the moment of giving in.
- The confession is too tidy. People do not summarize their pain neatly. They circle, contradict, qualify.
- The listener responds correctly. Real listeners react badly, defensively, or with their own counter-confession.

Earn the confession by establishing the cost. The character should be afraid of what they are about to say. The reader should feel the door opening.

## Dialect and Accent

Use sparingly. Heavy phonetic spelling ("yew kin git outta heah") is hard to read and condescending to the dialect being depicted. Convey accent through:

- **Word choice and idiom** (regional vocabulary).
- **Sentence structure** (some dialects allow constructions standard English does not).
- **Rhythm.**
- **The occasional marker word** rather than systematic respelling.

You can establish a character speaks with an accent in one line of narration and then write their dialogue in clean prose. The reader will hear the voice you have set up.

## Length

Long monologues are usually a mistake. If a character is talking for more than half a page without interruption, ask why. Sometimes there is a reason (a confession, a courtroom speech, a story being told). Usually, you are using monologue to do work that should be done dramatically.

When monologue is necessary:
- Break it with beats (physical action, listener reaction, environment).
- Give it shape (it should escalate, turn, surprise).
- Earn it (the character must be at the point where saying this much is psychologically credible).
