# 14. Further Reading

The craft books that actually teach craft. Curated rather than exhaustive. Every one of these is worth the time. Read at least three before writing a novel.

## On Sentence and Voice

**Steering the Craft** by Ursula K. Le Guin. The best short book on the technical craft of prose. Chapters on rhythm, syntax, point of view, with exercises. Required.

**The Art of Fiction** by John Gardner. Older but foundational. Includes the discussion of psychic distance that everyone borrows from.

**Reading Like a Writer** by Francine Prose. How to learn from the books you love by paying attention to what they are actually doing sentence by sentence.

**Several Short Sentences About Writing** by Verlyn Klinkenborg. Anti-rules and high attention to the music of prose. Polarizing but useful.

**The Elements of Style** by Strunk and White. Now mostly important as cultural artifact, but a few of the rules still hold. Read once and move on.

## On Story and Structure

**Story** by Robert McKee. Written for screenwriters but applies to novels. The clearest articulation of why scenes need to do work.

**Save the Cat! Writes a Novel** by Jessica Brody. The 15-beat structure adapted for novelists. Useful as a draft tool even if you do not follow it exactly.

**Wired for Story** by Lisa Cron. What cognitive science says about why stories grip us. The wound, the lie, the misbelief framework.

**Story Genius** by Lisa Cron. Companion to the above. A method for building a novel from the protagonist's interior outward.

**The Story Grid** by Shawn Coyne. Scene-by-scene craft analysis. The five questions every scene must answer.

**Into the Woods** by John Yorke. A deep look at why story structure is the shape it is across cultures.

## On Drafting and Process

**On Writing** by Stephen King. Half memoir, half practical advice. The most quoted craft book of the last fifty years. The advice on adverbs and the second-draft-equals-first-draft-minus-10% rule are foundational.

**Bird by Bird** by Anne Lamott. On the emotional process of drafting and how to keep going when it is bad. The "shitty first drafts" chapter alone is essential.

**The War of Art** by Steven Pressfield. On resistance and discipline. Short. Useful when stuck.

**Writing Down the Bones** by Natalie Goldberg. On generating material without judging. Useful if you are over-editing in early drafts.

## On Specific Skills

**Self-Editing for Fiction Writers** by Browne and King. The clearest book on revision technique. Show vs tell, dialogue mechanics, beats, voice.

**Writing the Breakout Novel** by Donald Maass. On what separates good novels from great ones. Strong on stakes and tension.

**Description and Setting** by Ron Rozelle. Underrated. Specifically on how to render place.

**Dialogue** by Robert McKee. A whole book on the subject. Excellent.

**Wonderbook** by Jeff VanderMeer. Illustrated guide to writing imaginative fiction. Visual, playful, deep.

## On Genre

**The Anatomy of Story** by John Truby. Includes detailed analyses of genre structures.

**Romancing the Beat** by Gwen Hayes. The romance structure manual. Required if you are writing romance.

**Writing the Thriller** by T. Macdonald Skillman. The thriller genre's particular demands.

**Sanderson's Lectures on Writing Science Fiction and Fantasy** (free on YouTube). Brandon Sanderson's BYU course. Long, but exceptionally clear on plotting, magic systems, and worldbuilding.

## Novels to Study as Craft Texts

Read these not for pleasure (though they give it) but to see specific skills at work.

For voice:
- **The Catcher in the Rye** (Salinger). First-person voice that established the template.
- **Beloved** (Morrison). What prose can do when stretched to its limit.
- **A Visit from the Goon Squad** (Egan). What it looks like to switch voices across a book and have it cohere.

For structure:
- **The Remains of the Day** (Ishiguro). Quiet, layered, devastating. Watch the way the past surfaces.
- **Atonement** (McEwan). On how endings can recontextualize everything.
- **Mrs Dalloway** (Woolf). Single day, free indirect discourse, time and consciousness.

For plot mechanics:
- **The Spy Who Came in from the Cold** (le Carré). Lean. Every detail planted, every detail used.
- **Mystic River** (Lehane). Three characters, one wound, perfect convergence.
- **Gone Girl** (Flynn). The unreliable narrator at its most weaponized.

For character:
- **Lonesome Dove** (McMurtry). Two men, hundreds of pages, you know them like family.
- **A Little Life** (Yanagihara). Whatever you think of the book's content, it shows what a deep character study looks like in modern fiction.
- **The Brothers Karamazov** (Dostoevsky). Four brothers, four temperaments, vast interior life.

For world:
- **The Left Hand of Darkness** (Le Guin). Worldbuilding as thought experiment.
- **Perdido Street Station** (Miéville). Worldbuilding as sensory overload.
- **The Lord of the Rings** (Tolkien). The deep-time approach.

For sentence:
- **The Sense of an Ending** (Barnes). Short, precise, every sentence working.
- **The Road** (McCarthy). On how to break the rules of punctuation in service of voice.
- **Housekeeping** (Robinson). Patient, lyrical, every sentence a small structure.

## Online Resources

**Brandon Sanderson's BYU Lectures** (free on YouTube). Extensive, generous, practical.

**Writing Excuses** (podcast). Fifteen-minute episodes by Sanderson, Mary Robinette Kowal, and others.

**KM Weiland's Helping Writers Become Authors** (blog and podcast). Strong on character arcs and structure.

**Story Grid** (website and podcast). Coyne's approach in detail.

**Ursula K. Le Guin Decoded** by JJK Lit on YouTube. Close readings.

## What to Skip

Most general "how to write a novel" books are repackagings of the basics. Read the foundational texts above and then read novels. The hours you spend reading the actual best fiction will teach you more than any second-tier craft book.

Avoid: any craft book that promises a formula, any course that promises bestseller status, any guru who has not written anything worth reading themselves.

## A Reading Plan

If starting from zero, in order:

1. **On Writing** (King) for orientation.
2. **Steering the Craft** (Le Guin) for sentence-level fundamentals.
3. **Story** (McKee) for structure.
4. **Self-Editing for Fiction Writers** (Browne and King) for revision.
5. **Wired for Story** (Cron) for character interiority.
6. Three novels in your target genre. Read them once for pleasure, once with a notebook.
7. Write.

The reading will not produce a novel. The writing will. But the reading shortens the time from bad first novel to publishable novel by years.
