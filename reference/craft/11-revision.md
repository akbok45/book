# 11. Revision

Most published novels are made in revision. The first draft is the ugly truth that you mine for the actual book. Writers who skip revision produce drafts that get rejected and never know why.

## The Four Drafts (Roughly)

A practical scheme. Different writers run more or fewer drafts and combine stages, but the underlying logic holds.

**Draft 1: The Discovery Draft.** Goal: find the story. Get to the end. Allow yourself to write badly. The job is to discover what the book actually wants to be, which is often different from what you planned.

**Draft 2: The Structural Draft.** Goal: fix the architecture. Cut, restructure, rewrite, sometimes throw out half. Big-picture changes. Move chapters, change POV, kill characters, rebuild the climax.

**Draft 3: The Scene Draft.** Goal: tighten every scene. Cut what does not earn its place. Sharpen the goals and conflicts. Strengthen voice.

**Draft 4: The Line Draft.** Goal: every sentence. Pacing, word choice, rhythm, error correction.

You may also have a draft 5 (proofreading after professional editing) and so on. The point is that each draft has one focus and you do not try to do everything at once.

## The Cooling Off Period

Between drafts, especially between drafts one and two, set the manuscript aside. Six weeks is good. Eight is better. Twelve is sometimes necessary.

This is not optional. You cannot revise effectively while the words are still warm. You need to read your own work as a stranger. The cooling period creates the distance.

Use the time to write something else, or read, or do anything except think about the draft. When you return, you will see the book.

## The Diagnostic Reread

Before starting major revisions, read the whole draft in as few sittings as possible. Two to four days for a novel. Read like a reader, not an editor. Take notes only on broad reactions, not line edits.

Track:
- Where you got bored.
- Where you got confused.
- Where the energy spiked.
- Where you put the book down.
- What questions you have at the end.

Now you know what the book is actually doing. You can compare that against what you intended. The gap is your revision agenda.

## Structural Revision (Draft 2)

The big picture. Ask:

1. **Is the protagonist's arc clear?** Want, need, lie, truth, change. Each visible on the page.
2. **Does the inciting incident force the protagonist into action?** Or do they drift into it?
3. **Is the midpoint a real turn?** If you removed the middle of the book, would the second half make sense?
4. **Does the climax force the protagonist to use what they learned?** Or does someone else save them?
5. **Is the antagonist effective?** Are they applying genuine pressure?
6. **Are the subplots earning their space?** Or are they vestigial?
7. **Is the theme clear without being stated?** What is the book about?
8. **Does the opening promise what the book delivers?** Or has the book drifted from its premise?

Structural problems do not get solved by line editing. They need surgery. Be willing to cut chapters, change POV, kill characters, restage major scenes.

## Scene Revision (Draft 3)

For every scene:

1. Is the POV clear?
2. Is there a specific goal?
3. Is there meaningful opposition?
4. Does the scene end in a different state than it began?
5. Does the scene work on at least two layers?
6. Does it earn its length?
7. Does it start late and end early?
8. What is the first image? The last?

Scenes that fail the audit get cut, combined, or rebuilt. Be ruthless. Beginners cling to scenes they like; professionals cut anything that does not serve.

## Line Revision (Draft 4)

Sentence by sentence. Pass through the whole book looking for:

- **Filtering verbs** (saw, heard, felt, thought, realized) that can be cut.
- **Adverbs.** Especially "ly" adverbs. Almost always cut or replace the verb.
- **Be-verbs** (was, were, is, are, being) that flatten the prose.
- **Throat clearing** at sentence and paragraph start.
- **Echoes.** The same word twice within a few sentences.
- **Tired metaphors.** Replace or cut.
- **Generic descriptions.** Replace with specifics.
- **Telling where you should be showing** (and vice versa).
- **Rhythm.** Read aloud. Listen for monotony.
- **Voice.** Does every paragraph sound like only this narrator?

This is the slowest and most tedious draft. It is also where the book becomes professional.

## Reading Aloud

The single most underused revision tool. Reading aloud catches:

- Rhythmic flat spots.
- Awkward dialogue.
- Repetition.
- Overlong sentences.
- Word choices that sound wrong.

You cannot fake reading aloud. The eye lies; the ear does not. Do it for the whole book. It takes days. It is worth it.

## Cutting

Most first drafts are 20 to 30% too long. Brutal cutting almost always improves a book.

What to cut:
- Backstory that the reader does not need yet.
- Scenes where nothing changes.
- Dialogue that does not advance two of the six things dialogue should do (see file 07).
- Description that does not characterize.
- Repetition of information the reader already has.
- Filler at scene openings and endings.
- Throat clearing in paragraphs and sentences.
- Adverbs and unnecessary qualifiers.

A trick: in revision, try cutting any sentence and see if the paragraph still works. If yes, the sentence was not pulling its weight.

Stephen King's rule: second draft equals first draft minus 10%. Often more.

## Killing Darlings

Faulkner's rule: kill your darlings. The sentences and scenes you love most are often the ones that need to go.

The reason: writers fall in love with material for personal reasons (it was hard to write, it sounds clever, it is autobiographical, it is what got you excited about the book). The reader does not have those reasons. They see the sentence or scene clearly, and if it does not work, it does not work.

Sign you are protecting a darling:
- You keep editing around a passage instead of fixing it.
- You feel defensive when a beta reader questions it.
- You cannot articulate what it does for the story.

If those apply, cut it. Save it in a separate file. You can use the writing elsewhere or in another book. But it leaves this one.

## Beta Readers

Outside readers see what you cannot. You are too close.

How to use them:

1. **Pick people who read books like yours.** A literary fiction reader cannot reliably critique a thriller and vice versa.
2. **Give them specific questions.** "Where did you get bored?" "Did you understand X?" "Did you believe Y's choice in chapter twelve?" Vague feedback gives vague results.
3. **Listen for patterns.** One beta reader saying a chapter dragged is data. Three saying it is a verdict.
4. **Diagnose the problem, not the solution.** When a beta reader says "I wanted more about her sister," they may be right that something is wrong. The fix is rarely literally adding more about the sister. Your job is to figure out what feeling they are reporting and address the root cause.
5. **Do not argue.** Listen, thank them, decide later. Defending the draft to a beta reader is a waste of everyone's time.

## Professional Editing

If publishing traditionally, an editor will work the book with you. If self-publishing or just polishing, consider hiring:

- **Developmental editor** for structural feedback.
- **Line editor** for sentence-level work.
- **Copy editor** for grammar, consistency, and style.
- **Proofreader** for final error catching.

These are different jobs. Few people do them all well. The line editor's gift is rhythm and voice; the copy editor's gift is catching that you spelled the character's name two different ways.

## Knowing When to Stop

You can revise forever. At some point, more revision starts producing worse work.

Signs you are done (or close):
- The changes you are making are minor and contradictory (you change a word back to what it was three drafts ago).
- The book reads as a unified whole.
- You can name what the book is about.
- The opening and closing earn each other.
- You no longer surprise yourself with what you find.

Ship it. Move on. The next book is waiting.

## The Long Truth About Revision

A draft is the question. Revision is the answer. Writers who hate revising and love drafting tend to produce work that feels half-baked. Writers who love revising and hate drafting tend to produce work that is over-polished and underfelt.

You want to love both, or at least respect both. Drafting gets you the raw material. Revision turns it into a book.
